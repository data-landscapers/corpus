# Ghana: progress report, 2025-08-01 to 2026-08-05

*Compiled 2026-08-05 from the Data Landscapers source base. Sections follow the status report. Each opens with a movement ledger comparing the position at the start and end of the period, which runs to the date of issue rather than to the last month's close.*

*Of 118 systems and instruments on this place's ledger, 54 changed position between 2025-08-01 and 2026-08-05, 17 did not, 29 carry no stated baseline, and 18 are ***Not held*** at both ends.*

*Shape check, run before the comparison: 229 sources for this place in the window — 43 in the earlier half (2025-08 to 2026-01), 186 in the later (2026-02 to 2026-08). **The earlier half of the window is thin: this is a shorter comparison wearing a longer label**, and the movement below rests mostly on the later half.*

**Movement values.** *Advanced* — a system entered service, a stage was completed or an instrument was made. *Stalled* — a stated target passed without delivery. *Regressed* — an instrument was withdrawn or neutralised, or a measured position worsened. *Closed* — the programme ended. *No change* — the position at both ends is the same. ***Baseline not held*** — the base carries no position at the start of the period, so no movement can be stated. A value may carry a qualifying clause after a comma, as in *Advanced, slipped*.

## Summary of the period

<!-- narrative: summary -->
Over the twelve months Ghana went from a jurisdiction with a data-protection law to one with an operating, multi-regulator instrument stack — built mostly by directive and notice rather than by Parliament. The shape check above records the coverage cliff: the comparison rests heavily on the window's later half, and rows whose only source is recent but which describe long-standing systems carry their position from earlier rather than as movement.

<!-- narrative: infrastructure -->
The clearest band change in this report is the 5G market. At the window's opening one operator held an exclusive wholesale licence and had already missed a June 2025 launch deadline. By July 2026 that exclusivity had been deleted after a full statutory objection sequence, a shared wholesale network had gone live in March, and a priced multi-lot competitive assignment was running with coverage obligations, a market-power premium and a published timetable — a move from monopoly design to open market with a number on it.

Data centres moved once, and in the opposite direction to the announcements. A commercial facility was commissioned between October and November 2025 at up to 1.7 MW and 500 racks; a facility announced in 2023 at 10 MW expandable to 30 MW has produced no dated status at all since; and the largest facility on the record entered the base in July 2026 through pan-African trade press with no Ghanaian primary behind it.

Cyber-security is where the base thickened most and matured least. At the start it held one commercial security operations centre; it now holds a central-bank directive, a named sectoral operations centre, an eighteen-institution coordinating committee and a journalist-training project — and still no national computer emergency response team, no Cybersecurity Act text and no incident statistics.

The rest is accretion on a network-quality problem the base already recognised: over 8,000 fibre cuts a year, and half-year capital expenditure down 36.3% at the operator carrying most of the build.

<!-- narrative: dpi -->
Identity changed band decisively. A credential became a compulsory verification regime: amendment regulations in force from 9 June 2026 made biometric matching the only lawful means, barred photocopying and retention, and fixed enforcement at 2 November 2026, while the card itself cleared a backlog of more than 640,000 and moved from about 18 million registered adults to 19.2 million with institutions integrated rising from 95 to 262.

Revenue and procurement systems moved from ambition to operating instruments — a customs valuation system fully deployed from March 2026 with a quantified revenue claim, mandatory electronic procurement integrated with the national payment system, and three revenue platforms approved by Parliament in July. Digital credit acquired a licensing regime, a lapsed deadline and an enforcement notice naming twenty applications, none of which existed a year ago.

The payments core itself only accreted detail. The rail was already there and got bigger, better instrumented and better measured: registered wallets from 76.4 million to 84.6 million, float to a record GH¢40bn. The significant new fact is not the growth but the 1.3% interoperable share, which reframes a position the base already held rather than moving it.

Three things went nowhere. The biometric SIM re-registration announced for the first quarter of 2026 has not begun; the services portal redesign promised for the fourth quarter of 2025 has not launched; and a licence-passporting memorandum signed in February 2025 is where it was — signed, unpublished, unreadable.

<!-- narrative: governance -->
At the window's opening this was one statute and one regulator, with a governing board sworn in that very day and a national data strategy validated thirteen months earlier and never adopted. Everything else in this section arrived inside the twelve months: identity regulations in force, a digital-credit directive with two enforcement notices, a central-bank cyber directive, a virtual-assets Act in implementation, an artificial-intelligence strategy approved and funded, a 5G assignment and the deletion of an exclusive licence.

The genuine maturity change is therefore from a jurisdiction with a data-protection law to one with an operating instrument stack — built by directive and notice rather than by Parliament. The second real change is the drafting wave: at the baseline the base held no bill at all, and it now names at least eight distinct proposed instruments and holds the text of none.

What merely accreted detail is the data-protection position itself. A year of awareness campaigns, a size-banded fee schedule, a data-protection week and a declared year of enforcement produced no amendment to the 2012 Act, no enacted successor and no published enforcement figure. Two positions moved backwards while looking active: the SIM re-registration instrument, stated ready to be laid in December 2025 and still not laid, and the identity agency's Act amendment, before Parliament since August 2025 with nothing since. And the regulator's own declared enforcement year is being delivered, on the evidence, by the central bank.

At the window's opening Ghana had no adopted artificial-intelligence policy: a draft in consultation, a coders programme four months old, a privately built security centre, and a research centre with a cumulative investment figure disclosed the week before. Nothing was funded and no institution existed.

Three things changed band. The strategy moved from draft to Cabinet-approved in February 2026, to launched with a US$270m headline in April, to a ten-year implementation plan under construction by July — an object that did not exist a year ago. Artificial intelligence moved from policy to production in one place only, at customs. And the country acquired physical adjacent capacity it did not have, in a commissioned commercial data centre and an approved but unbuilt compute centre.

Everything else accreted detail on a position already held. The oversight body has been planned continuously since April 2025, sixteen months without an establishing instrument, and the private security centre has had no update since its launch. The financing picture is the sharpest divergence: announced money with no disbursement on file, while the sector's largest actual spender cut half-year capital expenditure in the same period it raised a three-year pledge.
