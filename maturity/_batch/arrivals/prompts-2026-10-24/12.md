| 1 | Is there a national data policy, or one being written? | A policy, strategy or framework for data: being drafted, out for consultation, or adopted. A consultant hired or a team appointed to write it counts. A stated intention with no drafting begun is not a yes |

**National data policy**

|---|---|
| 1 Absent | A dated source says the country has no national data policy and none is being written |

| 3 Establishing | Yes to 1 and 2: a policy adopted and still running |
| 4 Operating | Yes to 1, 2 and 3: a body is named to carry it out |
| 5 Leading | Yes to all four: a rule or standard the policy called for has been issued |

**Settled as worded** *(Bill, 2026-10-23)*: an instrument in force that orders the bodies set up names them, for question 3.

- **Questions 1 to 3 have no time limit**: an adopted policy stands, with its year, until its end date passes or a dated source says it has been replaced or withdrawn.
- **A statement that there is no policy counts for three years.**
- **A draft validated at a workshop, or handed to the minister, is still a draft.** Question 2 needs approval or a launch by the government. Most of what is held is a validation, so most countries will sit at stage 2.
- **A policy on sharing or managing data across the whole government counts** (Rwanda's data sharing policy, Lesotho's data management policy), as it does in the row as it stands.
- **A policy on one matter with a row of its own does not count**: data protection, open data, keeping data in the country, cloud, interoperability, AI. Nor does a strategy for official statistics.
- **A country saying it will follow the African Union's Data Policy Framework, with no policy of its own being written, places nothing.** This is one change from the row as it stands, whose stage 2 counts it.
- **A body counts for question 3, and a rule for question 4, only where the source ties it to the policy.** A data protection authority is not the body unless a source says the policy is its job.
- **The ministry that wrote the policy being responsible for it in general is not a yes to question 3.**
- **A body set up to write the policy answers question 1, not question 3.**
- **The top stage is one rule issued, not a set.** This is the other change from the row as it stands, whose stage 4 asks for rules in two named areas and whose stage 5 is agreement with the African Union's framework.
- **A policy adopted but not yet in effect is a yes to question 2**, and the summary gives the date it starts.
- **How good the policy is, is not judged**, nor whether it follows the African Union's framework; a source saying it does is noted.
- **A policy that is part of a larger strategy or law** is named in the summary by that instrument's name; the digital strategy, cloud and data protection rows are not changed by it.

`study.json` holds this as two aspects: `standing` (`adopted`, `ended`, `draft`, `none`) for questions 1 and 2, and `use` (`issued`, `body`, `none`) for 3 and 4.

- aspect "state": "hosted", "none"

#### 1. What is assessed

**A data centre for hire in the country that is owned in the country**: by the state, a state enterprise, or a company owned mostly by the country's own people or firms, whatever it is called. "For hire" is the datacentres study's test: it rents space, power or computing to other organisations.

The study redraws `infra.store--local-data-centre-capacity-national-providers`, which as at 2026-08-31 puts 18 countries at stage 4, 7 at stage 3, 8 at stage 2 and 8 at stage 1, none at stage 5, and 13 without a stage. It touches no other row.

| Sub-indicator | Indicator text | Id |
|---|---|---|
| Nationally owned data centres for hire | Local data centre capacity (national providers): nationally owned data centres for hire | `infra.store--local-data-centre-capacity-national-providers` |

Anything else the evidence names is `other`, noted and never staged: a foreign-owned data centre, which `datacentres` assesses; one announced, financed or under construction; one used only by its owner; a rule that data must stay in the country, which `datalocalisation` assesses.
#### 2. The questions

Asked in order. Each is answered with a date and a link.

| # | Question | A yes is |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is a nationally owned data centre for hire in service? | A source states that a data centre for hire in service is owned by the state, a state enterprise or a company owned mostly in the country |
| 2 | Is one of them Tier III or above? | A source states that such a data centre is Tier III or IV, by certification, a TIA-942 rating or the operator's own statement |
| 3 | Are two of them Tier III or above? | Sources state that two such data centres are Tier III or IV |
| 4 | Does the government host its own data with a national provider? | A source states that ministries' or the state's own systems or data are hosted in a nationally owned data centre in the country |
#### 3. The ladders

**Nationally owned data centres for hire**

| Stage | Answers |
|---|---|
| 1 Absent | A source from the past three years says no data centre for hire in the country is nationally owned |
| 2 Preparing | Yes to 1: a nationally owned data centre for hire in service |
| 3 Establishing | Yes to 2: one at Tier III or above |
| 4 Operating | Yes to 3: two at Tier III or above |
| 5 Leading | Yes to 3 and 4: two at Tier III, and the state's own data hosted with a national provider |

- **No question has a window.** A data centre in service, and a state's hosting, stand until a later source says they have ended.
- **Ownership is the owner's, at the as-at date.** A company bought by a foreign group is foreign from the sale. A joint venture is national where the country's side holds the majority.
- **Stages 2 to 4 change.** The row banded the share of facilities nationally owned, at 25 % and 50 %. A share needs every facility counted, and no story states one; a share of 100 % also put a country with one small state facility above one with five. The study counts nationally owned facilities and their Tier instead, as `datacentres` does for all owners.
- **Stage 5 is the row's own end state**, asked as a fact: the row's wording also required a data classification in force, which needs a law read and belongs to `datalocalisation`.
- **Stage 1 needs a statement of absence**, as in every study. A country whose only data centres for hire are foreign-owned, with no such statement, is unplaced and says so.

`study.json` holds this as three aspects: `centre` (`two`, `tier3`, `service`, `none`) for a facility, `owner` (`national`, `foreign`) for who owns it, and `state` (`hosted`, `none`) for question 4.

### Study offsitebackup

sub_indicator: "backup" (class "backupsite")

Aspects and their values:
- aspect "site": "shared", "service", "none"
- aspect "plan": "tested", "plan"
- aspect "tier": "both"

#### 1. What is assessed

**A separate site that keeps a public body's systems and data recoverable when its main site fails**, whatever it is called: a backup site, disaster-recovery site, secondary data centre, mirror or alternative site. A public body is a ministry, an agency or the central bank; a state-owned company's own redundancy is not.

**The backup site must be at least 200 km from the main site** *(Bill, 2026-10-14)*, so that one flood, fire or outage cannot take both. A site abroad counts at that distance, which keeps the test open to a country too small to hold two sites 200 km apart. The distance need not be stated: the parent measures it from the two places a source names, and records it in `sites.csv`. A site whose place no held source names, or one nearer than 200 km, is `other`, and the long summary says which.

The study redraws `infra.store--off-site-backup-capacity`, which as at 2026-08-31 puts 9 countries at stage 3, 24 at stage 2 and 1 at stage 1, none at 4 or 5, and 17 without a stage; three countries have no cell. It touches no other row.

| Sub-indicator | Indicator text | Id |
|---|---|---|
| Off-site backup for government systems | Off-site backup capacity: off-site backup for government systems | `infra.store--off-site-backup-capacity` |

Anything else the evidence names is `other`, noted and never staged: backup equipment or a site tendered, financed or under construction; a commercial provider selling backup or disaster recovery, unless a source states it holds government systems; a second network link or satellite route, which is redundancy of connection, not of data; backup power; a backup site nearer than 200 km, or in no named place.
#### 2. The questions

Asked in order. Each is answered with a date and a link.

| # | Question | A yes is |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does a public body have a backup site in service, 200 km or more from its main site? | A source states that a ministry, agency or the central bank keeps its systems or data at a second site, in service, in a named place at least 200 km from its main one |
| 2 | Does one serve the government's shared systems? | A source states that the backup site in service is for the national or government data centre, the government cloud, or systems run for the government as a whole |
| 3 | Is a recovery plan or test on record for it? | A source states that a disaster-recovery or continuity plan covers the government's backup, or that a failover test or recovery exercise was held |
| 4 | Are the main and backup sites both Tier III or above? | Sources state that the government's main data centre and its backup site are each Tier III or IV, by certification, a TIA-942 rating or the operator's own statement |
#### 3. The ladders

**Off-site backup for government systems**

| Stage | Answers |
|---|---|
| 1 Absent | A source from the past three years says government systems have no off-site backup or disaster-recovery site |
| 2 Preparing | Yes to 1: one public body's backup site in service, 200 km or more away |
| 3 Establishing | Yes to 2: a backup site for the government's shared systems |
| 4 Operating | Yes to 3: a recovery plan or test on record for it |
| 5 Leading | Yes to 4: main and backup sites both Tier III or above |

- **Questions 1, 2 and 4 have no window.** A site in service stands until a later source says it has closed. **Question 3 has the usual three years**: a plan or a test older than that is not a yes.
- **Stage 2 changes.** The row put procurement, a planned site and one institution's own site at stage 2. Procurement and plans are now `other`; one institution's site in service is stage 2. Most of the row's 24 countries at stage 2 stand on tenders, so many will fall to unplaced unless the reading finds a site in service.
- **Stage 3 changes.** The row let a commercial or satellite provider's redundancy under contract stand at stage 3. A contract counts only where a source says it holds government systems; a satellite link does not.
- **Stage 5 is the row's end state, asked as a fact**: the Blueprint's Tier-classified primary and secondary site. Its continuity standard and published availability are not asked: ISO 22301 certification of a government estate is rare and availability is seldom published, so either would leave stage 5 empty.
- **Every question asks about a site 200 km or more away.** A yes to questions 2 to 4 is a yes for the distant site; a backup in the same city answers none of them. The row set no distance, so countries the frame staged on a backup in the capital will fall.
- **Stage 1 needs a statement of absence**, as in every study.

`study.json` holds this as three aspects: `site` (`shared`, `service`, `none`) for questions 1 and 2, `plan` (`tested`, `plan`) for question 3, and `tier` (`both`) for question 4.
