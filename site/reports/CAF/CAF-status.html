Status report

This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

In August 2026 the digital-economy minister opened a workshop in Bangui that validated the government's digital transformation roadmap. The roadmap was drafted under the World Bank-financed public sector digital governance project, with Orange Centrafrique and its subsidiary Sofrecom. In July 2026 the ministry had reported the strategy finalised, with a 2026-2030 roadmap built on a diagnostic of digital maturity. The World Bank-financed call for a firm to draft it closed on 3 January 2025. No adoption instrument or published text had been found as at November 2026. In 2025 the World Bank recorded the GovTech strategy as planned or in draft. It also recorded no whole-of-government approach to digital transformation and no cross-government forum of senior digital officials (2025).

In July 2018 the ministry called for a firm to draft an earlier plan, Centrafrique Digitale 2025. Its studies were presented to the ministries in Bangui in November 2021. The African Development Bank's completion report of April 2024 records all five of the plan's reports as produced and validated. No act adopting that plan had been published as at October 2026. Digital governance is an explicit strand of the National Development Plan 2024-2028 (February 2026).

Loi 23.008 of 19 June 2023 created the Agence Centrafricaine de Développement du Digital to implement the national strategy for the digital sector. By 2025 the country had a data governance body, set up as an autonomous institution, and an approved data governance strategy. It had no open source software policy for the public sector and no national strategy on disruptive technologies (2025).

In April 2025 the government approved a strategic diagnostic of the digital-identity and civil-registration system. A national civil-status policy and action plan were technically validated in April 2024. No part of the national digital identity system announced in late 2025 is being deployed, and procurement and an international tender are still ahead of it.

Effective Administration scored 25.7 out of 100 in 2023, 50th of 54 African countries, after a gain of 0.3 points across the decade.

Legislation and regulation

CIVICUS Monitor reported in October 2025 that a press law of 26 May 2025 reintroduced criminal penalties for press offences and added measures against false information. The World Bank counts the enactment of a cybersecurity law and a data protection law among the results of its Public Sector Digital Governance Project (May 2025). At the project's appraisal in March 2022 the country had no comprehensive data protection body and neither cybersecurity nor cybercrime legislation.

Loi 23.008 of 19 June 2023 created the Agence Centrafricaine de Développement du Digital as a public industrial and commercial body under the digital ministry. Décret 23.276 of 17 November 2023 approved its statutes. Décret 25.104 of 9 April 2025 gave the Presidency a seat on its board in place of the commerce ministry.

Identity rests on identification laws dating from the 1960s, the World Bank recorded in 2017. The civil register sits under the Family Code of 1997. No right-to-information law requires government data to be published online, and no entity monitors such a right (2025). Article 61 of the 2018 Electronic Communications Law makes registration of every SIM card against a valid identity document mandatory. Residents of provincial towns have struggled to travel to Bangui to obtain that card (2023).

A universal service fund has existed in law since loi 18.002 of 2018 and a 2019 implementing decree, financed by a levy of 2% of operator revenue. In April 2026 the regulator listed finishing the fund's implementation among its priorities for the year. The private printer of the national identity card charged above the tariff fixed in law for five years. An interministerial order of 26 June 2025 restored the statutory price, as the electoral roll was being finalised.

Data protection

The data protection law of January 2024 provides for an independent supervisory authority, which had not been established in 2025. The law gave the digital-economy ministry twelve months to set it up, and its functions until it exists. As at January 2026 the authority was still unappointed.

Law No. 24.001 of January 2024 sets out lawfulness, transparency, purpose-limitation and storage-limitation principles. It classifies biometric and genetic data as sensitive and gives data subjects rights of access, rectification and erasure. It requires a data protection officer. Its scope extends to processing for security, defence and justice, and it exempts purely personal use.

At appraisal in March 2022 the World Bank recorded inadequate safeguards for both identification and civil registration data. The identity credentials are produced under contract by a private company (2023). The data protection law allows personal data to be transferred to states offering similar protection. Registration of every SIM card against an identity document is legally mandatory.

Regional collaboration

In July 2026 the Treasury launched JUMAC, its cash-management application, built to the joint COBAC/CEMAC rules on claims and financial activity. The Treasury has a partnership with GIMAC, the CEMAC regional payments body, for infrastructure compatible with the regional banking system.

GIMACPAY, the CEMAC instant payment scheme run by GIMAC and overseen by the regional central bank, serves the country (2023). Company records are searchable in the OHADA regional commercial register (2023). BEAC reports on payment services for the region. Holders of a biometric national identity card or CEMAC passport may travel visa-free within CEMAC, under a 2019 Additional Act given effect by a police circular in September 2024. CEMAC instruments on electronic communications, payment systems and consumer protection apply alongside national law. The 2024 data protection law sets conditions on transfers to CEMAC and ECCAS member states. In March 2026 the Smart Africa Alliance welcomed the country as one of six new member states, taking the alliance to 42.

A national optical backbone inaugurated in Bangui on 6 February 2023 runs the Bangui-Douala corridor, with spurs to Cameroon and Congo. It was financed by the African Development Bank and the European Union, which places it within its Global Gateway strategy. The cross-border component of the World Bank's earlier Central African Backbone was cancelled. The ITU attributes this to reluctance to end the incumbent's monopoly on the international gateway (2017). In May 2026 the government announced it is pursuing a digital interconnection with South Sudan.

Standards

In 2025 ARCEP acquired spectrum-monitoring and quality-of-service equipment to check operators' service quality. In 2025 the government had no interoperability framework. Its systems ran without a data quality framework, uptime monitoring or guidance for replacing legacy systems (2025). None of its financial management, treasury, customs, tax, human resources and payroll systems had formal arrangements for compliance, security and audit trails (2025). The Treasury's cash-management application is built to CEMAC supervisory rules (July 2026). The regional instant payment system shares its scheme rules only with its members and participants (2023).

Public debate and participation in policymaking

Freedom of Association & Assembly scored 0.0 out of 100 in 2023, after a fall of 25.0 points over 2014-2023. That was the country's largest single deterioration in the Ibrahim Index. Civil Society Space fell 13.1 points over the same decade, to 35.5 and 36th of 54 African countries. Media Freedom scored 61.9 and ranked 15th of 54 in 2023, up 6.7 points over the decade. Freedom of Expression & Belief scored 15.4, ranked 52nd, and fell 7.5 points. Digital Freedom, at 65.4 and 20th of 54 in 2023, is one of the country's ten best-scoring measures. It is also one of its ten most deteriorated, down 14.7 points over 2014-2023.

In 2025 the country had no national platform through which citizens or businesses can take part in policy decisions. It had no government platform for feedback or complaints on service delivery. Citizens and businesses are not involved in designing any online government service the index assesses. There is no right-to-information law (2025). The government publishes no progress, results or spending report for any digital domain assessed. Disclosure of Public Records rose 33.3 points over 2014-2023, to 33.3 out of 100. Accessibility of Public Records fell to 5.0 and 51st of 54.

The overcharging for identity cards was contested in the courts and on the street, by members of parliament and a citizens' collective (July 2025). Stakeholder research records a perception that the identity system is under foreign control (2023), and the card's contract escaping public-procurement scrutiny. In June 2026 the media regulator framed AI-generated disinformation about the President and officials as cybercrime. In September 2026 a presidential decree created a communication-monitoring cell under the government spokesperson. One of its units watches national and international media and social networks to counter rumours.

A press law of 26 May 2025 reintroduced criminal penalties for press offences and added measures against false information, on a civil-society monitor's account. During the December 2025 campaign the media regulator sanctioned the online outlet Ndjoni Sango for a false report about an opposition candidate.

Finance

Financial sustainability

The 2026 budget puts FCFA 2.5 billion of grant money into the public-sector digital governance project, up from FCFA 1 billion in 2025. The World Bank grant behind it closes on 31 December 2026.

The domestic-state share of the digital lines in the Central African Republic's budgets, at the appropriated stage, fell from 43.3% for 2025 to 39.9% for 2026, as grant-financed lines rose from about FCFA 4.5 billion to 6.0 billion while domestic lines rose from FCFA 3.4 billion to 4.0 billion.

The digital ministry's 2026 budget sets FCFA 1 billion to pay the invoices of the company V-care, against FCFA 500 million in 2025. The 2026 budget adds FCFA 500 million for the invoices of the company Mossi and FCFA 250 million for the administration's telephone arrears to SOCATEL. It also carries FCFA 118 million for six months of 2024 salary arrears at the digital development agency. No outturn against the digital appropriations had been published as at September 2026.

A 2018 electronic communications law and a 2019 decree created a universal service fund, financed by 2% of operators' previous-year revenue. In April 2026 ARCEP listed completing the fund among its 2026 priorities, alongside a draft budget of FCFA 6.2 billion. In the 2026 finance bill the state's transfer to it is FCFA 1.6 billion. The Central African firm EDEN TiiiT pre-financed the early phases of the planning ministry's Dûnîa platform (March 2026), with no repayment or licence terms published.

MoUs and other agreements

In September 2025 Greenline Technologies announced it had been awarded the privatisation of SOCATEL, the state telecom operator. The memorandum, signed in Casablanca, foresees an initial US$150 million for a national digital hub, a Tier 3 data centre, telecom infrastructure and cybersecurity frameworks (September 2025). The privatisation entered an operational phase on 16 July 2026 with the underlying agreements still unfinalised. Nothing had been built as at November 2026.

On 30 July 2026 the government signed a memorandum with the Emirati firm Transland covering digital finance and land banking. It was presented as mobilising investment for infrastructure projects while modernising land administration. No amount, disbursement schedule or governance arrangement was announced with it. The minister coordinating the Cellule des grands travaux et des investissements stratégiques signed it, extending the existing Bangui-Abu Dhabi cooperation track.

In September 2026 the national internet and security unit signed a memorandum with AMINI, a data and artificial intelligence company. It provides for a national data centre, a security operations centre and a national computer emergency response team. On 10 November 2025 the International Finance Corporation signed three agreements on digital financial services, with the Ministry of Finance, Orange Centrafrique and Ecobank Centrafrique. In January 2025 the government signed a memorandum with the Kigali-based AIAL Group to build a data centre.

An earlier rescue of SOCATEL, agreed in November 2020, needed an estimated €20 million to be raised before any work could begin.

New investments

In August 2026 the strategic committee of the country's flagship digital project counted 54% of its funds disbursed and 78% of its activities complete. That was up from a third disbursed in April 2025. It is the World Bank's Public Sector Digital Governance Project, approved on 5 May 2022. It is an IDA grant of SDR 25.1 million (US$35 million), run by the Ministry of Finance and Budget and closing on 31 December 2026. Additional financing of SDR 4.8 million (US$6.4 million) was appraised in May 2025, adding a mining-governance component.

Twenty-three digital-sector financing commitments to the Central African Republic are on record, starting between 2017 and 2026 and running to 2027 at the latest (November 2026).

The African Development Bank financed the country's share of the Central Africa Fibre-Optic Backbone over 2017 to 2024, with XDR 13,892,239, about US$19 million, for 1,050 km of cable. In November 2024 France earmarked FCFA 3.28 billion of a €10 million budget-support convention to finance-ministry reforms covering the Si_mba, SYDONIA World and e-TAX systems. In April 2024 the UN Development Programme signed a financing agreement for the second phase of its public-administration digitalisation project. The European Union finances the Pata Polélé payment system, delivered by Expertise France (April 2026). The country was one of eight in an ITU ICT benchmarking project to 2023.

Donor money committed to the country's digital sector over 2017 to 2027 comes to roughly US$88 million (November 2026). A further US$150 million announced in September 2025 is a private investment that has not been signed as financing.

In September 2026 a World Bank mission in Bangui prepared the country's component of the regional Central Africa digital integration programme, due to launch in April 2027. No national allocation had been published as at November 2026. The ministry's data centre still required co-financing when the World Bank appraised it in March 2022.

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

In 2024 the ITU placed the country among landlocked states where the median data-only mobile plan costs more than 20% of monthly income per head. On the ITU's estimate about 13.8% of people used the internet in 2024. The regulator counted 1.56 million internet subscriptions at end-June 2026, a little over 23% of the population. The 2024 Ibrahim Index ranks the country last of 54 on internet and computers, at 2.9 out of 100 in 2023.

A national optical backbone financed by the African Development Bank and the European Union was inaugurated in Bangui on 6 February 2023. It runs the Bangui-Douala corridor with spurs to Cameroon and Congo. The Bank's completion report of April 2024 records about 900 km of fibre laid. The country has no undersea cable landing and carries most international bandwidth on costly satellite links (2017). Fixed broadband reaches under 0.1% of the population (March 2022).

Orange won the first 4G licence in November 2024 and opened service on 15 May 2025 in Bangui, Bouar, Berbérati and Bossangoa. Moov Africa renewed its licence with 4G in January 2025. Telecel obtained a 4G licence in December 2025 and launched 4G+ in Bangui in May 2026. In July 2025 ARCEP granted SOS Télécoms the first passive-infrastructure licence. Starlink opened commercial service on 16 March 2026, at a monthly fee of about 136% of monthly income per head. At end-June 2026 it held more than 70% of fixed-wireless internet clients.

A national internet exchange point was created in January 2026, on a foreign operator's half-year report. No membership or traffic figure has been found. On 8 October 2026 the digital-economy ministers of the country and the Democratic Republic of Congo agreed on a fibre link to the Moanda cable landing, with no timetable or financing announced. In October 2026 a ministry official placed a route east through South Sudan under study. Vandalism on the regional backbone had partly cut services in March 2026.

In July 2026 Batangafo spent weeks without calls, internet or mobile money after the Orange network failed.

Data Storage

In August 2026 an ECCAS programme listed a government data centre for the country as one of six new centres, at a stated US$9.00m for the whole programme. The programme was a quarter of the way through. In March 2022 the World Bank had recorded two government data centres under construction, one for public financial management and one at the digital-economy ministry. The ministry facility was designed as a national platform to host any institution's systems and data, to Tier II with a later extension to Tier III. It still required co-financing at appraisal.

Four foreign-backed data centre projects for the country were announced in the twenty-one months to September 2026: a memorandum of understanding with AIAL Group in January 2025, a Tier 3 build written into the SOCATEL privatisation memorandum in September 2025, a Huawei proposal in May 2026, and a national data centre provided for in a memorandum with AMINI in September 2026. Each of them remains at memorandum or announcement stage, and an announcement is not a facility.

Fire damaged part of the national elections authority's data-processing centre in September 2026, weeks before residual elections due in October, and the government opened an investigation.

The data protection law of 2024 allows personal data to be transferred to states offering similar protection. In 2025 there was no shared government cloud and no policy on where government data may be hosted. Cloud usage, security and savings were neither monitored nor published (2025). Cloud providers serve the country only remotely, from regions such as Johannesburg (2024).

Energy

Abu Dhabi-based Global South Utilities inaugurated a 50MW solar plant with 15MWh of battery storage at Sakaï on 12 August 2026. The plant was reported to raise national generation capacity by more than 60% (August 2026). In January 2026 a generating unit at the Boali 2 hydroelectric station was repaired and returned to service.

The national energy compact put electricity access at 16.8% of the population in 2024, with a target of 50% by 2030. In 2023 it reached 37.4% in urban areas against 2.3% in rural ones, among the lowest access rates in the world. Firms that do have a connection recorded about 28 power cuts in a typical month that year. Access to Energy scores 6.5 out of 100 in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 51st of 54 African countries in 2023. The score rose 2.1 points across the decade.

Inadequate electricity supply and a weak national backbone throttle the expansion of telecommunications networks (2017). Provincial relay sites run off-grid on generators and solar, and are stripped by thieves and vandals. Repair crews drive hundreds of kilometres from Bangui over degraded roads to reach them.

Generation is almost entirely water: renewable sources produced 96.5% of electricity in 2021. That share comes almost wholly from the Boali hydropower scheme and the newer Danzi solar plant, and marks no decarbonisation drive. The off-grid programme has World Bank-financed solar mini-grids at Bambari and Berbérati, with five more mini-grids and off-grid solar solutions planned (2025).

In August 2026 the state utility ENERCA signed a partnership with Telecel to digitise electricity bill payment. The utility gives the security and traceability of its receipts as its gain.

Technical Capacity

In September 2026 Expertise France advertised the third of three contracted local IT expert posts in the finance ministry's information systems directorate. The posts belong to SAFE, a €6.8m European Union programme running from 2025 to 2028. Two of them run and administer the tax administration's SIGICA system, and the third works on its interfaces with the budget and customs systems. Pata Polélé, the government payment platform, was built with outside expertise alongside the Office National de l'Informatique (April 2026). The identity credentials are produced under contract by a private company (2023). In 2017 the World Bank's identification diagnostic left the government to seek donor funding for civil registration and identification.

In 2025 no ministry or department led public sector digital transformation, and no cross-government forum brought senior digital officials together. Government information systems ran without a data quality framework, uptime monitoring or guidance for replacing legacy systems (2025). In May 2025 the World Bank recorded missed procurement dates, weak procurement documents and late evaluation committees on its flagship digital project. That project's government network had connected 25 pilot ministries by July 2026, with cabling under way and the connectivity tender under evaluation.

Cybersecurity

A cybersecurity law and a data protection law have both been enacted, recorded as achievements of the World Bank's Public Sector Digital Governance Project (May 2025). When that project was appraised in March 2022 the country had no national cybersecurity strategy, no computer emergency response team and no security operations centre. Loi 23.008 of 19 June 2023 charged the Agence Centrafricaine de Développement du Digital with setting up a CSIRT, a security operations centre and a public key infrastructure.

None of the government management systems in use has formal governance arrangements for compliance, security and audit trails (2025). The systems are financial management, the treasury single account, customs, tax, human resources and payroll. International assessment puts the country in the weakest tier for national cybersecurity capacity in 2024. That followed a score of 3.6 on the 2018 index against 29.5 for Sub-Saharan Africa.

In June 2026, after AI-generated deepfakes of President Touadéra and other officials circulated, the media regulator framed them as cybercrime. It proposed tighter identification of SIM-card holders as the remedy. A memorandum reported in September 2026 provides for a national computer emergency response team and a security operations centre. It was signed by the national internet and security unit with the data-infrastructure company AMINI. No national CSIRT or security operations centre had been found in operation as at November 2026. The unit itself, on its own account, was created by decree in December 2024 and attached to the Presidency. It received an organisation decree and a first coordinator in January 2025.

DPI

Data Exchange

In 2025 only the financial management, tax and customs systems were recorded as passing data to other parts of government. The country had no government interoperability framework (2025). At appraisal in March 2022 it had no shared technical architecture or interoperability layer for secure data exchange. A finance-ministry call of November 2025 for a firm to design the public-finance systems' interoperability was listed as awarded in May 2026. No framework had been published as at November 2026. Systems in use ran without a data quality framework, uptime monitoring or guidance for replacing legacy systems (2025).

In 2017 the electoral database, with close to 2 million records updated every year, was the country's largest digital identity database. An interoperable identification system is a goal of the diagnostic the government validated in April 2025.

In 2022 the Guichet Unique de Formalités des Entreprises issued the commercial register number and the taxpayer number in one registration. Revenue collection was digitised on two separate tracks: online tax declaration built from the government's own resources, and a customs move to ASYCUDA World with European Union support (2022).

Digitalisation of public services is carried inside the National Development Plan 2024-2028 (February 2026).

Digital Identity and CRVS

In November 2025 the government announced a national digital identity system, but no part of it is being deployed, and its implementation window runs from 2027 to 2032. Technical, environmental and social assessments, procurement and an international tender all remain ahead of it.

On 26 June 2025 an interministerial order restored the identity card's statutory price of 4,500 FCFA. For the five years before, the producer charged 6,000 to 6,750 FCFA. The card rests on identification laws dating from the 1960s. It carries the right to travel visa-free for up to ninety days across the six CEMAC states (2024). Most of the population held no formal identification in 2022. Refugees aged twelve and over are entitled instead to a refugee identity card under a 1968 decree (2024).

The contracted producer reaches the interior by town-by-town missions that capture photograph, fingerprints and signature locally, with the cards printed in Bangui (October 2023). In July 2024 issuance reached Bambari, after being confined to Bangui since 2013. A mission went to Birao in December 2025. Another opened in Bria in August 2026, after an earlier one there issued more than 19,000 cards.

In 2026 an EU-funded project handed over its 100,000th birth certificate. Between January and September 2026 the project registered 184,563 births and digitised 130,000 acts. Running since January 2021, it is building a secure civil registration database in a pilot zone. Births, marriages and deaths all fall within the register under the 1997 Family Code, but processing is manual (2017). Civil registration is the country's strongest public administration measure in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, at 50.0 out of 100, unmoved over the decade to 2023.

Digital Payments and Fintech

On 4 August 2026 Telecel Centrafrique and the state utility ENERCA formally launched electricity-bill payment over Telecel Money. Many subscribers were already using the service. The utility gives the security and traceability of its receipts as its gain.

In 2022 about 13.7% of the population had access to a financial account. In 2023 there were roughly 15 mobile money agents per 100,000 adults, concentrated in Bangui. The regulator counted more than 702,000 active mobile-money clients at end-June 2026. In October 2026 the digital governance project tendered kits for mobile money points outside the capital, with bids due on 13 November 2026. Displaced people cannot open a bank or mobile money account without an identity card, passport or driving licence (2024). Fewer than 5% of refugees hold a national identity card (2024).

In 2022 Patapaye, a pilot paying civil servants by mobile money through Ecobank and Orange Money, had signed up 2,500 of the 7,000 targeted, with about a fifth active. By May 2025 a World Bank project had moved 1,702 unbanked civil servants posted outside Bangui onto mobile-money salaries. Pata Polélé digitises the fees levied across some twenty revenue-generating ministries, replacing cash and paper receipts (April 2026). Justice, Finance and the Interior are its pilot ministries, and traffic fines are among what it settles online.

GIMACPAY, the CEMAC instant payment system launched in July 2020, serves the country, and its scheme rules are not published (2023). On 27 July 2026 the Treasury launched its JUMAC application for the treasury single account, linked to the regional settlement platforms SYGMA and SYSTAC. Its stated object is to close the parallel accounts holding state balances outside it. The 2024 finance law introduced a 1% tax on all digital transactions. In April 2024 the digital minister ordered Orange to reverse a mobile-money fee rise made in anticipation of it.

Registries

In late 2024 biometric voter registration ran on tablet-based kits ahead of the first local elections in about 36 years. The December 2024 drive deployed roughly 3,000 tablets and about 1,469 agents with UN Development Programme backing, in Bangui from 24 November to 7 December and nationwide to mid-December. Where registrants had no documents, authorities were to rely on testimony from traditional chiefs. The roll runs on a system from the Dutch firm Genkey, used since the 2020 general elections and revised in 2024. In 2017 the electoral database was already the country's largest digital identity database.

In 2021 about 0.1% of the country's land was titled and registered, and no digital cadastral system existed. The Conservation Foncière was the sole body keeping the cadastre and land transactions (2021). Its title records were kept on paper, neither computerised nor scanned (2021).

In 2023 Central African entries in the OHADA regional commercial register could be searched online by name or RCCM number. On 12 May 2026 the business single window launched a website, while company creation remained largely manual. Among surveyed firms, the share formally registered when they began operating has fallen sharply since 2011 (2023).

In 2025 the country had no national social protection register. A unified social registry has been in development with donor support since 2018, delayed by limited technical capacity and institutional change. Individual safety-net programmes keep their own beneficiary registries (2025). There is no digital address register or standard addressing system, and mail is routed by locality or district name. Between 2011 and 2016 a programme of the francophone mayors' association and the European Union drew an address plan for Bangui and numbered doors. No later record of the address file it built has been found.

Sectoral management information systems

On 8 October 2026 the government received a criminal data processing centre for the police and gendarmerie, financed by MINUSCA and UNODC. The interior minister said it would centralise fingerprint collection and digitise and archive criminal records. The project's first phase was funded in January 2023, with US$127,690 from MINUSCA and US$30,000 from UNODC. Extension to the judicial police and the regions is the stated next step (October 2026). On 30 September 2026 the Special Criminal Court received an electronic document management chain for its judicial archives, funded by France.

In 2025 none of the government management systems in use had formal governance arrangements for compliance, security or audit trails. Those systems cover financial management, the treasury single account, customs, tax, human resources and payroll. The financial management information system runs on off-the-shelf software, covers treasury and budget preparation, and reaches central and local government (2025). A customs system runs alongside it, the customs and tax administrations having merged (2025). A debt management system covers foreign and domestic debt (2025). A payroll system is linked to a human resources system that was still being implemented (2025). A payroll clean-up removed more than 2,000 ghost workers (May 2025). There is no public investment management system, and so no project database (2025).

In 2024 DHIS2, the health information system, ran from central and subnational data offices, and every user interviewed said it needed improvement. Health data was still gathered on paper forms and registers, against poor internet connectivity and insecurity (2022). Schools have reported by SMS through EduTrac since 2019, feeding the education management information system (2023). In February 2026 the planning ministry launched an internal platform for its own budget, staff and project data.

Other GovTech and e-Gov

The Gouvernemental Centralisé portal launched on 10 December 2025 as a single entry point to the state. It offers administrative procedures, tracking of requests and complaints, and access to official documents. Health, education and tax services were announced as future modules (2025). A national portal for online government services launched on 14 October 2026. It lists driving licences, criminal-record extracts, nationality and school certificates and medical appointments, with no usage figure published. About 12% of people in the country were online at the end of 2025.

The tax administration launched e-Tax on 24 March 2025 with four modules, one of them registration for the unique identification number. In 2025 it was the only citizen- or business-facing platform assessed as universally accessible across channels. In August 2025 the finance minister said more than half of the large taxpayers concerned still did not file or pay through it, and gave them until 15 August before audits. In 2025 the index recorded no e-procurement portal, no government job portal, no online social insurance portal and no way to start a business online. The social security fund had opened online declaration to employers on 26 May 2025. Consultants under the digital governance project designed a phased move to e-procurement, starting with health and education pilots (September 2025).

Of the five citizen- or business-facing portals the 2025 index assesses — public service, tax, jobs, open data, and social insurance and pensions — only the tax portal exists, and the ten government systems the country does run are otherwise all back-office administration. Four separate government digital platforms were launched in the fourteen months to April 2026, each with a different owning institution and a different financier.

The country sits in the low group of the UN's E-Government Development Index for 2024, scoring below 0.25. In August 2026 the World Bank's digital governance project had disbursed 54% of its funds with four months to run. In May 2025 the Bank recorded planned procurement dates not respected, weak procurement documents and evaluation committees not convened on time.

Digitalisation

Digitalisation of sub-national government

On 12 December 2025 the Bangui town hall signed a convention with the Ivorian company BMI-WFS to digitise the collection of the capital's municipal revenue. The minister for territorial administration chaired the signing. On 20 May 2025 the town hall's civil registry began producing birth, marriage and death certificates on a UNDP-financed platform. In December 2020 its IT service began a census of city-centre traders to build a taxpayer database.

In July 2026 the civil-registry directorate reported a national register of more than 225,000 birth records, with 77% of processing done by hand. Registry centres had been rehabilitated with solar power and computers, and integrated civil-registry software was in development. On 16 and 17 July 2026 its agents were trained in Bangui to scan civil-registry documents under a project financed by the European Union. No prefectural authority ran an administrative system of its own as at October 2026.

Rural digital data capture

In 2025 MINUSCA began fitting a few provincial police stations and gendarmerie brigades, Baoro and Kaga-Bandoro among them, with computers, printers and solar power. Reports previously written by hand could then be typed. In 2018 the same mission had supplied office registers to 30 police and gendarmerie units.

In 2022 health data in the districts was still gathered on paper forms and registers. The national health information system ran from central and subnational data offices (2024). Births and deaths were recorded by hand on non-standard paper forms, including at village registration centres and health facilities (2017). Rural primary schools keep registers on paper, but principals report enrolment and attendance by SMS into a central system, a channel opened in 2019 and still working in 2023.

In October 2023 enrolment missions took applicants' photographs, fingerprints and signatures in their own towns and printed identity cards in Bangui. In December 2025 a mission deployed to Birao, in the far north-eastern Vakaga prefecture.

In April 2026 President Touadéra inaugurated the country's first free public Wi-Fi, at Bocaranga, installed through Russian cooperation.

Technology

AI

In September 2026 the national internet and security unit signed a cooperation memorandum with AMINI, a data-infrastructure and artificial intelligence company. It followed President Touadéra's meeting with the company's founder at the UN General Assembly. The projects envisaged cover mining, agriculture, health, education, forests, climate and public administration. In October 2026 the Africa AI Governance Index scored the country 0.25 out of 4, 51st of 54, in its lowest tier. In November 2024 the digital-economy minister said a national AI strategy for 2024-2028 was being drafted. No text of it had been published as at October 2026. In 2025 the country had no national strategy on disruptive or innovative technologies.

In June 2025 the government named an Indian firm as its partner for an AI centre of excellence, with no financing or timetable disclosed. In June 2026 the Haut Conseil de la Communication called AI-generated deepfakes of President Touadéra and other officials cybercrime. It proposed tighter identification of SIM-card holders, and nothing had been adopted as at November 2026. The data protection law extends to decisions taken by automated processing.

In 2024 MSF installed an AI-assisted chest X-ray screening device at Batangafo hospital with the health authorities. In 2025 it detected 90 of the 203 tuberculosis cases found in the zone. An AI model built with the national tuberculosis programme mapped likely hotspots across Bangui on 100-metre grids (2025). In August 2024 the Institut Pasteur de Bangui presented MEDICINE, a four-year project pairing AI diagnostic aids with rapid tests for childhood fever.

ICT Industry

In 2025 there was no policy to support GovTech startups, no government financing for innovation by small firms, and no procurement preference for their bids. On 11 December 2025 the National Assembly adopted a public procurement code with a 5% preference margin for national and regional firms, and its promulgation has not been found. The regulator put operators' combined revenue above FCFA 41.68 billion for the first half of 2026. In July 2025 ARCEP granted SOS Télécoms the country's first licence to build and lease passive infrastructure. Telecel launched 4G+ in Bangui in May 2026. In 2023 firms reported about 28 power cuts in a typical month. In July 2024 the finance minister promised a guarantee fund and a legal framework for startups and small firms. Neither had been found on record as at November 2026.

Innovation ecosystem

In 2025 the state had no public sector innovation strategy or programme, no innovation lab, and no collaboration with the private sector on innovation. In January 2024 a digital training centre and start-up incubator opened at the University of Bangui. The African Development Bank and the European Union funded it. The same Bank programme committed a cybersecurity and e-certification platform and help to set up a national digital development agency (2023). On 30 October 2026 the women entrepreneurs' federation FAFECA opened a digital incubator in Bangui, financed by the European Union and the French development agency. In 2024 AfriLabs' directory listed Centrafrique Tech as an active hub in the country. The country is absent from the 133 economies ranked in WIPO's Global Innovation Index 2024.

Capacity

Literacy

In the 2024 Ibrahim Index, enrolment scored 41.3 out of 100 in 2023, 23rd of 54 African countries, after a rise of 8.1 points over the decade. Equality in education scored 17.6, ranked 52nd and fell 5.4 points. Education as a whole scored 19.6, 52nd of 54, with education quality at 0.0 and unchanged since 2014. The index holds no data for the country on whether children complete school or on who teaches them.

In 2025 the government ran no digital skills programmes for citizens or for schools. In 2023 rural primary schools kept registers on paper, and principals reported enrolment and attendance to the ministry through EduTrac, an SMS channel.

Training and skills

In 2025 the government had no strategy or programme to build digital skills in the public sector. In January 2024 a digital training centre and start-up incubator opened at the University of Bangui. The African Development Bank and the European Union funded it. The same Bank programme covered a cybersecurity and e-certification platform and help to set up the digital development agency (2023). By May 2025 civil servants had been trained under the World Bank's digital governance project, women a minority of them. In April 2026 a training workshop on the Pata Polélé payment system ran in Bangui for its pilot ministries. The University of Bangui's Institut Supérieur de Technologie supplies most of the country's holders of a professional licence in computer engineering.

On 9 October 2026 the government signed a five-year partnership with Russia, through HSE University, for digital-governance training of public servants, joint research and expert seminars.

Research institutions

As at August 2026 the University of Bangui's Institut supérieur de technologie supplied most of the country's professional engineering graduates and ran a master's in engineering sciences. Its fields are civil, industrial, computing, mining and geological engineering. No Central African research institution had been found producing published work on the country's digital development as at August 2026.

Inclusion

Access to services

Equal access to public services fell 18.7 points over 2014-2023 to 10.4 out of 100, 53rd of 54 African countries. It was one of the country's three sharpest declines on any Ibrahim Index measure (2023).

In 2017 the World Bank found that fewer than a fifth of the population held a national identity card even before the 2013 crisis destroyed its central production site. In October 2024, with registrants unable to produce documents, voter registration was to rely on traditional chiefs' testimony to establish identity.

In June 2025 an interministerial order restored the identity card's legal tariff, after the issuing company had charged above it for five years. The price had been contested in the administrative courts and on the street from 2020. The order came as the electoral roll was being finalised, with the card required in order to vote.

In 2024 the World Bank recorded that refugees aged twelve and over are entitled to a refugee identity card under a 1968 decree. A bank or mobile money account cannot be opened without an identity card, passport or driving licence, and fewer than 5% of refugees hold a national identity card (2024). UNHCR reported 37,389 Sudanese refugees registered in the second quarter of 2026. Registration is card-based through WFP, and a complaints and feedback system logged 535 complaints at the Korsi centre alone.

In October 2023 enrolment missions worked town by town, capturing photographs and fingerprints locally while the cards were printed in Bangui. A mission reached Birao in December 2025. The April 2025 strategic diagnostic recommends more enrolment centres, decentralised processing and a minimum service standard for issuance.

In 2025 the tax portal was the only citizen-facing government platform assessed as offering universal or omnichannel access. In 2020 information and communication largely remained inaccessible to people with disabilities, for want of sign-language interpreters and braille documents.

Digital divides

Women's access to public services scored 0.0 out of 100 on the Ibrahim Index in 2023, last of 54 African countries and 10.1 points below ten years earlier. Equal socioeconomic opportunity scored 4.5, also last, after a fall of 15.6 points over the same decade. Over those years mobile communications improved faster than almost any other measure the index tracks. Enrolment is among the country's stronger measures and equality in education among its weakest.

In 2020 a gigabyte of mobile data cost 19.4% of GNI per capita. In 2024 the median data-only mobile plan still cost more than 20% of monthly income per head, over ten times the affordability target. Electricity reaches a small minority of the population and is close to absent outside the towns (2023). Fixed broadband barely exists (2022), and the country has no undersea cable landing of its own.

In May 2025 Orange took 4G to provincial towns. In July 2026 a network failure left Batangafo without calls, internet or mobile money for weeks. The regulator counted 1.56 million internet subscriptions at end-June 2026, a little over 23% of the population. A publisher's estimate put people using the internet at 12.0% at the end of 2025.

Data

National statistics

On 23 September 2026 the statistics institute presented provisional results of the fourth general population and housing census to the census technical committee. It is the country's first digital census and its first count since 2003. Approval by the national census commission, chaired by the prime minister, comes next. Enumeration covered 96% of the country (May 2026).

From July 2026 the institute ran a harmonised household living-conditions survey of about 7,000 households, entirely by tablet. Its second wave began on 17 October 2026 and extends collection to regions left out of the first.

In the ten years to 2023 the country ran two health surveys, one household survey and one agricultural survey, and no labour force or business survey. It had held a population census in the past twenty years, but no agricultural or economic census (2023). On the World Bank's Statistical Performance Indicators, data use, services and products scored 50-69%, data infrastructure 20-49% and data sources under 20% (2023).

A UNDP-financed mid-term evaluation of the second national strategy for statistics, held in Bangui on 7 to 9 October 2026, is to revise its indicators and calendar. The draft statistics law replacing the 2001 law awaits the commission on draft official texts.

Capacity of the Statistical System scored 19.0 out of 100 in 2023, 53rd of 54, after a ten-year gain of 12.3 points. The 2024 Ibrahim Index holds no data for the country on 17 of its 96 indicators.

In 2017 civil registration offices recorded births, marriages and deaths by hand on non-standard paper forms. The latest national measure of birth registration completeness dates from 2010. The April 2025 identity and civil-registration diagnostic recommends producing statistics systematically from registration.

Open data

Disclosure of Public Records rose 33.3 points over the decade to 2023, to 33.3 out of 100 and 27th of 54 African countries. Accessibility of Public Records fell 4.5 points over the same decade, to 5.0 and 51st.

Across the 23 data categories Open Data Watch assessed in 2024, the average openness score is 5.2 out of 10, and ten of the 23 score 5 or below.

In 2024 government finance and pollution data were the most open official statistics, each at 7.5 out of 10. Balance-of-payments data scored 2.5 and food security data 3.5 (2024). In June 2026 the statistics institute made every indicator in its ODIN space downloadable as SDMX, CSV and JSON, each with its licence. In April 2026 an African Development Bank meeting gave it a model open data licence to adopt by 22 April. In September 2026 it was preparing to move its Open Data Platform portal to version 2.0, testing a local installation loaded with the national accounts. It posts the education yearbooks from 2015-16 to 2024-25 as open data under a CC BY 4.0 licence.

In 2025 there was no government-wide open data portal, no e-procurement portal and no procurement data published to the Open Contracting Data Standard. With no public investment management system, there is no project database to publish from (2025).

In 2025 the government published no progress, results or spending report for any digital domain the GovTech index assesses, and the GovTech institution's annual report was internal. The country has no right-to-information law, and no entity monitors such a right (2025). In July 2026 the digital ministry put the digital governance project's component progress online, at 52% complete.

The unified public-services portal launched in December 2025 carries ministries' official documents and regulatory texts. Company records are searchable by name or RCCM number through OHADA's regional commercial register (2023). A public statistics portal is planned within a platform the planning ministry launched in February 2026.

Geospatial data

In April 2023 FAO trained ten agriculture ministry experts in using space-based data for agriculture and hydrometeorology. The aeronautical meteorological centre at Bangui airport lists satellite reception among its instruments (August 2025). Only 3 of the country's 14 synoptic weather stations were working in 2022, and there was no multi-hazard early warning system. A continental research body projected the 2025 cassava harvest from satellite data (October 2025). No national Earth observation programme or geospatial data policy had been found as at November 2026.

Geopolitics

US / hyperscaler activities

On 18 September 2025 the US firm Greenline Technologies was awarded the privatisation of the state operator SOCATEL, under a memorandum signed in Casablanca. The memorandum promises a Tier 3 data centre and a national connectivity network, announced at US$150 million (September 2025). The revival entered an operational phase on 16 July 2026 with the underlying agreements still unfinalised.

China activities

In May 2026 the government and a Huawei delegation discussed a programme to modernise the administration's digital infrastructure, including a Tier III national data centre. The partnership is also to link ministries by microwave and support a national data centre (2026). No construction start, contract or completion date had been found for any of it as at November 2026.

EU activities

The tax administration launched its e-Tax platform on 24 March 2025, backed by the European Union under a 24-month service contract for electronic filing and payment (2025). The European Union finances the Pata Polélé payment digitalisation, delivered by Expertise France (April 2026). In November 2024 France earmarked part of a €10 million budget-support convention to finance-ministry reforms covering the Si_mba, SYDONIA World and e-TAX systems.

Since 2021 CIVIPOL has run an EU-funded modernisation of the civil register, building a secure digital civil registration database in a pilot zone. From January to September 2026 it registered 184,563 births and digitised 130,000 acts. The national digital identity system launched in November 2025 is co-financed by the African Development Bank and the European Union. It is to unify the civil registry and biometric databases under a single identification number.

In February 2023 the European Union placed the national optical backbone within its Global Gateway strategy. The African Development Bank's representative said it would end the country's "enclavement digital" by tying it to submarine cables landing in coastal neighbours. Orange won the country's first 4G licence in November 2024. In January 2024 an incubator funded by the African Development Bank and the European Union opened at the University of Bangui.

Set against the announced but unsigned US pipeline, European money — France bilaterally, the European Union through its programmes and its co-financing — sits behind the World Bank and the African Development Bank among the financiers of the country's digital sector (August 2026).

Gulf/UAE activities

On 30 July 2026 the government signed a memorandum with the Emirati firm Transland covering digital finance and land banking. It was presented as mobilising investment for infrastructure projects while modernising land administration. Pascal Bida Koyagbele, the minister coordinating the Cellule des grands travaux et des investissements stratégiques, signed it for the government. It extends the existing Bangui-Abu Dhabi cooperation track. No amount, disbursement schedule or governance arrangement was announced with it. On 12 August 2026 the Abu Dhabi-based Global South Utilities inaugurated a 50MW solar plant with battery storage at Sakaï.

India activities

India held no position in the Central African Republic's digital sector, commercial or governmental, as at August 2026.

Digital sovereignty

In April 2026 the digital-economy ministry deactivated Starlink kits roaming in the country without homologation, citing national security and the traceability of users.

In 2025 there was no government policy on where government data may be hosted and no shared government cloud. The 2024 data protection law lets personal data go to states offering similar protection, with conditions on transfers within CEMAC and ECCAS. No instrument fixing where state data is held, or under whose law it is processed, was in force as at September 2026.

In 2020 deputies called the custody of citizens' data a matter of sovereignty after Al Madina, a company registered in Oman and run by a Lebanese businessman, was chosen to produce the identity card. Its contract escaped public-procurement scrutiny (2023). The digital identity system announced in November 2025 is to put identity data under a new national identification office, with deployment from 2027. Where the national identity database is hosted, and under whose jurisdiction, had not been published as at September 2026.

In June 2026 Huawei's programme was reported to cover a Tier III national data centre and microwave links between ministries. Greenline Technologies holds the SOCATEL privatisation memorandum. AIAL Group signed a data-centre memorandum with the government. AMINI's memorandum names the state's technological sovereignty and the protection of its data among its aims. None of the four had published terms on data jurisdiction, portability or exit as at September 2026.

A government data centre for the country is one of six new centres an ECCAS programme plans across the community (August 2026).
