Status report

This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

Mauritania scored 42.5 of 100 for overall governance in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 41st of 54 African states, against a continental average of 49.3. Its score rose 4.1 points over the decade to 2023, and 0.7 points over the last five years of it.

The National Digital Transformation Agenda 2022–2025 was the governing strategy. Its public-administration pillar made interoperability and a national government cloud and intranet explicit objectives, with targets to double internet usage and triple digital-economy employment. On 4 December 2025 the digital ministry announced that preparation of a National Digital Transformation Agenda 2030 had begun. No text of the successor had been found as at November 2026. In November 2025 the digital ministry listed policies on the Internet of Things and on blockchain, and a digital economy vision, as in drafting or approval.

A 68-page IPv6 transition strategy published in 2024 targets 25% migration by 2026. A national IPv6 committee the strategy provides for runs it. A national strategy for open and interoperable data rests on three axes. They are an interoperability framework across government, open-data governance on Open Government Partnership principles, and open data for innovation. A national artificial intelligence strategy was published in 2024.

UNDP handed the government a costed design for ANETA, a standing state digital agency under the digital ministry, on 31 July 2021. It put the agency at around 50 staff, EUR 220,000 to establish and EUR 1.4 million a year to run (2021). An e-Cabinet platform and a public-procurement portal were among its proposed quick wins.

In July 2026 the digital ministry presented wide IPv6 adoption as a foundation for the planned 5G rollout and for cloud, IoT and AI services. The sector-by-sector economic-impact figures in its IPv6 strategy are taken from a 2022 consultancy report, not measured for Mauritania. On 27 July 2026 UNDP's representative told a workshop on blockchain in public administration that real transformation begins with a change in public governance. In September 2026 the Council of Ministers took a statement proposing an access threshold of 16 for social media, with recommendation algorithms off by default for minors.

Legislation and regulation

On 29 July 2026 Mauritania's Council of Ministers approved a draft decree replacing the 2020 decree that governs the universal-access fund. The communiqué announcing it did not reproduce the new terms.

Décret n° 2022-009 governs electronic exchanges between users and administrations, and between administrations, under the electronic transactions law of 2018. Loi n° 2021-005 of 10 February 2021 amends the Commercial Code and carries the trade register. Loi n° 2025-011 of 24 February 2025 replaces provisions of the 2011 Civil Status Code. Loi n° 2025-006 of 19 February 2025 carries the investment code, and its application decree of August 2025 provides for a single window.

Two bills were in train in 2026: one on access to and use of public information, under discussion since April 2026, and one on the national archives, adopted by the Council of Ministers in March 2026.

Law No. 2016-007 is the cybercrime statute. Law No. 2017-020 of 22 July 2017 protects personal data, but it had still not been brought fully into force in 2024. No dedicated cross-government data-sharing law was in effect (2024). Décret n° 2021-138 of 3 August 2021 governs the secure chip national identity card. No dedicated digital-identity legislation has been enacted, and the digital ID rests on decree and civil-registration law (2024).

Loi n° 2024-002 P.R on technology and innovative start-ups was promulgated on 2 January 2024. The sponsoring ministry's page names and dates the law but carries no text of it. A law on combating the manipulation of information prohibits spreading false or misleading information likely to distort elections, disturb public peace or harm army morale. It also penalises montages of a person's words or images, a term the law leaves undefined.

Data protection

Mauritania's personal data protection law of 2017 had still not been brought fully into force in 2024, and doing so is the first priority of the data protection authority's 2023–2026 strategic plan.

Law No. 2017-020 of 22 July 2017 covers processing by public and private bodies and created the Autorité de Protection des Données à caractère Personnel as national regulator. Data subjects hold rights of access, rectification, erasure and objection under it. A decree of 18 February 2022 sets the authority's composition and functioning.

The authority's deliberation 004/2025 sets the conditions for transferring personal data abroad, with a list of countries treated as adequate. Deliberation 005/2025, in the amended text published on 30 April 2026, requires prior authorisation for public and private bodies using cloud services abroad. A separate transfer authorisation applies where the host country is not on the approved list. Contracts must cover compliance with the 2017 law, return of the data at expiry and its deletion by the provider.

The authority's site carried an activity stream running to May 2026. Its monthly sessions have approved an inspection reference framework and the qualification of its sworn agents and given a conditional opinion on interconnecting the Maadeni information system with the national population register. Another session took up the hosting of the state digital agency's data and the accreditation of the Nouakchott data centre.

No general legal data-localisation requirement applied in 2026. The law restricts cross-border transfers to jurisdictions offering adequate protection.

Digital freedom fell 20.3 points over 2014–2023 to 40.0 of 100, 36th of 54 African states. It was the second most deteriorated of the 96 measures in the 2024 Ibrahim Index. In 2021 the UN Human Rights Committee noted that police records of detainees were poorly maintained.

Regional collaboration

On 5 September 2025 fifteen states adopted a Model Policy Framework for Information Integrity in West Africa and the Sahel at Praia. Mauritania was not among the signatories.

The ECOWAS Supplementary Act on data protection of 16 February 2010 covers processing in a UEMOA or ECOWAS member state. Mauritania is a member of neither. The BCEAO's instant-payment platform opened in September 2025 with participants from the eight WAEMU states alone. No Mauritanian participation appears in the pan-African instant payment network as at June 2026.

The ECOWAS roaming regulation binds member states, and the reduced-cost community roaming launched in April 2026 runs between Senegal, Togo and Benin. The regional unique-identification programme runs across six ECOWAS states, and Mauritania is not among them.

Mauritania has ratified the African Union's Malabo Convention on cyber security and personal data protection. In 2021 Mali, Guinea, Mauritania and Senegal agreed to link their networks over 1,728 km of fibre on the Senegal River basin authority's power grid. Mauritania sits on the liaison committee of the Trans-Saharan fibre backbone, which met in Algiers in July 2024. It is one of four country beneficiaries of the World Bank's Western Africa Regional Digital Integration Program. The digital transformation ministry is the implementing agency. It is not a member of the Open Government Partnership, to which the three comparators in its own open-data strategy belong.

Standards

Every licensed telecommunications operator in Mauritania had activated IPv6 by July 2026. The national transition strategy sets 25% migration by 2026, in three phases: preparation, dual-stack operation and continuous monitoring.

In March 2025 the government held a national workshop with German cooperation to set unified standards for digital public services. On 28 September 2026 the digital ministry began training its officials and 120 new staff to apply them to every new digital service. Its government design system, Ijitil, is published openly under a Creative Commons licence (November 2026). A 2024 scoping study for the digital ministry sets out a general interoperability framework and an interoperability hub. Electronic exchange between administrations rests on the 2022 decree, with the general interoperability framework proposed and not yet in place (June 2023).

The national standards office is Mauritania's ISO correspondent member, participating in five technical committees and observing ten (October 2026). On 12 August 2026 it opened 45 draft Mauritanian standards to a 60-day public enquiry, under decree 2024-172 of 9 December 2024.

Public debate and participation in policymaking

Mauritania ranked 5th of 54 African states for media freedom in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, at 73.8 of 100 after a 10.6-point gain over the decade. It ranked 41st for accountability and transparency, its worst governance dimension, at 22.6. It ranked 41st again for freedom of expression and belief, at 38.4 and down 2.5 points.

On 9 and 10 April 2026 the police Cybercrime Unit arrested two opposition members of parliament under the 2021 law on national symbols, over online videos criticising the President. On 4 May 2026 both were sentenced to four years in prison, with their phones confiscated and their social-media accounts ordered closed. A news site was shut down in August 2025 on a complaint from the media regulator. A journalist was held in October 2025 over a Facebook call for peaceful protests.

Mobile data was cut for 22 days after the June 2024 presidential election. The government spokesman said the decision to cut the internet followed legal and regulatory procedures. Mobile internet was cut again during the June and July 2025 examinations.

Disclosure of public records is among the country's ten worst measures in the 2024 Ibrahim Index. Rural representation and participation rose over 2014–2023 and is among the country's best-scoring measures.

In September 2023 a consultation run alongside drafting of the Startup Act found most respondents rating the existing regulatory framework poorly and the draft bill well. In August 2026 the Inspection Générale d'État put the financial impact of irregularities in its 2024–2025 exercises at over two billion ouguiyas, against more than thirty billion audited. It stated the sum as recovered. Its other findings were internal-control failures, fictitious competition and unjustified direct awards, with public procurement the domain most exposed to risk.

Finance

Financial sustainability

The revised 2026 finance law carries MRU 209 million of IDA loan for the regional digital integration programme WARDIP, and MRU 150 million for the regional statistics harmonisation project (FY2026). The revised 2025 law carried MRU 75 million and MRU 80 million on the same lines (FY2025).

The domestic-state share of the digital lines of the revised 2026 finance law is 73.5% at the revised stage, down from 87.5% in 2025 (FY2026).

The revised 2026 law pays MRU 82.5 million for Microsoft licences, voted at MRU 105 million (FY2026). In 2026 it pays MRU 68.7 million from domestic funds for running the national registry agency ANRPTS (FY2026). In 2026 it pays MRU 40 million for maintaining the customs information system, from a special treasury account whose receipts it does not name (FY2026). The state's counterpart to the EIB-financed second submarine cable was cut in the 2026 revision from MRU 64.9 million voted to MRU 23.9 million (FY2026).

Article 6 of the revised 2026 finance law creates a special allocation account under the digital ministry for universal access to electronic communications. It is financed from the sectoral universal-access account, up to its 2026 ceiling. In July 2026 the Council of Ministers approved a draft decree replacing the fund's 2020 decree. Among its grounds was the lack of a clear framework for folding the fund into the state's budget. The fund's line stood at MRU 233.8 million in the 2026 budget (FY2026). The 2025 revision had taken it from MRU 233.8 million to MRU 834.8 million (FY2025).

The digital minister credited the World Bank's WARDIP programme for the national cloud inaugurated on 29 June 2026. No storage capacity, migration timeline or total cost was disclosed at its launch.

MoUs and other agreements

On 30 July 2026 Omani and Mauritanian institutions and companies signed five memoranda on digital cooperation, during an Omani delegation's visit. They cover an IoT water-metering pilot, technology services, joint bids in surveillance and mapping, and training. On 14 April 2026 Mauritania and the Islamic Development Bank signed an agreement in principle on a digital financing package of about US$50 million. It covers digital infrastructure, cybersecurity, interoperability and skills. Mauritania's data protection authority signed a cooperation agreement with Morocco's CNDP at Marrakech in January 2026. On 31 May 2023 the digital ministry signed a memorandum with the Abu Dhabi group G42 and its listed subsidiary Presight, covering a national data centre and a sovereign cloud. No value has been found for the G42 or Omani memoranda, and no disbursement schedule for the Islamic Development Bank package.

New investments

In July 2025 the project connecting Mauritania to the EllaLink submarine cable was launched at Nouadhibou. The European Investment Bank finances it, with European Union support. It links the country directly to networks in Europe and the Americas. The EIB had signed a EUR 25 million loan in December 2020 for the first direct high-speed link to Europe and West Africa. Mauritania is a landing country of the MEDUSA system, backed by a EUR 38.5 million EIB loan and a EUR 40 million EU grant (2022). It is one of eight landing points on Maroc Telecom's EUR 150 million West Africa cable (2023).

In November 2023 the World Bank appraised an IDA credit to Mauritania of SDR 45.7 million, US$60 million equivalent. It falls under the Western Africa Regional Digital Integration Program. Mauritania is one of four country beneficiaries, with The Gambia, Guinea and Guinea-Bissau. On 16 September 2026 Mauritania signed a EUR 25.8 million IDA additional financing for the programme, for broadband, online services and electronic payments. The Council of Ministers adopted the bill to ratify it on 14 October 2026. An earlier US$30 million development policy operation supported private-sector, digital and human capital reform in 2021 and 2022.

Germany's BMZ is funding a EUR 4 million digital transformation programme from 2024 to 2027. A EUR 2.5 million French development agency grant, channelled through UN Women, is modernising the statistics institutes of Mauritania, Senegal and Chad (2024).

Mauritania's digital estate has drawn 35 external commitments, worth about US$294 million on windows running from 2015 to 2037 (November 2026). Regional operations naming it among several beneficiaries sit outside that count. Connectivity takes seven of them and about two-thirds of the money (2015-2026); nothing funds digital identity. The European Investment Bank is the largest financier by value, at US$116 million across five commitments over 2020-2025: three submarine cables, a terrestrial cable and the national data centre. The World Bank follows with three commitments worth US$105 million (2021-2026). Loans carry more of the money than grants (2015-2026); the 26 grants fund policy support, a reform operation, a surveillance system and statistics.

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

On 10 August 2026 Chinguitel switched on Mauritania's first fifth-generation mobile network at a Nouakchott ceremony, attended by the digital transformation minister. Chinguitel is a subsidiary of Sudan's Sudatel Group and entered the market in 2007. It set out a 2027–2030 investment plan covering coverage, transport and fibre networks, and national data centres. On 9 November 2026 Mattel launched commercial 5G in Nouakchott and Nouadhibou, with a phased rollout to every town.

The national Internet Exchange Point was officially launched in May 2025, hosted at International Mauritanian Telecommunications in Nouakchott. It is run jointly by government and the operators, and participation is limited to the IMT consortium and the three national operators. Every licensed operator had activated IPv6 by July 2026. By July 2026 one operator had moved more than 15% of its subscribers, against a national target of 25% by the end of the year.

37.4% of individuals used the internet in 2023, against about 113 mobile cellular subscriptions per 100 people in 2022. On 9 October 2026 a regulator re-inspection was reported to have found all three mobile operators still breaching their licence obligations on voice and data. On 5 November 2026 the regulator fined the three a combined MRU 421.3m, 3% of each one's turnover, and ordered a network audit. In 2025 a 1GB mobile basket cost 1.47% of GNI per capita, inside the UN Broadband Commission's affordability threshold. 5GB cost 2.94% and fixed broadband around 17.6% in 2025.

Mobile communications rose 17.0 points over 2014–2023 to 56.0 of 100 in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, still 43rd of 54 African states. Access to the internet and to computers reached 24.4 of 100, 38th of 54, on a gain of 5.5 points. Infrastructure overall scores 33.3 of 100, 35th of 54.

Data Storage

On 29 June 2026 Mauritania launched a national government cloud, giving agencies shared computing and storage and holding public data inside national borders. No migration timeline was announced. The National Data Centre in Nouakchott was inaugurated in May 2025, financed by a EUR 15 million European Investment Bank loan. It is certified Tier III by the Uptime Institute and operated by International Mauritanian Telecommunications. It runs to 1,372 m2 and 100 server racks, stated as extensible.

No general data-localisation requirement applied in 2026, and the national cloud is a sovereignty initiative, not a mandate. Amazon Web Services operates no region in Mauritania. Chinguitel names national data centres among its 2027–2030 investments. On 9 November 2026 Mattel opened a Tier III redundancy and network-supervision centre for its own systems and data.

Energy

50.3% of Mauritania's population had access to electricity in 2023, against 93.5% of the urban population in the same year. Access to energy scores 47.2 of 100 in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 21st of 54 African states and up 7.4 points over 2014–2023. It is Mauritania's best infrastructure measure after mobile communications.

Hydro, wind and solar together supplied close to half of electricity output in 2024. Firms reported several outages in a typical month when the measure was last taken, in 2014. In April 2024 the electricity company sought quotations to connect the national data centre to two 4 x 800 kVA delivery substations.

Technical Capacity

Mauritania's IPv6 strategy of 2024 weights its costing to human resources, on the stated ground that most installed equipment already supports the protocol. A national IPv6 committee provided for by the strategy runs the transition.

Effective administration scores 45.1 of 100 in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 36th of 54 African states, after a gain of 8.8 points over 2014–2023.

In July 2021 UNDP delivered a costed proposal, at the government's request, for a standing state digital agency under the digital ministry. The national data strategy proposes revising the decree that created the Agence Numérique de l'État to make it the national interoperability operator. UNDP records that national stakeholders still need considerable capacity building to govern the open-source components of the e-ID system. An external vendor built the system with a local partner.

Tertiary ICT and STEM provision rests on the University of Nouakchott Al Aasriya's Faculté des Sciences et Techniques, the École Supérieure Polytechnique and the Institut Supérieur du Numérique (2024). Private providers such as Groupe ISI and ESPRI sit alongside them.

Cybersecurity

Mauritania sits in the evolving band of the ITU's 2024 Global Cybersecurity Index. Law No. 2016-007 is the country's cybercrime statute, and Mauritania has ratified the African Union's Malabo Convention.

On 22 April 2024 the Council of Ministers approved a decree creating a National Cybersecurity and Electronic Certification Agency. The agency is charged with setting up a national response team. In April 2025 the digital ministry issued specifications for a national CSIRT and a security operations centre for the government intranet. In January 2026 the digital minister discussed a response team and security operations centre with the US chargé d'affaires and the firm Cybastion. Nothing was signed then. On 9 October 2026 the project unit issued a tender for the national response team and a security operations centre for the government intranet. Bids are due on 17 November 2026. On 10 November 2026 the digital ministry signed a memorandum with a US cybersecurity firm on protecting state systems and networks, digitising services in remote areas and Cisco-certified training.

In August 2026 the Council of Ministers approved a narcotics bill whose investigation provisions allow interception of communications, electronic monitoring, and geolocation and technical tracking. Cybersecurity is one component of the digital financing package agreed in principle with the Islamic Development Bank in April 2026.

DPI

Data Exchange

Since April 2025 a Mauritanian company can be created in one online transaction that enters it in the trade register, issues its tax number and registers it for social security. The service runs through the Khidmaty portal and is authenticated by the Houwiyeti identity app.

A 2022 UNDP procurement notice records that the population register has offered web APIs since around 2010. They let the electoral commission, mobile operators and financial institutions verify identity in real time. Inter-administration electronic exchange rests on Décret n° 2022-009, with a Référentiel Général d'Interopérabilité proposed and not yet in place (June 2023). A 2024 scoping study for the digital ministry sets out a General Interoperability Framework and an Interoperability Hub. The hub comprises an API gateway, identity and access management and a unified data repository. The study names civil-status and identity documents among the first service families to join it.

The 31 December 2024 date Mauritania notified to the WTO for a trade single window passed, and in February 2025 it asked to move it to the end of 2027.

The 2025 design of the planned national address register looks to the Agence Numérique de l'État to host the address database and build an interoperability API. None of it was yet operational in 2025. Unified standards for digital public services were set at a national workshop with German cooperation in March 2025.

On 12 May 2026 the health ministry opened a national workshop to draft a national health interoperability framework, with European Union support. Under the DIGISANTE MRT programme it set HL7 FHIR R4, a national patient identifier and harmonised terminologies as the common standards (October 2026). A patient registry linked to the population identifier is among the registries it identified, and its national steering unit first met in July 2026.

Digital Identity and CRVS

In January 2026 the regulator issued formal notices to Mattel, Mauritel and Chinguitel over their failure to complete biometric identification of SIM cards. Biometric identification against the national register is a legal precondition for selling or activating a SIM card. The regulator had ordered unidentified SIMs deactivated in October 2023.

SIGPTS, the register launched in 2020 and run by ANRPTS, had enrolled the great majority of the population by May 2025. On 18 June 2026 the interior minister told parliament that more than 5.1 million people were on the national register. He gave no coverage rate. Enrolment met protests from 2011 under the banner « Touche pas à ma nationalité », over claims that it excluded citizens (2019).

Décret n° 2021-138 of 3 August 2021 governs the identity card, a secure chip document and the official proof of identity and nationality. It is voluntary from age six and compulsory from sixteen for anyone holding a National Identification Number. Fingerprints are captured for the national biometric register, and the card is not issued free of charge.

Houwiyeti reached the public in January 2024, letting citizens sign in to government services by facial match against their stored eID over OpenID Connect. ANRPTS had launched it in December 2023 for requests for identity and civil-status documents. It was built by the Swiss vendor TECH5 with the local firm SmartMS, and funded by UNDP. It was designed for low-connectivity areas, with offline verification.

No dedicated digital-identity legislation had been enacted by 2024, and legislation for the digital ID was left to a future phase. The card is one accepted proof among several for enrolling in public health insurance (2023), and since January 2025 the health insurance fund has verified insured persons biometrically against the register at all partner facilities. It underpins tiered know-your-customer checks at financial institutions without being mandatory to open an account (2022). Refugees, around 128,000 people by March 2024, are registered by the national registry agency and issued National Identification Numbers.

Digital Payments and Fintech

In July 2026 66 public bodies were enrolled on the tax authority's online system, 53 filed electronically and 3 paid through it. From 1 November 2026 public establishments must invoice, declare and pay through an electronic platform, and no invoice is payable unless registered on it. Electronic payment of import and export duties is a Trade Facilitation Agreement commitment the WTO records as showing no progress (2026).

GIMTEL, the interbank card and payments switch, was established in May 2005 under the aegis of the Banque Centrale de Mauritanie. In October 2024 the central bank opened interbank interoperability between mobile wallet applications through GIMTEL, after a project begun in 2021. A 2025 tender sought an instant payment system enabling interoperability of financial services.

In September 2024 the central bank issued its National Digital Payment Strategy 2023–2028, with payment infrastructure the second of five objectives. The strategy also aims to ease the regulation of payment and electronic money institutions to attract fintech investment. Law No. 2021-014 of 5 July 2021 on electronic payment services and means is the statutory base, and the central bank's 2022 instruction on payment institutions was issued under it.

Mauritania appears neither among PAPSS's live participants nor in its extended network (June 2026). In September 2026 the central bank reported a week of sandbox tests of a digital-currency proof of concept. No design, participant, pilot date or legal basis has been published.

27.31% of adults held an account at a financial institution or with a mobile-money provider in the 2024 Global Findex fieldwork. Access to banking services scores 13.4 of 100 in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 36th of 54 African states. In 2024 the Alliance for Financial Inclusion found GIMTEL not well positioned to serve people of modest means. It found the targets of the National Financial Inclusion Strategy 2023–2028 not embedded in payment-scheme governance.

Registries

Fewer than half of Mauritanian births were registered by 2021, against 66% of children under five registered and 40% holding a certificate in 2015. Civil registration scores 75.0 of 100 in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 7th of 54 African states, unchanged from 2014.

In 2025 civil registration was run by ANRPTS, covering births, marriages, divorces and deaths at commune-level Citizen Welcome Centres. The same agency issues identity documents against the SIGPTS register, leaving registration and identity only partly integrated. A newborn is enrolled free at a reception centre on the health facility's birth extract, within 60 days of birth. 59,078 infants under one were enrolled in 2025, against 68,813 in 2023.

The 2024 presidential register held 1,939,342 voters against a voting-age population of 2,510,244, a coverage rate of 77.2%. It was compiled as the biometric RAVEL 2023 register, deduplicated against the national ID database. A single Tax Identification Number covers all tax operations, for individuals and businesses (2025). A National Social Registry of the poorest households carries social-protection targeting (2024).

In 2025 World Bank land governance work found no national cadastral plan or land register, with formal registration concentrated in urban areas. The TEHLIL legal cadastre and the electronic land registry set up in Nouakchott in 2014 sit under a modernisation reform launched in September 2025. Since January 2025 document checks, plans, occupancy permits and property deeds have been requested online on the Lagoud platform. On 27 August 2026 the Council of Ministers approved a draft decree creating a national digital referential for cadastral data, with a permanent unique identifier for each parcel.

In March 2025 terms of reference were issued for support to MAURIPOST in setting up a georeferenced digital addressing system under WARDIP. On 29 September 2026 the digital ministry presented the system, with a pilot version expected by the end of the year.

Sectoral management information systems

Health posts keep a paper patient registry and submit their information on paper to health centres, where it is made digital (2025). A two-day workshop in Brakna in April 2025 trained inspectors and regional education staff on the national SIRAGE platform. It included field visits to every school to collect the data. Smartphone data capture by community health workers exists as a nine-facility pilot in two districts (2025).

Since 2023 the education ministry has been testing a nine-module human-resources system for teachers, covering recruitment, careers, pay and postings (February 2025). On 28 September 2026 pupil registration and transfer for 2026–2027 opened on the Siraj platform. In May 2026 the health solidarity fund CNASS launched digital services for policyholders on the Khidmati platform, with claims filed online. A National e-Health Strategy for 2024–2030, launched in October 2025, sets out a national vaccination database, an E-CNAM health insurance platform and wider telemedicine. On 24 October 2026 the health and digital ministers agreed a joint technical team to make the new Nouakchott health centres digitally ready. The DIGISANTE MRT programme chose OpenClinic GA as the shared primary-care record and confirmed 12 of 21 assessed health centres as pilot sites (October 2026). The first deployment is due at Dar Naim. The Tekavoul cash-transfer programme enrols beneficiaries through civil-registration-linked identification drawn from the Social Register.

The tax authority's 2025–2029 strategic plan sets out work to interface its information system with other public bodies. Budgetary and financial management fell 7.0 points to 59.7 of 100 over 2014–2023 in the 2024 Ibrahim Index. The RACHAD electronic expenditure chain has been deployed under the 2021–2025 public financial management strategy. A EUR 46.3 million World Bank programme appraised in May 2025 makes its interoperability with the tax and investment platforms a results area to 2030. Criminal record certificates went online in March 2025 through the Houwiyeti application. On 10 November 2026 the University of Nouakchott launched a blockchain platform, built with UNDP support, to issue and verify its diplomas.

Other GovTech and e-Gov

Mauritania's Ijraati portal, launched in February 2026, centralises more than 800 administrative procedures in a single directory. Its roadmap moves from centralising procedures to keeping them current, and then to fully digitising the most-used ones on the Khdamati platform. On 28 October 2026 the digital transformation official put the services fully online at about forty, against nearly 900 targeted.

From 4 August 2026 the water utility SNDE's transactions became available remotely through the « Mes Services » portal. Digital consular services for citizens abroad launched on 9 July 2026 with nine online services. Mourchid, launched in June 2026, brings employment, vocational-training and entrepreneurship programmes into one portal, with no documented API link to the Social Registry or the employment agency database. On 9 October 2026 the national solidarity agency launched Sanad, a digital platform for social assistance. It opened with transport costs for 11,656 pupils and students from social-registry households, verified by facial recognition.

A national procurement portal publishing plans, tender notices and award results opened on 3 December 2024. In July 2026 the SIGMAP procurement system was in use by 200 contracting authorities and 91 procurement commissions, with electronic bid submission still in testing. In March 2024 the digital ministry issued terms of reference for an encrypted platform to be the sole channel between ministers.

Public administration is Mauritania's second best-scoring governance dimension in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 57.9 of 100 and 20th of 54. The country sits in the bottom fifth of the UN's 2024 E-Government Development Index. In July 2026 339 of 642 legally constituted public bodies were active with the tax administration, and around twenty were not in the national taxpayer register at all.

Digitalisation

Digitalisation of sub-national government

On 13 August 2026 the minister delegate to the interior minister presented the Diwan territorial-administration system to a German delegation, as part of decentralisation cooperation. Its integration with the Khidmaty services platform is under way. In 2024 UNDP described digital-government activity as concentrated at central level, in a country where much of the population is rural. Workshops in Brakna and Hodh Charghi in April 2025 trained regional staff to operate the SIRAGE education platform and organise school-visit data collection. In April 2025 the El Mouhassil revenue collector, used since 2020 in about 30 communes, was to be generalised. Rural representation and participation is one of Mauritania's stronger and faster-improving governance measures (2023).

Rural digital data capture

Health posts keep a paper patient registry and submit their information on paper to health centres, where it is made digital (2025). Inspectors trained on the SIRAGE platform in Brakna visited every school to collect its data (2025). Police records of detainees were found poorly maintained (2023).

Smartphone capture by community health workers runs as a small NGO-supported pilot (2025). ANRPTS reception centres, including missions that travel to villages, capture biographic details, fingerprints, photograph and signature into the national biometric database (2023). Applicants still bring paper source documents. The e-ID pilot was designed for rural users, with offline verification and several credentials on one phone (2024). Electricity access is heavily skewed towards towns (2023).

Rural representation and participation rose 15.8 points over 2014–2023 to 74.2 of 100, 16th of 54 African states. Rural economy support rose 12.6 points to 66.6, and rural land and water access 7.7 points to 63.8. The rural economy sub-category fell 4.9 points to 55.9 of 100, 32nd of 54, and market access is the sole cause of that decline.

Technology

AI

Mauritania's national artificial-intelligence strategy was drafted by the digital ministry in April 2024 for 2024–2029, around five priorities, twelve objectives and thirty measures. In October 2026 the Africa AI Governance Index placed Mauritania 33rd of 54 states, scoring 0.99 of four. Its weakest pillar was implementation and impact, at 0.22.

In December 2025 the state digital agency's AI cameras detecting overloading and seat-belt offences went into service across the national road network. A police recruitment competition in March 2026 used AI to check documents, set the questions and mark the papers. In November 2024 the tax directorate built an open-source activity dashboard with EU-funded support and tested language-model report drafting. The digital ministry presented two strategy pilots, multilingual translation and national virtual teachers, and no launch has been found since. In June 2026 the youth and employment ministry launched Mourchid, an AI assistant answering in Arabic and French from ministry data. The operator that launched 5G in August 2026 presented it as groundwork for AI, cloud and connected-device services.

ICT Industry

Mauritania scored 34.4 of 100 for business and competition rules in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 48th of 54 states (2023). The score was flat across the decade, and the wider business and labour environment lost 2.2 points to 40.3.

In 2026 African Business described the domestic technology industry as small and thinly financed. The Startup Act bill presented in September 2023 offered customs and VAT relief on imported equipment and a subsidy towards protecting intellectual property.

The shipping and postal network sits among Mauritania's ten worst-scoring measures in the 2024 Ibrahim Index. It and the transport network both lost ground over the decade to 2023.

The operator behind the 2026 5G network is a subsidiary of Sudan's Sudatel and has set out an investment plan for 2027–2030. Nouakchott's urban security system was funded by a Chinese government grant in 2021 and built by a Chinese contractor.

Innovation ecosystem

In 2026 African Business found no venture capital fund headquartered in Mauritania, minimal angel activity, and start-up financing coming largely as donor and government grants. The country placed 126th on WIPO's Global Innovation Index in 2024, in the bottom quartile.

The Startup Act was promulgated in January 2024. As presented in September 2023, the bill built a merit-based start-up label carrying benefits for five years, awarded by a labelling committee. Labelled firms were to get a three-year exemption from the patente business tax and partial corporate-tax relief. They were also to get relief on social contributions, a preferential margin in public procurement and a right of access to open data. The finance side added investor tax relief, foreign-currency accounts, a state-backed start-up fund and a guarantee fund. The ministry's targets were a few hundred labelled firms and several thousand jobs over five years.

In 2023 the incubator Hadina RIMTIC, founded in 2014, had incubated five start-ups and pre-incubated 26 more. It runs recurring programmes including the MauriAppChallenge, and InnovRim/SahelFabLab operates as a second fab lab and incubator.

Capacity

Literacy

Education scored 34.5 of 100 in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, jointly Mauritania's second worst-ranked governance dimension and 48th of 54 African states. The score rose 5.9 points over the decade to 2023.

Equality in education rose 11.2 points to 41.5 over 2014–2023, and completion 9.8 points to 36.4. Enrolment rose a tenth of a point to 31.5, 38th of 54, and quality 2.1 points to 28.3, 40th of 54 (2023).

In 2025 pupil and attendance records in rural primary schools were kept on paper. They reach the national platform only when regional inspectors collect them on field visits and enter them at district or regional level.

Training and skills

Mauritania's IPv6 transition strategy of 2024 weights its costing towards human resources. It states that most installed equipment already supports the protocol.

In 2024 tertiary ICT and STEM provision was concentrated in a few institutions: the public university's science faculty, a polytechnic school, a digital institute and some private providers. Hadina RIMTIC, a Nouakchott incubator founded in 2014, runs recurring programmes including the MauriAppChallenge (2023).

Training in April 2025 equipped regional education staff to operate the SIRAGE platform and run the school-visit missions that feed it. On 25 August 2026 the University of Nouakchott opened a first summer school on language models for low-resource African languages, at Nouadhibou. On 8 September 2026 the education ministry opened a ten-day course in electronic document management for 20 inspectors. The Mourchid portal, launched in June 2026, draws employment, vocational-training and entrepreneurship programmes into one place.

Research institutions

In March 2024 the think tank Mauritanie Perspectives ran a workshop on digitalisation and citizenship under a Global Development Network project. In December 2025 it was one of two Mauritanian teams in a six-country review of digital and governance research priorities. In July 2025 the legal research centre CMERJES and the Max Planck Foundation held a workshop for judges and scholars on AI and procedural justice. In January 2026 the Mabda research centre held a roundtable on AI in education and employability. No public research institution working on digital, data or statistical questions had been found as at November 2026. Nor had a national research programme on them.

Inclusion

Access to services

Inclusion and equality scored 25.2 of 100 in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 48th of 54 African states, after a 7.0-point gain over the decade. Equal access to public services scored 28.8, 40th, and up 1.3 points. Equal socioeconomic opportunity scored 18.9, 49th and among the country's ten worst-scoring measures.

In July 2026 the Council of Ministers moved to replace the 2020 decree governing the Fonds d'accès universel aux services. It said six years of application had exposed shortcomings in how the fund's object is defined and how its resources are mobilised and allocated. It also cited the lack of a clear framework for folding the fund into the state's budget and accounts. The communiqué did not set out what the replacement provides.

From 4 August 2026 water utility transactions moved onto the « Mes Services » portal, and a consular platform opened for Mauritanians in Saudi Arabia, Bahrain and the Maldives. Health insurance enrolment accepts several proofs of identity, with equivalents for migrants and refugees. In 2024 refugees reached financial services only partly, with digitisation and mobile money for them at an early stage. The national payments switch was judged ill-suited to people of modest means.

Digital divides

About 55% of Mauritania's population did not use the internet in 2024. A 5 GB mobile data plan cost 2.94% of GNI per capita in 2025, above the 2% international affordability benchmark. An entry-level 1 GB basket sits within that threshold, and a fixed connection costs many times more.

Mobile communications is among the country's ten most improved measures in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, while access to internet and computers moved far less over the same years. Equality in education and completion both improved over the decade, while enrolment stayed flat and quality barely moved.

Electricity access is overwhelmingly urban (2023). In 2024 UNDP described rural Mauritania as having comparatively limited connectivity and public-service infrastructure.

Data

National statistics

Statistical capacity scored 55.7 of 100 in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 20th of 54 African states, after an 11.6-point rise over the decade to 2023.

In 2023 Mauritania scored above 89 per cent on data use in the World Bank's Statistical Performance Indicators, but only 20 to 49 per cent on data sources. Data services and infrastructure sat in the 50 to 69 band, and data products at 70 to 89.

The fifth population census, taken in 2023, counted 4,927,532 people in 834,352 ordinary households in its final results of June 2025. There has been no agricultural census and no economic or business census in the last twenty years. The decade to 2023 saw three or more health surveys, two household surveys, two labour surveys, one agricultural survey and no business survey.

The 2024 Ibrahim Index has no data for Mauritania on 15 of its 96 indicators, among them every public perception measure.

The 27th foreign trade yearbook, for 2025, is compiled from customs SYDONIA declarations. The 2023 health statistics yearbook was the first to draw primary-level data from DHIS2. The first vocational-training yearbook, for 2023–2025, was extracted from the TEKWINE management platform. In July 2024 a peer review by the Cameroonian and Senegalese statistics offices found the National Statistics Council not functioning, and made 35 recommendations.

Open data

In June 2023 Mauritania published 24 open datasets from four providers, against Tunisia's 2,346 datasets from 161 providers. The government's open-data strategy places Mauritania last on every measure it publishes, against Morocco, Senegal and Tunisia.

Publication runs through mauritania.opendataforafrica.org, operated by the statistics agency ANSADE (June 2023). The strategy rests on three axes: an interoperability framework, open-data governance on Open Government Partnership principles, and open data for innovation. Mauritania is not an OGP member, though all three of its chosen comparators are. The WARDIP procurement plan of December 2025 scheduled consultant selection for a national portal at data.gov.mr for March and April 2026. On 18 January 2026 the digital ministry launched a portal publishing all its contracts and spending since August 2024, free and without registration.

In the 2024 Open Data Inventory, balance of payments scores 7.5 out of ten and money and banking 5.5. Government finance, labour and prices sit at 4, and national accounts and international trade at 3. Population and vital statistics lead the social statistics at 5.5, and crime and justice is lowest at 2. Environmental statistics run between 3 and 4.5.

Accessibility of public records scores 24.7 of 100 in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 34th of 54, after an 11.9-point gain over the decade. Disclosure of public records is among Mauritania's ten worst measures, at 10.1 of 100 and 50th of 54 (2023).

Geospatial data

By July 2026 Mauritania's telecoms regulator ARE had selected three companies to provide satellite telecommunications services, after a tender launched in January.

In June 2025 the national employment agency's TechghilAgro project gave more than 100 rice farmers at Rosso and R'kiz a mobile app built on Sentinel-2 data. The app follows crop growth and irrigation needs. A predictive drought model built on satellite rainfall, vegetation and biomass indices targets Elmaouna lean-season cash transfers through the social register (2023).

Geopolitics

US / hyperscaler activities

In January 2026 the digital minister held talks with the United States chargé d'affaires and the firm Cybastion on a national cyber response capability, and no agreement was signed. Amazon Web Services operates no region in Mauritania. No United States government programme or global cloud provider had an established presence in Mauritania's digital sector as at September 2026.

China activities

In 2021 China granted Mauritania MRU 629.4 million, about US$15 million at the time. The grant funds an Urban Security and Monitoring System for the national security directorate in Nouakchott. The grant was drawn from an Economic and Technical Cooperation Agreement, with no digital share split out. The design runs to 316 cameras extensible to 1,000 observation points, and a two-storey command centre. It adds three regional security buildings and 34 police observation posts.

Chinese public money accounts for one of the 35 external financing commitments on the record for Mauritania's digital estate (November 2026), against three from the World Bank and five from the European Investment Bank. On count of commitments, China is a minor financier of the sector and a singular one in what it has chosen to fund.

EU activities

The national data centre in Nouakchott was inaugurated in May 2025 on a EUR 15 million European Investment Bank loan. It is certified Tier III and operated by International Mauritanian Telecommunications. The EIB signed a EUR 25 million loan in December 2020 for the country's first direct high-speed fibre link to Europe and West Africa. In July 2025 the EllaLink connection project was launched at Nouadhibou. Mauritania is a landing country on the MEDUSA system connecting Portugal to North and West Africa, backed by an EIB loan and a European Union grant.

Germany's BMZ is funding a EUR 4 million digital transformation programme, DIGITAL-Y, from 2024 to 2027, aimed at the state and the digital sector. German cooperation supported the March 2025 national workshop on unified standards for digital public services. A EUR 2.5 million French development agency grant, channelled through UN Women, is modernising the data systems of the statistics institutes of Mauritania, Senegal and Chad (2024).

The European Investment Bank is the largest financier of Mauritania's digital estate by value, five of the 35 commitments on record (November 2026): three submarine-cable connections, a terrestrial cable and the national data centre. Add Germany's development ministry and the French, Spanish and Maltese grants, and European financiers account for 15 of the 35 and about US$121 million (2015-2026). That is the largest bloc by count, and the one that has shaped what the country's international bandwidth and hosting look like.

Gulf/UAE activities

On 30 July 2026 Omani and Mauritanian institutions and companies signed five memoranda on digital cooperation, during an Omani delegation's visit. The Islamic Development Bank signed an agreement in principle with Mauritania in April 2026. It covers digital infrastructure, cybersecurity, interoperability and skills. On 31 May 2023 the digital ministry signed a memorandum with the Abu Dhabi group G42 and its subsidiary Presight. It covers a national data centre and a sovereign cloud.

India activities

India had no established role in Mauritania's digital sector as at September 2026.

Digital sovereignty

On 2 July 2026 the digital transformation minister told the National Assembly that all public-administration servers had moved into the national data centre. He said the national cloud launched days earlier ends the state's reliance on foreign hosting platforms. The cloud was inaugurated at the Nouakchott data centre on 29 June 2026 with World Bank support through WARDIP.

The data protection authority's deliberation 005/2025 requires prior authorisation for any use of a foreign cloud. The contract must provide for returning and deleting the data.

On 13 September 2026 an MP asked for the data centre contract and the RIAD network contract to be published and audited. He put the data centre contract at over five billion old ouguiya. The state company SDIN is to own the national backbone and delegate its operation to the International Mauritania Telecom consortium. The consortium also runs the national data centre. The terms on data jurisdiction, portability and exit under that arrangement had not been published as at September 2026.

For the Houwiyeti digital-ID pilot, the local firm SmartMS built the back end around TECH5's face-matching technology (2024). TECH5 contributed the source code of the customised solution to the open-source MOSIP platform. No statement had been found as at September 2026 on whether the state holds the source code of the SIGPTS population register.
