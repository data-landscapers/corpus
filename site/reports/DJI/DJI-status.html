This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

Djibouti's 2026 budget law orders three pieces of digital work: the customs single window, an update of the Fichier Unique and a budget data-exchange platform. Its tables stop at title-level totals and name no ICT ministry line, no ANSIE allocation and no e-government envelope.

The Council of Ministers approved the decree adopting the third National Development Plan, PND-ADEEG 2025-2030, on 30 June 2026. The World Bank's eighth implementation report records a decision to begin updating the flagship Smart Nation Strategy. The update is to align it with the new plan and the new cybersecurity agency, and to speed up the Digital Code's telecommunications and cybersecurity books. On 1 September 2026 the President named the digitalisation of procedures, paperless public services and interoperability between administrations as the means of modernising the state.

The main lines of the artificial-intelligence strategy for 2026 to 2030 were presented on 3 September 2026 at the country's first national forum on artificial intelligence. The strategy is led by the ministry responsible for education, innovation and digital development, with UNESCO and UN-ESCWA support. No adopted text has been found.

A feedback meeting on 20 April 2026 considered the final report defining the Universal Service Fund's regulatory and operational framework. It produced a draft decree, an internal bylaw and an operations guide. On 21 September 2026 the regulator ARMD consulted the four operators on a three-year strategic plan for communications and ICT. The plan in drafting has eight axes, among them the Digital Code and universal service.

A national carbon-market framework was tabled for validation in August 2026. An Agence souveraine carbone had already been given a director-general. Djibouti launched the PAGEF public governance reform programme on 11 June 2026 with USD 20 million. It scores 43.1 out of 100 for Overall Governance in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 38th of 54 African states and below the African average of 49.3. Its score improved 3.9 points over 2014-2023, at a pace that has slowed since 2019.

Most of the digital strategy instruments sitting beneath Vision 2035 are at consultation, validation or revision stage rather than in force.

Legislation and regulation

Djibouti adopted its Code du numérique on 30 June 2025 and published it in the Journal Officiel of 18 September 2025. Its eight books and nearly 800 articles cover personal data protection, e-commerce, consumer rights, cybersecurity and cybercrime.

The Startup Act, Loi n° 179/AN/25/9ème L, was adopted on 12 November 2025 and published on 15 December 2025. It creates a Startup Visa: a three-year renewable residence card for founders and staff, with an automatic work permit. It also provides for a regulatory sandbox under the Haut Conseil Supérieur de l'Économie Numérique et de l'Innovation. A 2022 law created that council when it founded the delegate ministry for the digital economy.

Loi n° 26/AN/18/8ème L of 27 February 2019 created the national statistics institute to coordinate the national statistical system and produce official statistics. A law of 18 February 2021 renamed it INSTAD and attached it to the ministry in charge of statistics. The 2019 law cites the older law of 20 November 2011 on the organisation of statistical activity. A 2023 decree named the eleven members of the institute's board.

In January 2026 Djibouti Telecom held the national and international telecommunications monopoly, under laws of 1998 and 2004. Décret n° 2026-105/PRE of 11 June 2026 tasks the Justice ministry, with MDENI, with digitalising legal services.

The 2026 finance law requires trade transaction records to be kept for ten years. It sets a cash-declaration threshold of 1,000,000 Djiboutian francs. It also creates a two-stage administrative appeal against customs decisions and binding twelve-month advance rulings. The National Gendarmerie is required by law to keep its procès-verbaux and custody registers by hand.

On 27 October 2026 the Council of Ministers approved a draft organic law on finance laws in eleven titles. One of its titles provides for an integrated public financial management system, electronic archiving, digital signature and online publication of budget data.

Data protection

Djibouti's Digital Code, enacted in June 2025, creates a National Commission for the Protection of Personal Data. The commission is an independent supervisory authority with administrative and financial autonomy. Book I of the Code, on personal data protection, is carried in the annex published as Journal Officiel special issue n° 04.

The 2025 regime runs to 156 articles and requires breach notification to the commission within 72 hours. It gives every citizen rights of access, rectification, erasure and portability. Data protection officers are mandatory for public authorities and for large-scale monitoring or sensitive-data processing. The Code provides for a public register of processing operations. Higher-risk processing, such as biometric and genetic identity data, must be authorised before it begins. Any processing involving the national identification number needs the commission's prior authorisation. The country's mobile identity programme therefore falls in the highest-scrutiny tier.

Officers of the Police Nationale and the road-safety police wear body-worn cameras on the uniform as an everyday tool (August 2026). Protocols require the camera to be worn visibly and the subject to be told they are being recorded. Recording starts manually or automatically, according to the type of intervention or the threat level detected.

Co-design workshops in October 2025 for a digital agricultural advisory service wrote their own data-governance framework. It covers traceability, user feedback and data protection.

A July 2025 Horn of Africa Initiative ministerial meeting called on IGAD and the East African Community to pilot a trusted cross-border personal-data-flow mechanism within twelve months.

Regional collaboration

The Project Implementation Unit for Phase I of IGAD's Horn of Africa Digital Market Integration project is based in Djibouti (May 2026). In May 2026 it opened a call for in-country training and mentorship of women- and youth-led MSMEs in gender-sensitive digital trade. Delivery runs from June to December 2026.

Djibouti's senior policymakers and regulators mapped national data-governance contexts with UNESCO's Data Governance Toolkit at a regional workshop in Nairobi on 9-11 June 2026. They examined alignment with the African Union Data Policy Framework and the Smart Africa Data Governance Blueprint. The workshop, with Burundi, Ethiopia, Kenya, Somalia, South Sudan, Tanzania and Uganda, drafted regional data-policy harmonisation roadmaps. The health ministry's secretary-general represented Djibouti at IGAD's second ministerial meeting on pandemic preparedness in Kampala on 29 August 2025. The meeting inaugurated the regional PREPARE project. Djibouti is a member state of the Digital Cooperation Organization. In September 2026 its secretary-general discussed cooperation on artificial intelligence, digital skills and capacity building with the digital economy minister.

Djibouti hosted two regional digital-policy convenings on its own territory within three months, an IGAD cyber-resilience workshop at Arta in October 2025 and the African Union's 16th Private Sector Forum in December 2025.

The government's secretariat-general is to host the second African Forum of Official Journals in Djibouti on 16-17 December 2026. The forum is to sign a cooperation charter, adopt a Djibouti Declaration and set up a permanent secretariat. Its agenda includes common publication standards and interoperability between national gazette platforms.

In February 2026 IGAD sought a consultancy to draft regional payments rules covering the country. The cross-border fibre corridors the government relies on are agreements, with no infrastructure yet in service. In July 2026 the communications minister took the digital agenda to the WSIS Forum in Geneva. He discussed ITU support for digitising public services and, with Smart Africa, alignment of national projects with continental initiatives. Djibouti scores 56.3 out of 100 on Regional Integration in the Ibrahim Index in 2023, 16th of 54 African states. Its score rose 10.0 points over 2014-2023.

Standards

The Agence Djiboutienne des Normes et de la Qualité brought two Comités Techniques de Normalisation into operation during 2025. They cover agri-food and electrical, electrotechnical and household appliance standards. The agency's 2026 provisional budget was approved at 134.9 million Djiboutian francs on 13 January 2026, up from 112.1 million in 2025. Of the 2026 budget, 54.4 million is investment in software, equipment and tooling, IT equipment, laboratory fit-out and vehicles. The agency was founded by Loi n° 100/AN/20/8ème L of 7 January 2021. A decree of May 2022 organised it as a public industrial and commercial establishment under the Ministry of Commerce and Tourism. Its remit covers policy on standardisation, metrology, certification, accreditation and quality promotion. It joined ISO and the International Organization of Legal Metrology in 2024 and sits in ISO as a correspondent member.

No national spectrum management plan had been adopted as at November 2025. The regulator ARMD and the ITU held a workshop on 9-10 November 2025 on spectrum strategy, pricing, licensing and sharing. Its recommendations are to found a National Spectrum Management Plan.

ANSIE's Cadre d'Interopérabilité of 2022 defines interoperability principles and four layers: legal, organisational, semantic and technical. It also sets a conceptual model for the country's e-government architecture. The framework supports the six axes of the 2020–2022 e-government strategy and the e-governance aims of the "Bonne Gouvernance" pillar of Vision Djibouti 2035.

From 31 August 2025 the central bank required by communiqué that cheques conform to SYRAD platform standards. A 2020 decree fixes the composition of the national identification number. The Code du numérique of 2025 is the framework instrument above all of it. It covers personal data protection, electronic communication, cybersecurity and cybercrime, and innovative digital services.

Public debate and participation in policymaking

Djibouti's Digital Freedom score in the Ibrahim Index fell 17.0 points over 2014-2023 to 24.6 out of 100, 46th of 54 African states. It is the third most deteriorated of the country's 96 indicators, against rising infrastructure scores. Rights, one of the country's three weakest sub-categories, scores 24.7 out of 100 in 2023, 49th of 54. Media Freedom stands at 22.0, and Freedom of Expression and Belief at 44.8 after a 12.5-point rise. Freedom of Association and Assembly scores 0.0, unchanged across the whole decade. Freedom House scores the country 24 out of 100 and rates government openness and transparency at the lowest available level (2025). The media regime is still governed by Loi n° 2/AN/92/2ème L of 15 September 1992, never substantially reformed (April 2026).

A workshop on 22 January 2026 on the national AI strategy drew roughly 80 participants from government, UN agencies, academia, the private sector and civil society. It used UNESCO's five-dimension readiness methodology as its diagnostic instrument. The week of bilateral meetings before it found varying levels of awareness of, resistance to and capacity to use AI, alongside strong national commitment. The national development plan was formulated through three weeks of thematic debates in Djibouti-Ville and consultations across the five regions in August 2025. Each region had to produce a validated contribution report drawing in elected officials, local authorities, civil society and experts.

In November 2025 MDENI presented the Digital Code to the France-Djibouti Business Group's General Assembly, after it had become law. The minister's legal adviser set out its personal data protection and e-commerce provisions. The PAGEF programme, launched in June 2026, introduces the country's first Budget Citoyen alongside strengthened control and accountability mechanisms.

Public participation in digital policymaking rests on a handful of dated events between April 2022 and February 2026, only some of which put a text to non-state participants before it was settled.

Finance

Financial sustainability

Djibouti cut the 2026 budget of ANSIE, the agency that runs the state's information systems, by 160.6 million Djiboutian francs to 396.1 million. The Council of Ministers cited the creation of the separate cybersecurity authority among its reasons. The agency lives chiefly on a state subsidy plus receipts for its services. Its 108.1 million francs of 2026 investment goes on firewalls, archiving, backup colocation and support renewals for the data centre and X-Road. Staff costs take 267.9 million in 2026. Its receipts rest on a tariff decree adopted in Council on 14 November 2023. The decree charges public and parapublic bodies across six service categories, from hosting in the national data centre to network and information-system security.

ANSIE was appropriated 451.7 million francs for 2024 and 556.8 million for 2025. Its 2024 accounts, approved by law in February 2026, show charges of 479.2 million francs against income of 457.1 million. The statistics institute INSTAD was appropriated 485.7 million francs for 2026, all of it subsidy, up from 456.9 million in 2025.

The two digital bodies' appropriations read together rose from 897.6 million francs for 2024 to 1,013.7 million for 2025, up 12.9% at the appropriated stage.

The 2025 Digital Code obliges telecommunications operators to pay the regulator a share of turnover for digital development. A further turnover contribution feeds a Universal Service Fund. Both percentages are left to regulation (articles 210, 302 and 308). The fund's draft decree came out of a World Bank-financed mission reviewed on 20 April 2026. The regulator's own 2026 budget of 258.1 million francs draws on spectrum and licence fees and a state subsidy of 139.06 million.

The state has put its own funds into digitising public archives since 2023 (August 2026). The President financed putting 126 years of the Journal officiel online. In February 2026 the World Bank's Digital Foundations Project was finalising the purchase of internet capacity to connect the remaining public institutions.

MoUs and other agreements

Djibouti signed a memorandum of understanding with China's LinkWise Group on 19 July 2026. The July 2026 memorandum is stated at US$1.35 billion for a 100 MW artificial-intelligence compute centre, up to 500 MW of solar, wind and storage generation, a digital industrial park and international data corridors. The memorandum gives no split between the digital and the energy parts. It is valid for one year, with phase one due for completion within three years. It is to be financed entirely by the investors, with no state equity and no sovereign debt. In July 2026 the signing minister set out terms for a dedicated artificial-intelligence special economic zone financed by two Shanghai companies. They include a policy-stability clause and a tax-exemption period.

In all, 21 commitments are on the record, starting between 2015 and 2026, and that single unclosed memorandum outweighs all the finance actually committed to Djibouti's digital sector put together more than three times over (as at November 2026).

The Horizon Fiber arrangement between Djibouti Telecom, Ethio telecom and Sudatel became a binding agreement in February 2026. It began as a memorandum of understanding and covers a cross-border build with Ethiopia and Sudan. Italy's Fondazione Med-Or has signed a memorandum with the Ministry of Economy and Finance covering critical infrastructure, digital connectivity and strategic dialogue. Under it the foundation is building Djiboutian capacity to inspect and protect submarine cables at the landing station.

In September 2025 Djibouti signed a Smart Wallet memorandum with Visa to develop a national digital wallet. In October 2025 the European Investment Bank and Sparkle formalised their collaboration on the Blue Raman cable. Djibouti and Romania began exploring digital cooperation in September 2025.

Across the record as a whole most of the committed value arrives as debt: nine of the 21 commitments are loans, seven concessional and two commercial, worth about US$157 million together, against 11 grants worth about US$30 million and a single memorandum of understanding (as at November 2026).

New investments

Five of the 21 digital commitments on Djibouti's record were active as at November 2026, worth about US$64 million; four had closed, one stood at pipeline stage and 11 carry no recorded status.

Of the live World Bank operations, the Integrated Community Resilience Project is an SDR 23.8 million grant (about US$31 million) approved in March 2025. The project is a safety-nets operation, with an ANSIE interoperability upgrade for the Social Registry as one element. The Economic and Financial Governance Project lends US$20 million over 2025 to 2030 for tax administration and financial management systems. The Digital Foundations Project carries US$10 million to open ICT to competition (February 2026).

On 21 November 2026 Djibouti and the Agence Française de Développement signed a EUR 5 million financing convention for AREFIP, a four-year public finance reform project. Its first axis supports digitalisation and the spending chain.

The World Bank holds five of the 21 commitments and about US$60 million, roughly a third of all committed finance once the unclosed memorandum is set aside; the Islamic Development Bank follows with two submarine-cable loans worth about US$56 million (as at November 2026).

In 2015 a concessional loan from the Agence Française de Développement financed Djibouti Telecom's stakes in the consortia building the AAE-1 and SMW5 cables. The Islamic Development Bank lent US$17 million for the AAE-1 connection over 2015 to 2017, on commercial terms. It lent US$39 million for DARE-1, closed in 2021, also on commercial terms. The closed Public Administration Modernization Project, SDR 10.4 million approved in 2018, funded an interoperable government platform, a national CERT and a public key infrastructure. The Agence Française de Développement has lent EUR 10 million (about US$11 million) for 2020 to 2027 to digitalise and professionalise higher education.

Connectivity is where the money has gone, four commitments worth about US$101 million, with about US$28 million to government systems, about US$24 million to statistics and US$20 million to cybersecurity; the much larger data-storage total is the unclosed memorandum (as at November 2026).

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

Djibouti's regulator granted NetCatOnline SAS an authorisation on 22 March 2026 to build and operate a national wired electronic-communications network. It may sell internet access and private data transmission, and reach Djibouti Telecom's cable-landing stations. Djibouti Telecom has held the national and international telecommunications monopoly (January 2026).

The Prime Minister put the count of submarine-cable landings at fourteen on 1 March 2026. He described Google's Blue Raman as coming soon, outside the total. That system runs 11,700 km and lands in Djibouti, India and Oman. In October 2025 the European Commission presented it as the physical layer of an EU–Africa–India Digital Corridor.

Djibouti Telecom's terrestrial fibre to Ethiopia, built from August 2007, has a backup route through Galafi. Two cross-border fibre builds are in train. Djibouti Telecom, Ethio telecom and Sudatel signed a binding tripartite agreement on 4 February 2026 for the Horizon Fiber corridor. It links Djibouti's and Sudan's landing stations through Ethiopia. It is specified at roughly 2,800 km with 144 fibre pairs and about 14 ms latency. On 7 September 2026 in Addis Ababa the three operators presented the corridor to Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, other carriers and equipment makers, seeking partners before deployment. Djibouti Telecom plans to extend DARE1 along Africa's east coast to South Africa. The extension is a new 3,200–3,500 km link from Mombasa to Mtunzini, with a 2028 ready-for-service target.

Both of the cross-border fibre projects Djibouti has joined are running in parallel, and neither has been confirmed complete.

DataReportal estimated 786,000 internet users, 65.3% of the population, for the end of 2026. At a ministerial review in October 2026, 26 of the Digital Foundation project's 35 activities were complete. By October 2026, 56% of its US$10m had been disbursed. The regional project's fibre loops to Dikhil, Tadjourah and Obock and twelve 4G sites await commercial, environmental and social studies. The country ranks ninth of 54 African states on the Ibrahim Index's internet and computers measure, at 61.2 out of 100 in 2023. It ranks forty-sixth on mobile communications, at 47.3. No national spectrum management plan had been adopted as at November 2025.

Data Storage

In July 2026 the government agreed terms for an artificial-intelligence compute zone with a Chinese investor. It offered land, guaranteed seawater abstraction rights for cooling, a policy-stability clause and a tax-exemption period. Technical training takes the place of local employment quotas. The terms sit in a memorandum of understanding, and no signed investment protocol has been found.

Wingu Africa's TO7, a Tier-3 carrier-neutral data centre with a submarine cable landing station and an innovation centre, was inaugurated on 25 November 2024. It was the first integrated facility of its kind in the country. Its Technology Park facility carries 3MW and certified Tier III status, alongside the Djibouti Data Center established in 2013. The operator provides infrastructure-as-a-service, cloud-as-a-service and satellite teleport capabilities. In June 2025 it raised US$60m from Rand Merchant Bank to expand across Djibouti, Ethiopia and Tanzania.

PAIX Data Centres and the Fonds Souverain de Djibouti announced a joint venture in May 2024 to build a further carrier-neutral facility, JIB1. PAIX still put it at design stage in April 2025. That month the sovereign fund was dissolved by decree and a liquidator named. No construction has been found. A World Bank Group diagnostic released on 13 September 2026 found data-centre capacity nearing full utilisation. The September 2026 diagnostic put the private investment that reform could bring into the sector at US$160 million to US$240 million.

A World Bank GovTech case study recorded a fully operational government data centre (2024). A World Bank project that closed on 30 June 2021 was restructured to establish an automated National Payment System. The customs directorate installed a backup site and a new high-availability data centre in 2024. On 2 August 2026 the Secretariat-General of the Government and the UNDP signed a letter of accord to digitise the state's archives. The data are to be backed up at the ANSIE data centre.

Energy

Djibouti's government validated a national energy pact in May 2026. It sets a target of 60% rural electricity access by 2030 and universal access by 2035. In May 2026 rural access stood at 17.8%, and close to 30% of the population was unserved. The May 2026 pact puts the investment needed at US$1.745bn, of which over US$728m is sought from the private sector.

Around 65.2% of the population had access to electricity in 2023. Residential tariffs are among the highest in Africa (2023). The contract for the 230kV double-circuit line from Semera substation to Galafi was awarded on 24 November 2025. The line is the Ethiopian side of the second Ethiopia–Djibouti power interconnection.

A 165kW off-grid solar plant with 500kWh of battery storage was inaugurated at Adailou in Tadjourah in 2025. It brings electricity to homes, schools, health centres and businesses. Japan funded and the UNDP launched a US$373,333 twelve-month solar pilot on 21 April 2026 at Yoboki in Dikhil and Omar Jaggaa in Arta. It pairs electricity with water access and community maintenance, and names no digital element. The country scores 37.1 out of 100 on the Ibrahim Index's access to energy measure in 2023, 29th of 54 African states.

Rural access is 42.2 percentage points short of the pact's own 2030 target. The 500 MW of generation pledged to the single artificial-intelligence compute project announced in July 2026 is about three thousand times the capacity of the Adailou plant, and the US$1.35bn behind that project is roughly 77% of the entire investment the pact says universal access requires.

Technical Capacity

A cross-ministry digital-maturity diagnostic presented to the Council of Ministers on 7 October 2025 found the administration fragmented and unevenly equipped. Bilateral meetings before the January 2026 national AI strategy workshop found varying levels of awareness of, resistance to and capacity to use AI.

The cybersecurity authority created by law in December 2025 was assembled around ANSIE's existing information systems security directorate. A decree published on 12 May 2026 created a dedicated civil-service corps for the statistics institute's staff. It covers statistical engineers, senior administrators and senior technicians. Technical assistance is strengthening INSTAD's own capacity to collect sensitive population data. A digital-skills training programme for civil servants runs with the Digital Cooperation Organization, Oracle University and the national public administration institute.

Djibouti's STEM centres, the first two opened in September 2025, run on a split model. STEMpower supplies the equipment and the initial training. The education ministry and host schools provide premises and ongoing upkeep. IGAD handed over digital equipment to three cross-border health facilities, district health offices and the health ministry's health information system units in December 2024.

Cybersecurity

Loi n° 195/AN/25/9ème L, published in the Journal Officiel on 15 December 2025, creates the Autorité Nationale de Cybersécurité. It is an autonomous public establishment under the Presidency, built around ANSIE's existing information systems security directorate. The Council of Ministers had approved the bill on 4 November 2025 and appointed the authority's director-general on 18 November 2025. The authority stood up in December 2025.

A National Cybersecurity Strategy for 2024–2030, built on five pillars, was released on 28 November 2024. The Council of Ministers adopted the decree on it in the week of 29 May 2025. The Code du numérique was published in the Journal Officiel on 18 September 2025. A World Bank supervision report records a decision to speed up its telecommunications and cybersecurity books (February 2026).

Djibouti sits in the evolving tier of the ITU's Global Cybersecurity Index (2024). The state daily reports that the authority's DJ-CERT team took first place in both simulation scenarios at an IGAD regional exercise in Addis Ababa on 26-30 June 2026. DJ-CERT's alert page listed 511 vulnerability alerts with severity ratings between January 2023 and 21 October 2026. The central bank created a cybersecurity department to help banks secure transactions and protect its own operations (November 2024). The government's October 2025 digital-maturity diagnostic still lists putting a cybersecurity policy in place among the challenges ahead. The closed Public Administration Modernization Project included creating a national computer emergency response team and a public key infrastructure.

At the ITU in November 2026 the communications minister said Djibouti had a national emergency telecommunications plan to keep communications running in a crisis. Satellite technology is to complement it in isolated areas. No text or adoption date for the plan has been found.

Italy's foreign ministry and Fondazione Med-Or run a project at Djibouti's submarine-cable landing station to strengthen the authorities' capability to inspect and protect it (August 2026). Djibouti hosted IGAD's cyber-resilience workshop at Arta on 6–8 October 2025. It consulted on a regional information sharing and analysis centre still at the stakeholder stage.

DPI

Data Exchange

Integration of government services into the national interoperability platform met with delays (2024). Fewer than ten platforms were using it, partly for want of an enabling legal framework.

The platform is an X-Road installation known as Caravan, completed at the end of 2021 with Estonia's e-Governance Academy. It is managed by ANSIE, the state information-systems agency. No binding law required ministries to connect to it as at 2024. The 2019 identification law had already made the national identification number obligatory for exchanging data between public and parapublic services. A 2022 statute charges the digital economy ministry MDENI with designing, steering and managing e-government.

In February 2026 the World Bank's Digital Foundations Project was connecting 33 ministries, departments and agencies in its pilot phase, 11 in Djibouti city and 22 in the regions. The first of them were still being connected. The generalisation phase beyond it adds roughly 100 more. In July 2026 the population and family directorate described its own interoperability layer as still in preparation. The layer is to give other administrations, banks and social-protection bodies real-time verification of the identity card.

The 2026 finance law requires the customs and treasury information systems to be interconnected, with daily and monthly reconciliation of fiscal and non-fiscal revenue. It constitutes the Sydonia World clearance system as an electronic single window. The window centralises declarations, certificates of origin, licences, freight manifests, inspection results and sanitary and phytosanitary certificates. At a workshop on 1 September 2026 customs set out an electronic single-window project that goes beyond the SYDONIAWORLD system it uses. It is prepared with the commerce ministry, the AfCFTA Secretariat and the Korean agency CUPIA.

Digital Identity and CRVS

The Ministry of the Interior launched a Mobile ID credential on 10 February 2026. It was developed with the company TO7 Network and steered jointly by the national cybersecurity authority and the population and family directorate. Its design keeps the biometrics in one pair of hands: the ministry is to store the biometric data exclusively. Other institutions reach it only through encrypted server-to-server requests. Partner administrations reach only the authentication services, without direct access to the databases. At the forum that preceded the launch, President Ismaïl Omar Guelleh set the benefits of biometric identity against the risks of identity theft, cloning, hacking, espionage and over-centralisation. He called for continuous public vigilance.

At the February 2026 launch its priority uses were civil registry services, tax procedures, bank account opening and electronic signature of official documents. Driving licences and selected health information are planned for integration. It complements the national identification number and the modernisation of the civil registers.

The statutory base long predates the app: Law No. 39/AN/19 of 21 January 2019 created the Registre National des Personnes Physiques. It also created a national identification number assigned from birth. A 2020 decree fixed the number as a twelve-position code encoding sex, date of birth, a sequence number and a check digit. It required the number on virtually every official document, from property and hospital records to school, university, utility and commercial ones, telecom subscriber records included. At its launch in April 2022 the card cost 2,500 FDJ and was valid for ten years. Foreign nationals residing regularly are eligible for the register. The register and number were launched in April 2022 under the population and family directorate. The German firm Dermalog built them, with the state information systems agency supervising.

An earlier attempt was abandoned: the e-ID subcomponent of the World Bank's Public Administration Modernization Project was dropped at the first restructuring on 17 March 2022 and never delivered. A national statistics institute survey activity with the World Bank and UNHCR flags a risk: digital identification could inadvertently exclude undocumented residents from healthcare and education.

Digital Payments and Fintech

Djibouti is a live participant in the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System (2026). AfricaNenda's 2024 review dropped SYRAD from its landscape of live instant payment systems because its instant-payment service was not yet live. The central bank's clearing modernisation is in a second, French-funded phase that builds a national switch to bring every payment instrument into clearing (November 2024).

SYRAD, a real-time gross settlement system paired with an automated clearing house, went into service on 15 July 2021 with the Treasury as a participant. The Banque Centrale de Djibouti owns, oversees and operates it, reporting its volumes and values annually. The statute behind it is Loi n°118/AN/15/7ème L of 16 July 2016. The central bank ruled by communiqué that from 31 August 2025 no cheque failing the new SYRAD format, security and encoding standards would be cleared.

Fewer than a third of the population hold a bank account (2023). Access to Banking Services is among Djibouti's ten worst Ibrahim Index indicators, at 10.0 out of 100 in 2023 and 40th of 54 African states. Open and active electronic-money accounts rose to 142,681 in 2024 from 69,133 in 2023. The country's first online payment gateway opened on 10 December 2023, run by the state operator Djibouti Telecom through its D-Money service. D-Money supports person-to-person transfers and merchant payments and added international transfers to 15 destinations in July 2025. Electronic payment has since been introduced for vehicle road tax, stamp duty and licensing fees. From November 2026 the Djibouti–Ethiopia corridor road operator takes road-use fees only by mobile money. A national payment gateway for government was part of an e-government architecture still being implemented (2024).

Djibouti signed an MoU with Visa on 16 September 2025 to develop a national Smart Wallet combining payments, government services and digital identity.

Registries

Djibouti's Social Registry held 515,730 people in February 2025 against a target of 600,000. The World Bank-financed expansion meant to close that gap had not commenced on data to 31 January 2026. A 2020 decree made the register the sole instrument for targeting beneficiaries of social-protection programmes. Its target population is poor Djiboutian households and the refugee population resident on national territory.

In 2017 birth, marriage and death registration was processed manually on paper, with no regular process for establishing vital statistics. Djibouti-Ville has run a computerised civil status system, SIGEC, since 2011, and commissioned an expert diagnostic of it in 2024. In a 2025 regional survey the country reported a national strategy for digitising civil registration but no national or central database of civil records. Its Civil Registration score in the Ibrahim Index was 62.5 out of 100 in 2023, 24th of 54 African states, unchanged over the decade.

The 2018 land-property law gives the centralised scanned and electronic database of land books legal effect as the operative title. It requires every registered private parcel to be mapped and recorded, and obliges the registry to let the public consult it free of charge. By 2018 most deed records had been computerised or scanned, sharing a single database and common property identifiers with the cadastral agency. There was no electronic database for checking encumbrances, and cadastral maps were still on paper. The Registre de Commerce et des Sociétés, maintained by ODPIC, is legally mandatory within a month of incorporation. It issues registration receipts and certificates.

The electoral register for the presidential election of 10 April 2026 was fixed by arrêté at 261,857 voters. The 2016 revision of the roll registered 187,000, and the African Union's observers found it had not won the consensus of all parties. La Poste de Djibouti adopted what3words as the official national addressing standard in February 2017.

Sectoral management information systems

The national DHIS2 platform does not yet take in community-level data, and there are no community indicators or tools for collecting it (2024). The medicines procurement office announced mSupply for pharmaceutical stock installed across all health facilities in April 2025. In May 2025 its project coordinator put it at 35 sites, with the whole health map to be covered by the end of 2025. In April 2025 WHO's country office recorded that assessments had shown limitations in the health data system, including challenges in the use of DHIS2. Resources were still being sought to revamp it. An HIV/TB Tracker built by HISP Rwanda was launched on DHIS2 in 2025 with the health-information department and the national HIV/TB programme.

In education the ministry made consolidation of the education management information system through the GOSE and SMIS platforms a strategic priority for 2025-2026. The aim is to identify at-risk pupils and track them individually. In 2019 school directors collected data digitally through the OPAD application, but the ministry's statistics office still re-entered it by hand because the systems were incompatible. The statistical yearbook appeared about seven months late. Djibouti is the first country to have validated 100% of its school locations on Giga Maps (April 2026), after AI identification of school buildings from satellite imagery.

The tax directorate is finishing an Integrated Tax Administration System begun under an earlier World Bank project (December 2024). E-filing, e-payment and other modules are still to be implemented. For the state's own payroll, the 2026 finance law requires the Fichier Unique database of state personnel to be updated to take in contract staff and headcounts awaiting regularisation.

The sectoral management information systems now being built in Djibouti — across education, social protection, health, justice, tax and land — are financed by external donor projects rather than from the domestic budget.

Innovation ecosystem

Djibouti's Startup Act has been in force since its promulgation in December 2025. It creates a state "Startup" label, granted by ministerial order on the binding opinion of a Collège des Startups. A national Startup Djibouti portal centralises applications.

Under the 2025 Act a company must be under seven years old and built on technological innovation. More than half its capital must be held by natural persons or seed investors, and it pays no dividends while it holds the label. The label brings a simplified customs regime for imports used in research and development, prototyping and production. The same law also carries a renewable startup visa, a regulatory sandbox and state-backed investment vehicles including a fund of funds and a crowdfunding regime.

In 2024 two hubs carried the sector: CTID, the country's first incubator, established in 2017, and Startup Djibouti, a government-backed accelerator. CTID runs entrepreneurship events and training cohorts, and Startup Djibouti offers incubation, acceleration and grant funding. In July 2026 the state added LIMO, a platform meant to route startups, developers and entrepreneurs towards support mechanisms. Work on an École 42 coding campus launched in April 2026 with the European Union, Expertise France and Association 42.

In June 2026 one Djiboutian company was running a marketplace and delivery service across merchants, restaurants and pharmacies. It settles in cash on delivery and selected mobile money. Workshops in October 2025 designed digital agricultural advisory services for Ali-Sabieh and Tadjourah under an African Development Bank programme. The Digital Economy and Innovation Roadmap, launched in April 2022, set out digital economic transformation, digital administration, sectors and professions of the future, and citizen participation.

Inclusion

Access to services

51.8% of Djibouti's working-age population was outside the labour force altogether in 2025. 46.2% of 16-24-year-olds were not in employment, education or training in 2025. 40.3% of the unemployed already held an upper-secondary or tertiary qualification (2025). On the first results of the 2025 EDST labour force survey, 77.9% of private-sector jobs are informal. Employment in Djibouti City is concentrated in services, at 70.4% (2025). Outside the capital the economy is more mixed, agriculture accounting for 17.1% of employment in 2025.

Identity-card issuance is fee-based, on tariffs set by a 2022 decree. The card is legally required proof of identity and a prerequisite for voter registration, passports, government benefits, employment and school enrolment. People without a valid card, particularly parts of the nomadic population, risk exclusion from them. A survey activity run with the national statistics institute, IOM and UNHCR flags that digital identification could inadvertently exclude undocumented residents from healthcare and education. The Social Registry's own decree names the refugee population resident on national territory in its target group. Loi n° 151/AN/22 obliges the state to give persons with special needs access to information and communication, ICT systems and electronic services included. It also requires minimum national accessibility standards.

A civil-status branch opened in the sous-préfecture de Mouloud on 27 April 2026, in a rural part of the Dikhil region. The Universal Service Fund for equitable access to communications remains at design stage (April 2026). Online, the public can order a criminal record extract from La Poste, register a company on a platform launched in January 2026 and file complaints to the housing agency through its E-Doléances portal. The validation workshop for the draft National Land Policy in December 2025 addressed access for women, youth and vulnerable groups.

Geospatial data

The China Meteorological Administration handed MAZU-Djibouti 2.0 to Djibouti on 17 July 2026, an AI-driven monitoring and alerting system supporting port activities. The upgrade sharpened forecast resolution from 9km to 3km. It builds on MAZU-Urban, commissioned on 18 December 2025, which supports aviation weather services at the international airport and operational safety at ports such as Doraleh. The national meteorological agency itself ran four automatic weather stations, all in the south and none compliant with the global basic observing network (March 2025). Its PUMA satellite workstation was not operational, and imagery and model output were taken from the web. In September 2026 it was seeking US$2.01 million for 2027 to 2031 from the Systematic Observations Financing Facility. It would upgrade the airport surface station and rebuild an upper-air station. On 16 September 2025 it issued its first warning in the Common Alerting Protocol, through a new website built on the WMO's ClimWeb template.

Djibouti's own programme, HYDROSAT, flies two 1U CubeSats, DJIBOUTI-1A and DJIBOUTI-1B, launched in November 2023 and on 21 December 2024. Its primary mission is store-and-forward data collection rather than imaging. The payload interrogates ground stations and collects up to 4.2MB of data, received by the national research centre several times a day. The secondary imaging payload resolves at 1km per pixel with files capped at 40KB. Both have a two-year nominal mission life against a five-year orbital lifetime.

DJIBOUTI-1A passed that two-year mark around November 2025 and DJIBOUTI-1B reaches it in December 2026. The two postures point opposite ways on ownership: the sovereign asset is a coarse ground-station relay, while the operationally consequential capability — port and airport hazard alerting — runs on Chinese satellites, models and hardware, donated rather than procured.

IGAD, headquartered in Djibouti, issued an expression of interest on 5 March 2026 for a consultant to build satellite-based crop-condition monitoring across the member states. The Council of Ministers approved a draft decree on 11 June 2024 setting one geodetic reference for all mapping and cadastral work. Domestically, a JICA-supported project to modernise the management of geospatial information closed in December 2025.

In November 2025 a Bahraini banking group and its Djibouti subsidiary structured a US$10 million private credit facility for the country's digital infrastructure with TLG Capital. The announcement names no borrower. In April 2026 Gulf Cooperation Council placements for graduates of Djibouti's SKILLS Project were still under discussion. By then sixteen of the programme's candidates had been placed in employment in Canada. No signed digital instrument between a Gulf government and Djibouti had been found as at November 2026.

India activities

In July 2026, working through India's ESC export-promotion council, the government invited Indian AI companies to its National Forum on Artificial Intelligence of 2-3 September 2026. It offered visa-support letters. It presented the Forum's launch of the national AI strategy as a platform for business partnerships, technology collaboration and investment.

In October 2025 the European Commission presented the EU-Africa-India Digital Corridor as a concrete deliverable under the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor. The corridor is centred on the 11,700 km Blue Raman system landing in Djibouti, India and Oman. The economic corridor was announced at the September 2023 G20 summit in New Delhi. An accompanying memorandum pairs Europe's GEANT research network with India's National Knowledge Network and Africa's UbuntuNet Alliance.

Digital sovereignty

Djibouti's 2025 Digital Code requires a host of health data to hold a certificate from the national cybersecurity authority (articles 758 and 760). The host's contract must state where the data is held and how portability and reversibility work. At the end the host must return all the data and destroy its copies.

Under the 2025 Code personal data may leave the country only for a jurisdiction the data-protection commission judges adequate, or with its authorisation (articles 99 to 102). No instrument fixing where state data is held, or under whose law it is processed, had been found as at September 2026. The government told its first national AI forum on 2 September 2026 that it intends to develop national sovereign-cloud and high-performance computing capacity.

In August 2026 ANSIE's data centre was named to hold the secure backup of the digitised state archives. Its 2026 budget pays for colocation to back that data centre up and for annual support contracts on its equipment and on X-Road. The Interior ministry stores the Mobile ID biometric data exclusively (February 2026). Other institutions reach it only through encrypted server-to-server requests. The central bank owns and operates the SYRAD payment system.

The January 2026 consultation on the draft national AI strategy covered data governance and digital sovereignty. China's MAZU-Djibouti 2.0 port weather-alerting system was handed over with its own terminal in July 2026. The terms of the LinkWise and Visa memoranda on data jurisdiction, portability and exit had not been published as at September 2026. In June 2026 Djibouti's policymakers examined alignment with the African Union Data Policy Framework at a UNESCO workshop in Nairobi.
