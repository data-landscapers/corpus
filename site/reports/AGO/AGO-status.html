This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

Presidential Decree 256/25 of 3 December 2025 approved Angola's cybersecurity strategy, organised around six strategic objectives. Its strategic actions are the creation of the National Cybersecurity Council, the National Cybersecurity Centre and CERT.ao. It separately commits to a two-phase legislative reform, whose first phase is the passage of laws by the legislature. Presidential Decree 258/25 created the Conselho Nacional de Cibersegurança on the same day, as a 21-member consultative body chaired by the President.

The statute followed as Lei n.º 9/26 of 28 September 2026. On 31 July 2026 the government told the National Assembly that the existing programme to reinforce technological infrastructure will help the country protect itself from attacks like the one on the national carrier.

The White Paper on Information and Communication Technologies 2023-2027, approved on 5 December 2024, is the ICT strategy. It sets the sector's policy and strategic measures towards five objectives for 2027. The 2025 GovTech assessment records a current digital transformation strategy and an established institution with a mandate for digital government. It records a data-governance function held by an autonomous institution and applied across government, not sector by sector. In March 2026 the head of the Casa Civil set out a digital transition agenda for public administration.

UNESCO's readiness assessment, presented on 30 June 2026, identified the absence of a National AI Strategy as a gap. It also named deficits in human capital and interministerial coordination. A national AI plan built on its recommendations was in preparation in July 2026. No draft has been found.

Angola scored 43.8 out of 100 for overall governance in 2023, 36th of 54 African states and below the continental average of 49.3. Budgetary and Financial Management is its most deteriorated measure of the decade, down 13.9 points to 44.4 (2023).

Legislation and regulation

Angola's cybersecurity statute was published as Lei n.º 9/26 of 28 September 2026 and is in force. It revokes the 2017 law and gives operators of critical infrastructure, essential services, electronic communications, data centres and cloud 180 days to register with the national cybersecurity centre. The National Assembly adopted the bill on 12 August 2026 by 104 votes to 56, the governing party for and the opposition against with no abstentions. Its penalties are set in multiples of the national minimum wage, not in kwanza. They run from seven to 70 for individuals on the lightest administrative infringements up to 450 for individuals and 4,000 for legal persons on the most serious. It obliges telecommunications operators to transmit any communication whose content is criminal or prejudicial to state security. The opposition and part of civil society object to this duty. They say the law defines neither the qualifying content nor when a subscriber's data reaches the authorities.

Lei n.º 6/26 was gazetted in Diário da República I Série n.º 146 and took effect on 4 August 2026. It carries one to three years' imprisonment for publishing false information, and three to eight where the content incites hatred or damages a reputation. The term runs up to ten years where the content compromises electoral processes or state security.

Angola has no dedicated legal regime for electronic signatures, digital certification or public key infrastructure. Lei de Autorização Legislativa n.º 12/26 of 2 October 2026 gives the President 90 days to legislate the electronic signature and digital certification regime.

A complete draft artificial-intelligence law went out for public consultation in September 2025. A digital government law entered public consultation on 6 May 2026. A bill setting the legal regime for collecting and processing identity-card and criminal-record data was cleared by the Council of Ministers for the Assembly on 29 April 2026. The National Assembly approved its criminal-record and minors' register chapters in detail on 30 October 2026. What binds meanwhile is Law 23/11 on electronic communications, 2/20 on video-surveillance, 11/20 on cellular location and electronic surveillance, and the Criminal Code 38/20.

Data protection

The Agência de Protecção de Dados' own register listed 15 fines between 2022 and 2026, from US$75,000 to US$525,000 (October 2026). The largest was levied on Banco de Poupança e Crédito in 2022. Deliberations reported in June 2026 fined Crescer Tech US$505,000 for notification and authorisation failures and for moving client data to cloud servers outside Angola. They fined Fast Digital Center US$112,000 for inadequate security measures in 2026. A US$115,000 penalty against a hotel was published in August 2026.

Law 22/11 of June 2011 is the omnibus statute setting processing principles, regulating sensitive data and establishing the authority with notification and authorisation powers. Under it the authority applies fines itself, and refusing the collaboration it requires is a crime. Circular 2/2024 of October 2024 requires operators to notify it of any breach compromising personal data. Public consultation closed in June 2026 on a revision adding a data-protection officer duty for high-volume processors, stronger oversight and sanctions, and fines in local currency. It goes next to the Council of Ministers and then the National Assembly.

Under a 2016 presidential decree every seat on the authority's board is a presidential appointment. Any member may be removed by decree at any time, without stated grounds. The whole board may be dissolved on a change of orientation about its management.

The 2011 law dispenses with authorisation wherever processing derives from a legal instrument. Processing under the rules on state secrecy, state security and segredo de justiça falls outside the law altogether. Lei n.º 11/20 of 2020 gives the authority only knowledge and monitoring of the criminal police database. It obliges private video-surveillance owners to give the police access. Amnesty International's Security Lab confirmed Predator spyware active on an Angolan journalist's phone on 4 May 2024. In February 2026 Portugal's Expresso reported that an April 2021 contract record names the state intelligence service SINSE as its end-user. On 8 August 2026 the South African researchers Intelwatch found no rule stating when the intelligence service may obtain identity, biometric or mobility data. It found no independent body to review such requests.

Regional collaboration

Mozambique's Boane Earth Station was receiving the ANGOSAT-2 signal in operational tests when the two countries signed space and ICT cooperation memoranda in June 2026. Telecom Namibia signed a pilot commercial agreement for ANGOSAT-2 services at ANGOTIC 2026 in Luanda, after a proof of concept. The space agency GGPEN signed a space-science cooperation agreement with Nigeria's NASRDA at the same event. In October 2026 Angola and Tanzania were reported to have signed an ICT memorandum in Dar es Salaam during President Lourenço's state visit. Tanzania is seeking access to ANGOSAT-2 services under it.

The National Assembly approved the African Union Convention on Cyber Security and Personal Data Protection by Resolution 33/19 of 9 July 2019. No deposit of an instrument of ratification has been found. Angola has signed the UN Convention against Cybercrime, with ratification outstanding as at 3 March 2026.

The regional IDEA digitalisation programme held its inaugural forum in Lusaka on 29 July 2026, which recommended harmonising member states' digital policies. The Instituto de Modernização Administrativa is Angola's national focal point for the programme. Angola's Digital Acceleration Project is one national part of a five-state programme with the DRC, Zambia, Malawi and Eswatini.

Regional Integration is among Angola's ten worst-scoring governance measures, at 21.2 out of 100 in 2023 and 48th of 54 African states. It ranks there despite membership of both ECCAS and SADC. The data-protection authority signed a memorandum with Brazil's Autoridade Nacional de Proteção de Dados on mutual assistance at the 47th Global Privacy Assembly in Seoul.

Angola signed the CPLP Charter of Rights and Principles in Digital Environments at São Tomé on 19 July 2024, with all nine member states. The charter is expressly declaratory and non-binding. Its regulators joined a Maputo workshop in December 2025 where UNESCO launched the Portuguese edition of its Data Governance Toolkit. On 10 September 2026 Angola was one of 49 states that signed, with the European Space Agency, a declaration that satellite observation data be open to all save justified exceptions. No domestic measure taken under any of these instruments has been found.

Standards

Angola's quality-infrastructure institute published a 2026 national standards catalogue incorporating 140 new standards. The catalogue states that Angolan standards are voluntary unless legislation makes them mandatory. The institute is an ISO correspondent member, a category carrying no vote on technical work. Lei n.º 8/25 of 16 September 2025 fixes unique sequential alphanumeric codes for the country, its 21 provinces, 326 municipalities and 378 communes. The codes follow ISO-derived criteria, expressly to ease data exchange with international systems.

The video-surveillance regulation of December 2021 fixes housings, encoding, IPv6, resolution and zoom for closed-circuit cameras. It encrypts all transmission, with keys rotated every six months. It specifies the mandatory warning sign down to its mounting height.

Presidential Decree 46/18 of 2018 approved PNAGIA, the national interoperability architecture plan for the state administration. The 2025 GovTech assessment records that framework and a Government Service Bus in place but only partially used across public bodies. The 2026 terms of reference for its replacement cite a study across 25 public institutions that found only 27% of systems interconnected in 2026. Integration was predominantly ad hoc. No public key infrastructure has been established, and the law behind the identity card's chip provides for no authentication mechanism (2024).

Under Angola's e-invoicing rules, as reported in October 2025, invoices must be issued from software certified by the tax authority and transmitted in real time, on SAF-T. The central bank completed the migration of domestic payments to the ISO 20022 messaging standard in July 2026.

Portugal's telecommunications regulator ANACOM held cooperation meetings in Luanda in March 2026 with INACOM and the EU Delegation. It offered training in ICT policy and regulation. MINTTICS republished its 2025 procurement plan on 5 March 2025. The correction cut the ministry's 2025 total from Kz 616.7 biliões, larger than the entire state budget, to Kz 225.6 mil milhões.

Public debate and participation in policymaking

Digital Freedom is one of Angola's ten best-scoring governance measures, at 70.9 out of 100 in 2023 and 19th of 54 African states. Media Freedom fell 2.7 points over 2014-2023 to 45.1 out of 100, 38th of 54. Freedom of Expression and Belief fell 1.3 points over the same decade to 49.2, 35th of 54. Both fell while the country's overall governance score rose.

The law governing what Angolans may publish online took effect on 4 August 2026. UNITA voted against it, saying the text opens the way to criminalising journalists and ordinary users. The MPLA presented it as a measure against structured disinformation. Opponents of the cybersecurity bill warned that without strong judicial guarantees it could become an instrument of abusive surveillance and digital repression. The responsible minister called the bill strategically important.

The artificial-intelligence bill was published in full on the telecommunications ministry's consultation portal in September 2025. The digital government bill went out for consultation on 6 May 2026. On 25 June 2026 the Assembly rejected in plenary a UNITA bill to open the electoral personal-data registers. The majority argued that it would expose sensitive data without consent. In 2025 the GovTech assessment recorded an online public participation platform, with a separate portal handling petitions. It found a citizen feedback mechanism for public services that lacks every supporting capability the assessment tests for.

The 2025 GovTech assessment grades a right to information law as in force, not adopted and dormant. Disclosure of public records is among Angola's worst-scoring governance measures (2023). The documentation behind most assessed government systems and oversight functions is kept internally and not published. That covers the interoperability framework, the national service portal, the digital government mandate, the transformation strategy and the data-protection authority's reporting.

Finance

Financial sustainability

Angola's 2025 budget law appropriates Kz 107.8 billion to the national broadband network in 2025 (FY2025). It appropriates Kz 70.5 billion in 2025 to the analytics platform, main and backup data centres and national cloud platform (FY2025). The same two lines received Kz 1.4 billion and Kz 2.0 billion a year earlier, in 2024 (FY2024).

The digital lines of the 2025 budget law come to Kz 384.4 billion appropriated, up from Kz 142.1 billion in 2024, and those two lines account for most of the rise (FY2025).

The 2025 law prints its financing split only for the budget as a whole, 20.47% of it external (FY2025). The 2025 budget law does not print the source of financing against its digital lines (FY2025). No outturn against the digital appropriations had been published as at September 2026.

The World Bank's US$300 million digital acceleration loan of 2024 is on IBRD terms, a reference rate plus variable spread with a 0.25% front-end fee. It closes in March 2030. The President authorised US$210.91 million of digital-state purchases between February and August 2026, all run through that project. They range from public digital infrastructure and digital identity to interoperability, AI and cybersecurity.

A 2010 presidential decree gives the communications development fund 1% of public telecommunications operators' gross revenue. The national cybersecurity centre's 2025 statute sends 30% of the revenue it collects, fines included, to a cybersecurity fund. No decree had constituted that fund as at September 2026. Presidential Decree 178/24 of July 2024 approved the public administration's digital transition agenda, with financing and sustainability one of its seven axes. That axis is to identify funding sources and bring in the private sector.

MoUs and other agreements

Angola's national government cloud originates in an Angola–United Arab Emirates memorandum of December 2021. The telecoms ministry lists its five heads: a three-year digitalisation master plan, public-private partnerships, build-operate-transfer and other financing channels, digital skills, and further joint projects. It was put into effect between MINTTICS and the Abu Dhabi big-data firm Presight. Presight signed its own memorandum with the government in the same month, to develop a transformation programme for "Digital Angola".

At the ANGOTIC conference in June 2026 GGPEN and Africell agreed to expand ANGOSAT-2 services. Eutelsat and the Angolan provider Mercury Telecom signed for multi-year LEO capacity. Telecom Namibia and GGPEN signed a pilot for ANGOSAT-2 capacity, alongside a ministerial ICT cooperation memorandum between the two countries. Unitel and Angola Telecom agreed to share infrastructure in underserved border regions.

In July 2026 the Banco Nacional de Angola and the DRC's central bank signed a memorandum on payment-system interoperability and cheaper cross-border payments. A digital-governance pact was signed with Cape Verde in March 2026. In November 2025 Angola and Kenya agreed at the Luanda summit on financing Africa's infrastructure to fast-track an ICT cooperation memorandum. Three memoranda signed at the modernisation institute's forum in March 2026 cover broadband connectivity, digital inclusion and the national digital-identity system. The data protection authority and Brazil's ANPD signed on technical assistance, regulation and supervision at the Global Privacy Assembly in Seoul.

The Cybastion and Angola Telecom investment announced in June 2025 rests on a memorandum of understanding signed at Mobile World Congress in Barcelona in March 2025.

New investments

By June 2026 the World Bank's US$300 million loan for the Angola Digital Acceleration Project had disbursed US$112.21 million, 37.40 per cent of the total. That is the Bank's fourth supervision report, against a closing date of March 2030. Approved in June 2024, the loan was still not effective in March 2025, with only US$1 million drawn from a preparation advance. Both its ratings were then cut to Moderately Unsatisfactory. The Bank restored them to Moderately Satisfactory in June 2026. The loan allocates US$100m to connectivity and inclusion, US$122.25m to digital public infrastructure, US$54m to productive digital usage and US$23m to project management (June 2026). The regional programme's report for January to June 2026 records 17 contracts signed and 29 tenders under way.

China Eximbank lent RMB 1,736,207,810.54 on concessional terms for the national broadband network under a loan agreement of October 2023. France arranged the largest European commitment, €225,257,201 through Société Générale for the Earth-observation satellite programme, signed in January 2025. Projecto Njila, launched in 2023, carries US$250 million for local governance and civil registration; its digital share is not split out. A World Bank project carries US$60 million for statistical capacity to 2027. Cybastion and Angola Telecom announced an investment in cybersecurity and a Luanda–Lobito cable in June 2025. In 2015 JBIC extended a US$66 million buyer's credit for the South Atlantic Cable System.

The 2026 state budget cut the named connectivity programme 30.5% to Kz 159.2 billion, with the 4G expansion line down 42% to Kz 62.7 billion. The World Bank's tertiary education project of 2023, a US$150m loan and a US$50m education-partnership grant, carries US$10m to digitalise higher-education services. It carries US$15m for the sector's management information system (2023). By 1 October 2026 thirteen public higher-education institutions held results-based agreements worth US$38.7m under it, of which US$710,000 had been disbursed. In September 2026 the US Export-Import Bank announced a US$99.6m loan to Africell for network technology in Angola.

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

Unitel, the largest operator, reported more than 21.2 million subscribers and 12,302 base stations for the first half of 2026. When its network went down at the end of July 2026, Africell was the only operator running fully.

The regulator reported to the ITU in November 2025 that 55.50% of individuals used the internet in 2024, against 42.10% in 2022. In 2024 4G covered 77.41% of the population, and 5G reached only Luanda, Benguela and Huambo. In March 2026 the ministry, counting on a different basis, put the country above 28 million mobile subscriptions and above 17 million broadband users. An entry-level mobile broadband basket cost about 1.53% of gross national income per capita in 2024.

The terrestrial fibre network connects every provincial and communal capital (May 2023). In May 2026 the justice ministry blamed four months of outages at identification and civil registry posts across most of the country on telecommunications breakdowns. The first link of the national fibre broadband network, from Luanda through Bengo and Cuanza Sul, went into service at Lobito on 16 September 2026. Huambo, Namibe and Cabinda are named for the next phase.

Unitel put US$43 million into Angola's branch of the 2Africa cable at Cacuaco in December 2025, securing 23.6 Tbps for the country. A June 2026 agreement with Telecom Namibia and SATCAB gives Angola Telecom an initial 1.0 Tbps of Equiano capacity. The state operator gives June 2027 for SARSSy, a cable landing in five Angolan provinces and joining Equiano at Swakopmund, to replace the ageing SAT-3 (July 2026). Terrestrial fibre already runs to Namibia between Santa Clara and Ondjiva. In June 2026 the ministers of Angola and Mozambique signed a memorandum for a Nacala-Lobito submarine cable. Used international bandwidth stood at 5.50 kbit/s per capita in November 2025, unchanged from the June 2024 baseline, against a project target of 10. Two internet exchange points were active in June 2026, with 29 members between them.

On 30 April 2026 the Digital Acceleration Project's outcome indicators for broadband users, users of digital services and adopting firms all still read zero. Its target is 13.5 million people brought to broadband by March 2030.

Data Storage

Angolans' passports and education certificates are to be stored on national infrastructure in the state's own data centre, opened in April 2026. Until then foreign entities or platforms held them.

President João Lourenço inaugurated the Data Center e Cloud do Governo at Camama in Luanda on 28 April 2026. It is built to Uptime Institute Tier III, with 208 racks and 1,040 kW of power. As at April 2026 the US$89 million project, led by the state IT agency INFOSI with Presight and G42, hosted more than 80 government services on a single sovereign cloud. A secondary site replicates it, and fibre links it to the ministries of finance, telecommunications, interior, education and health. Migration onto it is progressive and mandatory for public services. The World Bank's 2025 GovTech assessment records no whole-of-government cloud service or government cloud policy.

Commercial capacity is thin and sits in Luanda, where Raxio opened AG1 at Cacuaco in October 2025 as a US$30 million investment. It offers up to 3 MW. In May 2026 it was one of five Uptime Institute-certified facilities. The others were MSTelcom's, Banco Millennium Atlântico's and the interbank company EMIS's two. Paratus runs two data centres in the city and in 2023 began building a third. Africell built an enterprise data centre at its Luanda headquarters in 2021. There is no in-country hyperscale cloud region (2024).

No broad data localisation requirement applies (2025). Banking and oil and gas data is mostly hosted locally in practice. Personal-data transfers abroad are subject to an adequate-protection test and notification of the data protection authority. Financial institutions need the central bank's prior approval to contract cloud or data-processing services. They must also disclose the hosting country. Private credit bureaux must keep their primary processing and replica sites in Angola, at least 30 km apart (Presidential Decree 275/20, 2020). They may use cloud storage only with the data protection authority's prior authorisation. The National Press inaugurated its own data centre in Luanda on 11 September 2026, for producing and certifying high-security state documents. It has more than 170 TB of storage; no cost is published.

Energy

Just under half of Angolan households, 48.6%, had electricity at the 2024 census, up from 31.9% in 2014. Only 8.5% of rural households had it in 2024, against 68.9% of urban ones. Afrobarometer reported in 2023 that 14% of rural Angolans lived in areas the national grid serves, against 92% of urban residents.

51.1% of the population had access to electricity in 2023. Angola's Access to Energy score reached 48.0 out of 100 in 2023, 20th of 54 African states, after rising 10.2 points over the decade. Hydropower supplied 88.5% of electricity output in 2020. Five high-voltage towers fell on 16 December 2025, leaving much of Cuanza-Sul and Benguela without power until 18 December.

In 2024 off-grid provision was covered by a comprehensive policy framework spanning geospatial planning, subsidies, private-sector participation, mini-grids and standalone solar. Ilumina Angola reaches remote sites with shared towers, solar power and satellite backhaul. Cubango's move to electronic provincial administration is planned around weak electricity and weak internet in its municipalities (December 2025).

Technical Capacity

The World Bank's 2024 diagnostic found the civil registration software locked to its vendor, which has never handed the government its source code. It found the Chinese state firm CEIEC providing technical coordination of the PGIBI identity platform. It found services constrained by limited human and logistical resources, and recommended standard operating procedures backed by staff training.

At April 2025 all but one of the Digital Acceleration Project's technical activities were significantly delayed, the exception being interoperability work. The delay extended to the upstream technical studies meant to shape the investments that follow them. The Bank raised both project ratings in June 2026 on improved collaboration between the participating institutions. Institutional capacity and stakeholder risk both stayed above their levels at approval.

UNESCO's readiness assessment, presented on 30 June 2026, named deficits in human capital and interministerial coordination alongside the absence of a national AI strategy. In 2025 the public service's human resource management system was still being implemented, on commercial off-the-shelf software. On 28 October 2026 the public administration ministry launched SINGERH, a single civil-service personnel database. Angola sat below the African average on the UN's e-government index in 2024.

Cybersecurity

Angola's cybersecurity law was published as Lei n.º 9/26 of 28 September 2026 and is in force, revoking the 2017 law. It creates a national cybersecurity system, with CERT.ao as the operational arm of its centre. It requires operators to notify significant incidents.

Presidential Decree 263/25, published on 10 December 2025, created the Centro Nacional de Cibersegurança as a public institute under the superintendence of the telecommunications minister. It holds regulation, rule-making, supervision, inspection and sanctioning powers. Under the 2025 decree it splits the revenue it collects, fines included, 40% to the Treasury, 30% to the Centro and 30% to a cybersecurity fund. The World Bank recorded no national CIRT established as at 30 April 2026. On 12 October 2026 the telecommunications ministry said a national commission would be formed to create, within 180 days, the conditions to implement the Centro. The one binding sectoral rule is older: the central bank's Aviso n.º 8/20 of 2 April 2020 sets cyber-security and cloud-contracting rules for the institutions it supervises.

In January 2026 the national bar association argued that the Centro would combine regulation, rule-making, supervision, inspection and sanction in breach of the separation of powers. It argued that access to private communications on an administrative decision should require prior judicial authorisation.

On 28 July 2026 Unitel lost mobile voice, data and internet nationwide to a cyberattack. The attack came in the week its shares were admitted to trading on the BODIVA exchange. The operator declared its core mobile services fully restored on 21 August 2026. It states that its forensic investigation found no evidence of unauthorised access to customer or partner personal data; that is its own account, not a regulator's finding. No attribution or attack vector had been disclosed as at 31 July 2026.

Angola sat in the evolving tier of the ITU's Global Cybersecurity Index in 2024. The 2026 state budget proposal cut the appropriation for improving that position. The President authorised US$4.6m on 31 August 2026 to strengthen state cybersecurity.

DPI

Data Exchange

On 17 June 2026 the Instituto de Modernização Administrativa signed the implementation contract with TIS Tech Angola for the US$150m Projecto de Infra-estruturas Digitais Públicas. The twelve-month build is specified as the secure-zone host for the interoperability platform, PKI-based electronic signatures and digital identity. The framework study and platform roadmap were completed in the first half of 2026. The platform itself, an enterprise service bus on X-Road, was under tender in 2026 at about US$13.0m.

In 2025 the GovTech assessment recorded a government interoperability framework and a Government Service Bus, the framework only partially used across public bodies. SEPE, the public services portal, is meant to serve as the interoperability platform. Most government websites and online services still run separately from it. The identity platform is interoperable with the financial management system SIGFE, the social-security institute and the Investor Portal (2024).

Civil registration has no central database, its two systems keeping separate records and some offices still on paper (2023). In June 2026 the President created an interministerial commission to stabilise interoperability between the civil-identification database and the adult-citizen database. Kwenda, the main social protection programme, issues its own beneficiary identifier and does not use the national identity system (2024).

Under a 2021 law the electoral register is populated from the national civil identification database by an annual computerised file. The identity card is generated from the birth-registration record through interoperability between the two systems. The central bank completed migration of domestic payments to ISO 20022 on 10 July 2026.

A single window launched in September 2025 has a citizen lodge a land-concession file once at the municipal administration. The state then routes it through the tax, cadastral and property-registry bodies. Presidential Decree 11/26 of January 2026 requires the compulsory social-protection manager's information exchange to meet national and international standards of interoperability, security and data protection. In September 2026 the tax and customs administration was integrating the commerce ministry's and other licensing bodies' platforms with its own.

Digital Identity and CRVS

By 24 September 2026, 17,166,000 identity cards had been issued, the registries director said. More than 1,100,000 of them were issued in the universalisation campaign. The target is a card for every adult in the civil registry by December 2026. At the 2024 census reference date, 52.4% of Angolans aged five and over held the Bilhete de Identidade. It was 63% in towns and 30.9% in rural areas (2024). A Council of Ministers bill of 29 April 2026 would make the card the sole valid identification document. On 30 October 2026 the National Assembly approved in detail the bill's chapters on the criminal record and a confidential register for minors.

All 21 provinces printed the card locally as at July 2026. A home enrolment service started across Luanda province on 28 July 2026. More than 120,000 issued cards sat uncollected in July 2026.

The project target is 10 million people with digitally verifiable identification by March 2030; it stood at zero in April 2025. The credential is biometric, with ten fingerprints, photograph, signature and height captured at enrolment. The civil-identification law provides for no authentication mechanism, digital certificate or public key infrastructure for its chip. Despacho Presidencial 300/26 of 13 August 2026 authorised USD 28,000,000 and a public tender for a platform to carry up to 30 million digital identities and electronic signatures. No supplier or delivery date is stated.

39.2% of children aged 0-4 had their birth registered in 2024, up from 24.8% in 2014. The 2023-24 health survey found 38% of children under five registered and 36% holding a birth certificate. Most civil registration and identification records are held in unindexed paper files. Nascer com Registo, launched at Mbanza Kongo in March 2026 with EU and UNICEF support, places registry posts inside maternity wards.

On 9 October 2026 a private firm released its AngolaID wallet in test mode, with credentials created by video liveness check and verified by QR code. The company says it neither issues nor replaces the state identity card. Later in October 2026 the state information-society agency certified the platform for use nationwide.

Digital Payments and Fintech

The kwanza was introduced as a settlement currency in the SADC real-time gross settlement system on 27 July 2026, only the second in the system's history. Angolan customers reach it indirectly, through five participating commercial banks.

The central bank's annual accounts put KWiK at Kz 590.36 thousand million across 35,000,724 transfers in 2025, up 725.4% in value on 2024. They record the effective end of the cheque in 2025, with 4,989 items cleared against 38,664 in 2024. By number, Paypay handled 52.2% of KWiK transfers in February 2026, against 21.9% for Banco BAI and 12.6% for Unitel Money. Of the Kz 557.4bn moved over KWiK in the first half of 2026, banks originated about 62% and digital wallets about 38%. Wallet-to-wallet transfers were 0.6% of the total in the first half of 2026.

Financial inclusion stood at 51.7% in the first quarter of 2026, against a 65% target for 2027, with banking at 32%. The identity card is a prerequisite for opening a bank account or obtaining a loan (2024). In its first three years to August 2023, Kwenda made 77% of its payments in cash and 0.4% by mobile money. Afrimoney counted 3 million wallet users among Africell's 8 million subscribers in September 2026.

Aviso 4/25 of 22 August 2025 set the central bank's rules for electronic-money accounts, graded by how the holder proves identity. Aviso 1/26 of 28 January 2026 required every non-bank acquirer of payment terminals or ATMs to be an authorised payment service provider. The central bank oversees the payment system under a new Payments System Law. Instrutivo n.º 02/2025 caps Multicaixa payments at Kz 30m a day and credit transfers at Kz 50m. It exempts the finance ministry, the social-security institute and the central bank from the credit-transfer limit.

After the cyberattack on Unitel of 28 July 2026, card terminals were still unreliable in Luanda almost three days later, with queues at ATMs.

Registries

Angola launched its single social registry, the social affairs ministry's Cadastro Social Único for mapping vulnerable households, in July 2026. Presidential Decree 136/19 had established it in 2019. Field enrolment opened in a pilot at Sumbe on 24 August 2026.

By September 2026 the territorial administration ministry had updated more than two million adults on the electoral register, against more than 16.7 million targeted by 31 March 2027. The update runs through the Balcão Único counter network from 15 June 2026. The main opposition party says state means are being used to register the governing party's members early. A civic movement says parties and civil society are kept out of supervising the proof-of-life step (September 2026). At the last election, in August 2022, the roll recorded 14,399,391 voters against a voting-age population of 15,939,218.

Under the 2026 register update, the undocumented must obtain an identity card before they can join the electoral base. Under the 2025 official electoral registration law, enrolment is drawn automatically from the civil identity database. The voter card is used for the last time in 2027. Angola operates no independent biometric electoral registration, and the roll has never been through a comprehensive independent technical audit. Presidential Decree 245/21 of 2021 makes a citizen's taxpayer number identical to their identity card number.

In 2024 the digital cadastre ran in all 18 provincial offices of the cadastral institute, with 450,000 urban parcel boundaries mapped. The SIRP property registration system is installed in all 22 registry offices. In April 2026 the Executive set a target of legalising 40,000 properties by end-2026.

The Guiché Único da Empresa issues a taxpayer number as part of incorporation and logged more than 14,000 company constitutions in 2026. Law 7/26 of 19 August 2026 creates a compulsory central register of beneficial owners. In October 2026 a technical group of ten state bodies was given 180 days to set it up. The nominal worker register RENT is live and rolling out province by province (2025).

Sectoral management information systems

Electronic invoicing became mandatory in Angola on 1 January 2026 for large taxpayers and suppliers to the state. By November 2026 more than 80,000 taxpayers were issuing electronic invoices, against a tax administration target of two million by 2027. The obligation reaches general and simplified-regime VAT taxpayers only from 2027. AGT runs SIGT, a centralised real-time online tax system. It is integrating with SIGFE so that electronic invoices are validated automatically before the state pays a supplier (2026).

SIGFE has reached 164 municipal administrations (2023). About 40% of municipalities lack consistent internet access and must go to the provincial capital to transact (2023). Presidential Decree 194/25 of 21 October 2025 established an integrated management system for the public investment programme. It is designed to share data with SIGFE.

The health ministry has been moving its information system onto DHIS2 since 2015. Data are collected on paper at health facilities, then aggregated and entered at municipal or provincial level. The move is held back by limited resources, inadequate infrastructure and insufficient IT training. SIGE, the education management information system, is in operation but not a finished product. Its gaps forced the ministry onto structured spreadsheets to verify the data for the 2022/23 statistical yearbook.

Customs runs on ASYCUDA, and on 5 January 2026 Angola became the first country to put its fifth generation, ASY5, into live operation. A presidential order of 14 September 2026 authorised about US$98m, by direct award, for a customs supervision project. The project covers centralised monitoring and automated inspection, and no supplier is named. Social protection delivery was assessed as nascent in 2024. The Cadastro Social Único was then being linked to the Kwenda management information system and the welfare ministry's SIGAS registry. Digitisation was low and interoperability limited. A social insurance and pension system runs on a hybrid of custom and commercial software (2025). On 28 October 2026 the public administration ministry launched SINGERH, a single civil-service personnel database. It had registered more than 300,000 staff by September and found more than 31,000 recorded as active without proof of employment.

Other GovTech and e-Gov

The electronic procurement system publishes both tender notices and awarded contracts. In 2025 the state payment reference system RUPE, run by EMIS, accounted for 36% of the state's current revenues. It takes tax and fees at ATMs, POS terminals, online and by mobile. Angola scored 0.4149 on the UN E-Government Development Index in 2024, 156th of 193 member states and below the African average.

In March 2026 the head of the Casa Civil set out the Agenda de Transição Digital da Administração Pública, with 218 structuring projects across six objectives and seven axes. Technology

AI

Angola's draft artificial-intelligence law, published for public consultation in September 2025, runs to 86 articles in nine chapters. It creates a right to an explanation of, and to refuse, AI-based decisions. It leaves the competent AI authority to a future regulation, and assigns those rights to no named body, the data protection agency included. The bill repeals no existing law, so it sits on top of Law 22/11 and Lei n.º 11/20 with the interactions unstated. It claims extraterritorial reach over AI activity outside Angola that affects Angolan interests.

As at 27 August 2026 the ministry's portal still carried the September 2025 draft, with 41 contributions recorded. No dedicated artificial-intelligence law is in force. One named class of system, "IA Crítica", carries additional registration, security-risk assessment, disclosure and emergency-response duties. Any wider risk classification is left to a system the state is to establish. The bill puts the state on the supply side, promoting national computing infrastructure and the release of public data.

UNESCO's readiness assessment, presented on 30 June 2026, found 50.9 per cent of its indicators fully met, 32.7 per cent partially met and 16 per cent insufficient. In June 2026 the tax authority's machine-learning system found that 15,000 of the 40,000 companies filing zero-turnover 2025 industrial-tax returns had traded. A road-safety protocol with Portugal approved on 16 April 2026 names artificial-intelligence methods in traffic-offence processing as an area of cooperation. It states that it creates no legally binding obligations.

Despacho Presidencial 342/26 of 27 August 2026 authorised US$6 million and opened a public tender for an artificial-intelligence platform for the public administration. The money is the World Bank's, run through the digital acceleration project. No statement has been found of what the platform is to do or for which bodies. In March 2026 the modernisation institute tendered a consultancy for a governance framework for digital government and for artificial intelligence in the public sector. That instrument is distinct from the bill.

Inclusion

Access to services

Angola's equal-access-to-public-services score was 30.6 out of 100 in 2023, 36th of 54 African states and slightly lower than in 2014. The identity card is a prerequisite for education, municipal administrative services, registering property and vehicles, applying for a job and opening a bank account (2024).

Kwenda had reached more than 1.3 million households, about 6.5 million people, by July 2026. In 2025 beneficiaries with disabilities received a Multicaixa-linked payment card alongside assistive items such as wheelchairs. Community workers delivered benefits to the homes of those who could not travel to distribution points.

In October 2026 the President put free internet access points in 18 provinces at about eight million citizens reached. Digitising passport, driving-licence and vehicle-document services in mid-2025 cleared the crowds and the informal "mixeiros" fixer economy from the doors of the migration service and the traffic directorate. The Unitel outage from 28 July 2026 cut off ride-hailing, balance checks and payments for days. It pushed subscribers onto Africell and Movicel and slowed those networks too. Small-business owners were still counting the cost on 10 August 2026, with phone bookings and card payments both cut off.

Digital divides

In October 2026 the President authorised US$50m, by public tender, for a programme paying market operators to extend broadband to underserved areas, schools and community centres first. What is bought is the state's from the outset. In June 2026 the government named weak internet and mobile coverage in hard-to-reach areas as the chief risk to completing the electoral-register update.

The Fundo de Apoio ao Desenvolvimento das Comunicações rests on a presidential decree of November 2010, not a statute. The 2010 decree sets it at 1% of the gross revenue of public telecommunications operators and service providers, alongside a share of the regulator INACOM's receipts. Its non-repayable grants are reserved for non-commercial expansion of universal service into disadvantaged areas. Its only reporting duty is an annual plan and a report and accounts sent to the supervising ministry; nobody is obliged to publish what it does.

Identity-card holding falls off sharply between urban and rural residents (2024). Under-five birth registration is almost twice as high in towns, 48.7% against 24.8% (2023–24). Registered property parcels are a small fraction of those mapped and cluster in urban areas. Rural residents live overwhelmingly outside the reach of the electricity grid (2023). Adult literacy in Luanda is near-universal (2026).

Angola's equality-in-education score was 30.7 out of 100 in 2023, 47th of 54 African states, after a rise of more than nine points across the decade. National languages other than Portuguese are widely spoken, which drives the multilingual requirement on broadcast and public-information services.

Data

National statistics

In 2023 the World Bank's Statistical Performance Indicators placed Angola in the 70-89% band for data use and data products. It sat in the 50-69% band for data services and infrastructure, and only the 20-49% band for data sources (2023).

A population census, an agricultural census and a business census were all conducted within the ten years to 2023. The definitive results of the 2024 population census were published in November 2025. Only two household surveys and two health surveys were run over the decade, and no agricultural survey at all (2023).

The statistics institute's social statistics yearbook for 2017-2022 warns that national coverage of administrative information is still too thin to compute many indicators. In 2021 civil registration's paper records were traced to the absence of registration infrastructure in rural areas. INE's business census counted around 83,700 enterprises, roughly two thirds of them formal (2019–20). The share of businesses on the tax register has not been found. A UNESCO readiness assessment scored the country's data governance at 47 out of 100 (June 2026). A World Bank programme running to 2027 is funding modernisation of the statistical system. Its first component covers an inventory and evaluation of the administrative data the public administration already keeps.

Presidential Decree 43/26 of 10 March 2026 re-establishes INE as the national statistical authority, with technical, administrative and financial autonomy. It writes technical independence, impartiality and confidentiality into the text. INE put a revision of the statistics law and a draft statistics strategy for 2026 to 2032 to public consultation from 29 September to 2 October 2026.

In May 2026 the US$89 million government cloud at Camama was run by the state IT agency INFOSI. Its twelve modules held Tier III certification in the name of Presight, of the Abu Dhabi G42 group (May 2026). The building awaited its own audit. In August 2026 a rebuilt backup centre on INFOSI's premises, with 48 racks and about 8.5 petabytes, replicated it in real time. ANGOSAT-2 gives it a further continuity path. No terms on data jurisdiction, portability and exit had been published as at September 2026 for the December 2021 memorandum with the United Arab Emirates. The same holds for the agreements with Presight and G42.

In 2024 the state did not have the source code of its civil-registration software, which stays locked to its vendor. Nothing found as at September 2026 shows whether the state has the source code and operating skills for the PGIBI identity platform. Nor does anything found show whether it can exit its operating contract.

In September 2026 a US$6 million public-sector AI platform was framed as giving the state sovereign capacity to adopt, develop and operate AI. It was authorised under Despacho Presidencial 342/26 and financed through the World Bank project. In August 2026 the telecommunications minister defined digital sovereignty as the state's capacity to govern its digital space and choose technologies in the national interest, not technological isolation.
