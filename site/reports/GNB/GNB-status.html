This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

Decreto n.º 10/2025 of 28 March 2025 approved the Estratégia Nacional para a Transformação Digital (ENTD.GW) 2025–2030, the country's first plan for the digital sector as a whole. UNU-EGOV prepared it under a UNDP contract, with six strategic objectives, ten pillars and 55 interventions. The pillars run from infrastructure and data through literacy and trust to public services. Each intervention has a duration in months and suggested start and end dates. Its authors chose a five-year horizon so as not to be limited to the current legislature. The Prime Minister presided at its launch on 29 January 2025, two months before the decree.

The Council of Ministers approved a National Digital Skills Policy by decree on 19 May 2026. In July it was confirmed as approved, with no implementation plan or budget line published. On 9 September 2026 UNU-EGOV presented a government delegation with a project document on the strategy's implementation priorities, governance and resources. No date for its adoption has been found.

The ITU recorded no adopted national broadband policy or plan for Guinea-Bissau in 2019. Cabo Verde, Guinea, Senegal, the Gambia, Mali and Angola each had one in the same edition. The European Union's Multiannual Indicative Programme 2021–2027 names digital skills only among those built through vocational training for jobs and the green economy.

The Ministry of Public Health records that the national health development plan for 2018–2023 was never formally approved. It says this compromised the IANDA Guiné Saúde project. The education sector plan counts sixteen changes of executive power in the four decades to 2017, at an average tenure of 2.5 years. It says this instability weakened the public administration, education included. The government's Beijing+30 report names recurrent political instability, which breaks the continuity of public policy, among the persistent challenges to programmes for women and girls. Guinea-Bissau scored 40.9 of 100 for Overall Governance in 2023, 44th of 54 African states. Its Foundations for Economic Opportunity score fell 2.1 points over 2014–2023 to 28.5, the only one of four categories to fall.

Legislation and regulation

By May 2026 the ICT regulator ARN had put a draft Electronic Transactions Law out to public consultation, with responses closing on 28 May 2026. The 112-article bill would settle the legal effect and evidential value of data messages, the formation of electronic contracts and the licensing of trust-service providers. It also covers signatures and time stamps, consumer protection and the limits of intermediary liability. Entry into force is left to a date the responsible minister designates, possibly provision by provision.

Lei n.º 5/2010, the Lei de Base das Tecnologias de Informação e Comunicação, still sets the rules for establishing and operating ICT networks and services. It constitutes the ARN as the body that regulates, supervises and sanctions them. Four regulations made under it were published together in September 2010. They cover interconnection, supervision and sanctions, public consultation and the offer of networks and services. They give the regulator sanctions from fines through restriction and suspension to revocation.

Decreto n.º 6/2018 gave the 1998 Lei da Terra its general regulation twenty years late. Its preamble blames political-military imperatives and constant governmental instability. A draft access-to-information bill was validated in April 2024 and had gone no further by October that year. The Council of Ministers approved a draft Electronic Communications Law with amendments, at a session chaired by the transitional president, Horta Inta-a. In May 2026 the transitional legislature passed a Digital Integrity Law on false information online. That month it also ratified the African Union, Budapest and United Nations cybercrime conventions. Guinea-Bissau had signed the United Nations convention in October 2025.

Four gaps sit in the digital statute book at once — no access-to-information law, no electronic-transactions law, no domestically effective data-protection regime and no cybercrime or cybersecurity statute, three cybercrime conventions ratified notwithstanding — and in each case a text exists while the last step of adoption, promulgation or gazette publication has not been taken.

Data protection

In September 2026 Guinea-Bissau had no dedicated data protection law and no data protection authority. Several institutions handled the matter sectorally. A draft law would create a Data Protection Agency and leave its organisation to decree.

Both obligations have been owed for sixteen years, binding the country since the ECOWAS Supplementary Act was adopted in February 2010, and neither has been met.

Article 2 of the 2010 ECOWAS Supplementary Act requires a legal framework protecting the privacy of data in its collection, processing, transmission, storage and use. Article 14 requires an independent administrative authority. Articles 16 and 17 secure its members' immunity, freedom from instruction and incompatibility with government office. Article 3(3) applies the Act to any processing in a UEMOA or an ECOWAS member state, so Guinea-Bissau falls inside its scope on either footing. Article 36 lets personal data leave the Community only after the controller informs the national authority. No such authority has been found in Guinea-Bissau. UNECA reads the regional signature as not legally binding while the Act remains unpublished in the country's official gazette. Article 44.º(1) of the Constitution recognises rights to personal identity, good name, image and the privacy of private and family life. Article 48.º(1) makes the home, correspondence and other private communications inviolable, save as criminal-procedure law expressly provides. Article 42.º(6) voids all evidence obtained by abusive intrusion into private life, the home, correspondence or telecommunications. Its title on fundamental rights and duties carries no article on informática or automated data processing.

The draft electronic transactions bill of 2026 would impose a privacy duty at article 95. Every collector, processor or controller would observe accountability, lawful processing, data minimisation, retention limits, security and protection by design. A breach that harms another would be punishable by a fine of up to 2,000 monetary points of 100 FCFA each, five years' imprisonment or both.

Regional collaboration

In March 2026, 262 women cross-border traders received ECOWAS biometric identity cards in Cacheu under a Guinea-Bissau–Senegal mobility project. The regional customs transit system was not functional in Guinea-Bissau in November 2024, when a roadmap to connect every member state was adopted. In May 2025 the ECOWAS Commission again named it one of two states that had not installed the system.

ECOWAS Supplementary Act A/SA.1/01/10 on Personal Data Protection was adopted in Abuja on 16 February 2010. Article 48 annexes it to the ECOWAS Treaty, and makes its entry into force conditional on publication in each member state's Official Gazette. A Draft Directive Governing Electronic Communications in West Africa, begun in 2019, reached a validation workshop in September 2025. ECOWAS ministers adopted the Digital Sector Development Strategy 2024–2029 at Cotonou on 4 October 2024. In 2021 Guinea-Bissau created a technical committee, chaired by ARN-TIC, to implement the ECOWAS free-roaming regulation.

The ECOWAS Community budget for 2026–2028 carries no separate digital or ICT appropriation. Its only ICT-named line runs at nil from 2024 through the 2028 estimates, against total estimated 2026 income of UA 411,724,082. On 31 August 2026 General Birame Diop took office as President of the ECOWAS Commission, and Amin Amidu Sulemani took Infrastructure, Energy and Digitalisation.

In 2025 the Ministry of Finance described the treasury single account as an innovation of the harmonised UEMOA public-finance framework. Its domestic basis is article 29 of Lei n.º 2/2015, which transposes Directive n° 06/2009/CM/UEMOA.

Sixteen years separate that directive from the national regulation that makes the account operable, the obligation entering the statute book in 2015 and acquiring its implementing regulation only in March 2025.

Standards

In 2017 the WTO Secretariat found that the lack of operational quality control mechanisms hinders the expansion of the country's foreign trade. No later assessment has been found. ISO's member record for Guinea-Bissau shows no technical-committee liaisons of any class. The national standards body is the DSNPQ, a directorate of service inside the Ministère du Commerce, Industrie, Tourisme et Artisanat. It is not an autonomous national standards institute.

In August 2026 the government approved as a national regulation the interoperability framework produced by consultants under the World Bank-financed WARDIP programme. No entry-into-force date has been published. The framework names four critical success factors: process identification, standards compliance, IT-systems collaboration and a legal framework. It cites political instability and uncertainty across successive governments as the reason it needs a formal governance structure. It proposes a central authority with an oversight committee or advisory council that includes the private sector and civil society.

In March 2024 an online ECOWAS expert workshop with the EU's D4D Hub discussed priorities for a regional electronic signature certification policy. It formulated guidelines, with a roadmap still to be defined. In 2021 the regulator recorded its part in the 13th General Assembly of ARCTEL-CPLP, the 10th meeting of Portuguese-language communications authorities and its work with the ITU.

In its market observatory for the second quarter of 2025, ARN found that both mobile operators hold significant market power. It warned that Telecel's residual share of investment could seriously compromise competition.

Public debate and participation in policymaking

From 27 November 2025 the coup leaders ordered radio stations off air and blocked four major social platforms nationwide. A law against false information online followed in May 2026.

Decreto n.º 15/2010 makes public consultation obligatory before eleven named categories of telecoms regulatory decision. They include defining relevant markets, declaring significant market power, and imposing or removing obligations on operators. They also include limits on frequency use-rights, quality-of-service parameters, consumer protection, number portability and universal-service obligations. The terms of specific offers for users with disabilities are covered too. Notice goes in the national press or on the ARN website, and the document is available at the regulator's seat for at least 15 working days. Responses run for at least 20 working days, are accepted by any written means and are published, save identified confidential material. A final document records the ARN's overall view of every response received. Interested parties are defined more widely than those holding a legally protected interest. Urgent measures are excluded, provided the measure is immediate, proportionate and provisional.

In 2021 ARN ran a public consultation on the wholesale and retail telecoms cost model, alongside work on submarine-cable implementation and short emergency numbers. The draft Electronic Transactions Law was put to public consultation in 2026.

A draft access-to-information bill was validated in April 2024, and no statute has been found since. Over the decade to 2023, what government published of its own accord improved, while what a citizen could obtain declined. Media freedom fell 5.1 points over 2014–2023 to 53.7 of 100 in 2023, 29th of 54. Rural representation and participation was among the country's most deteriorated measures over the same period. No duty to consult like the regulator's has been found outside the telecoms sector.

Finance

Financial sustainability

Guinea-Bissau's treasury carries 3.0 per cent of the digital lines in the 2026 budget as appropriated, XOF 25 million of XOF 821.5 million, against 17.6 per cent of XOF 966.5 million in the 2025 budget as proposed; the grant lines are the same in both years, and the fall is on the domestic side.

The 2026 budget gives the head of government's digital transformation project XOF 10 million of domestic money. The project covers accounting software, a government data centre and communications. The 2026 budget gives a revenue and expenditure platform at the territorial administration ministry XOF 5 million. The 2026 budget gives the WARDIP connectivity programme XOF 10 million of counterpart beside XOF 454.6 million of grant. The 2025 proposal had put XOF 20 million of domestic money into the same transformation project. The 2025 proposal put XOF 60 million into a data platform for the public investment programme and XOF 50 million into an e-health project. In 2026 a grant with no domestic counterpart carries the education management information system, at XOF 140 million. In 2026 another grant carries a Sightsavers health digitalisation project, at XOF 201.9 million. Both were funded the same way in 2025. Execution of the 2026 budget has been reported only in-year, to the second quarter.

The World Bank's WARDIP grant of November 2023 came with a US$0.5 million government counterpart and runs to 2028. In March 2026 the backbone it finances was put out to international tender as a public-private partnership to design, build, operate and maintain it. A draft strategic plan for the Universal Access and Service Fund, prepared for ARN in November 2025, records new resources from a higher levy on operators' revenues.

In 2021 the Ministry of Justice renewed its contract with Semlex, which financed the national biometric document production centre in Bissau and the equipment in the regions.

MoUs and other agreements

In June 2026 the IMF completed the eleventh review of its Extended Credit Facility programme with Guinea-Bissau. The programme names the revenue administration platform and a taxpayer census among its reform actions. No memorandum of understanding specific to the digital sector had been found as at September 2026.

New investments

In November 2023 the World Bank approved the Western Africa Regional Digital Integration Program SOP1, a US$266 million concessional programme running to 2028. Guinea-Bissau's 2023 allocation is an IDA grant of SDR 45.7 million, US$60.0 million equivalent. The programme is shared with The Gambia, Guinea and Mauritania. It covers broadband, digital economy governance and digital public services.

In 2025 MIGA held an active political-risk guarantee of US$25.4 million over Sonatel SA's equity investment in Orange Bissau. It runs to 2029, as one tranche of a EUR 433.7 million Orange programme across Guinea and Guinea-Bissau.

UNDP's Mais Digit@l for Guinea-Bissau, run with the Ministry of Digital Economy, covers 2025 to 2030. In August 2026 the African Development Bank launched a Strengthening Tax Administration Capacity Project for six West African countries, Guinea-Bissau among them. It runs on an African Development Fund grant.

In January 2026 Guinea-Bissau launched a customs platform deployed with UNCTAD's technical assistance and funded by the African Development Bank. The Government of Japan financed the technical assistance behind the IMF's enhanced data dissemination system, implemented in July 2024.

Five commitments bear on Guinea-Bissau as at September 2026 — four distinct operations, the World Bank's appearing twice — starting between 2023 and 2026 and ending out to 2030; three name the country as recipient, two are regional. Three of the five are grants, one a concessional loan to the region and one a guarantee, with no equity investment and no commercial borrowing. Connectivity takes three of them and about US$85 million of the roughly US$88 million attributable to the country as at September 2026, the rest going to skills and tax administration. The World Bank leads by a wide margin at US$60.0 million as at September 2026, ahead of MIGA's US$25.4 million and UNDP's roughly US$3 million; the African Development Bank appears only through regional grants shared across several countries.

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

In the second quarter of 2025 effective mobile stations fell to 1,828,708, the regulator ARN reported. This was the third fall in four quarters, from 1,989,605 in the third quarter of 2024. In the same quarter of 2025, 3G users rose 9% to 1,031,923, while 4G users fell 2% to 823,110. Internet subscriptions on 3G and 4G boxes and USB modems fell from 22,295 to 20,456 over the quarter.

In the second quarter of 2025 two operators served the mobile market: Orange Bissau with 1,290,663 subscribers and Telecel Guiné-Bissau with 538,045. Orange took 81% of sector turnover in that quarter of 2025. It supplied 99% of the quarter's 7,181,811,064 FCFA of sector investment in 2025.

As at 21 August 2026 Guinea-Bissau was one of 51 countries with no internet exchange point. The country has no national fibre backbone. Middle-mile capacity runs mainly on microwave links, with an embryonic metropolitan fibre network in Bissau. President Umaro Sissoco Embaló launched the WARDIP programme in Bissau on 2 April 2025. The backbone designed under WARDIP-GB specifies 2,877 km of cable and 56 nodes, and targets more than 99% of the population (2026). A private wholesale operator is to run it under an exclusive licence.

In May 2023 the regulator measured mobile service across the Bijagós and Bolama. In 2023 it found a signal in 81.4% of MTN readings and 73.8% of Orange's. Quality in 2023 was non-existent, very poor or poor in 56.0% and 58.7% of them. Barely half of the data tests completed. 4G networks covered 35.8% of the population in 2024. Internet use reached 32.5% of individuals in 2025. The Ibrahim Index scored internet and computers at 11.5 of 100 for 2023, 46th of 54.

Starlink began commercial service on 19 June 2025, at about XOF 228,000 for hardware and about $63 a month. ARN-TIC had granted it a provisional general authorisation in April 2025. On 14 September 2026 the regulator extended the authorisation to 31 December 2026, pending a definitive regime. Four Digital Public Squares at Farim, Gabú, Buba and Mansoa were given connectivity during 2025.

Data Storage

A feasibility study assessed what a national government cloud would require and prepared terms of reference and tender documents for it (2025).

The national digital strategy plans a phased Centro Nacional de Dados, beginning at small scale. It would end the fragmentation of small processing centres spread across ministries and serve as a test-bed for private-sector projects. The strategy states that its viability depends on reliable electricity, which it places outside its own scope.

A contractor completed a 2,124 square-metre data centre of Tier 3 design for Orange Bissau at Nhacra in February 2025. No date of its entry into service has been found. No top-five cloud provider had an established or announced public cloud region anywhere in West Africa (early 2025). A Tier III colocation data centre positioned to serve the WAEMU bloc broke ground in Abidjan in November 2022. The effect shows in public data. The country's national summary data page is hosted on a national Open Data Platform provided by the African Development Bank.

Energy

In 2023 Guinea-Bissau had 0 MW of installed on-grid capacity. Since 2019 a single rented 34 MW heavy-fuel-oil barge has been the national grid's only supply. In 2023 peak demand was 25 MW and the generation mix 100% heavy fuel oil. The regional OMVG interconnection is intended to replace the rented barge.

In 2023 the average cost of service was US$0.42/kWh, against an average tariff of US$0.38/kWh. In 2023 transmission and distribution losses ran at 35% and bill collection at 80%. EAGB is the state electricity utility, and the Ministry of Energy the sector's policy and regulatory authority.

In 2023 access ran at 58% in the capital and under 5% across the rest of the country. In 2023, 64.3% of the urban population had electricity, against 20.7% of the rural population, a divide of 43.6 percentage points. The absolute deficit was 1.28 million people in 2023. Over 2010-23 the country was among the twenty fastest-electrifying in the world. The Ibrahim Index put access to energy at 16.9 of 100 for 2023, 43rd of 54 and among the country's ten worst indicators.

In January 2024 an EU-funded 500 kWp solar mini-grid on Bolama became operational. A World Bank project document of 2024 plans a US$8.5 million subcomponent for solar mini-grids connecting 1,200 households on the Bijagós islands. The same subcomponent plans individual solar systems for at least 100 health facilities.

The environmental and social management framework for the WARDIP fibre build, dated April 2023, ties the programme to energy and water efficiency, climate risk and e-waste. UNDP found that lack of electricity, connectivity and internet availability were the main challenges to the country's e-justice pilots.

Technical Capacity

In 2023 the World Bank judged the mandate of ITMA, the digital transformation agency, possibly overly ambitious against comparable institutions. It found that mandate overlaps in part with that of ARN, the telecoms regulator. It said the government had expressed a commitment to digital skills, but "little has been done to date". It rated digital capability across the civil service and political bodies as low. In UNDP's measured baseline for the justice sector, almost all surveyed staff had never received digital training at work.

Public Administration scored 26.7 of 100 in 2023, 50th of 54, having fallen 1.5 points over 2014-2023. Effective administration scored 25.8, 49th of 54.

A hydromet peer review of July 2024 found the national meteorological institute has few technical staff with the relevant skills and a low budget for its core activities. Its technological infrastructure is not adapted to digitalisation. It operates no automatic weather stations.

Civil registry officials were trained on digital registration systems during 2025. In 2021 the regulator's technicians took a benchmarking mission to the Nigerian Communications Commission. They also entered a Sida-backed regional training programme.

Cybersecurity

The ITU Global Cybersecurity Index 2024 placed Guinea-Bissau in Tier 5, Building, the lowest of five tiers, for an overall score below 20 of 100. A feasibility study in 2025 assessed what a national CERT and critical information infrastructure protection would require. It prepared terms of reference and tender documents for both.

The 2010 ICT framework law sets the ground rules for ICT networks and services, and constitutes the regulator. In 2024 UNECA found no Electronic Transactions Act and no comprehensive legal regime for personal data protection.

On 22 January 2025 the government presented the results of studies on cybersecurity and undertook to create a national cybersecurity and data protection agency. In July 2025 the Council of Ministers approved a draft National Cybersecurity Strategy, with a data governance policy and an interoperability regulation. The national digital strategy schedules a public campaign on secure and responsible online behaviour from July 2026, repeated to 2029. It covers security, privacy protection and resistance to hate speech and distorted information.

In October 2024 ECOWAS ministers adopted a directive on confidence-building measures in the cyber and ICT field. They urged member states to implement it without delay upon its publication.

DPI

Data Exchange

In July 2025 the Council of Ministers approved a draft regulation on the interoperability of public and private digital systems, alongside draft data-governance and cybersecurity texts. In August 2026 the government approved a national interoperability regulation, announced by the WARDIP programme with no decree number or entry-into-force date. The design behind it is eGIF-GW version 2.0 of 31 July 2024, produced under the World Bank-financed WARDIP programme. It runs over legal, organisational, semantic and technical layers, and specifies GovStack building blocks, PKI with digital signature and an eID. It names ITMA as coordinator and as responsible for the framework's design.

In 2023 the World Bank found government digital platforms mostly siloed. The national digital transformation strategy makes the same finding under objective OE3: platforms built in silos, at different maturity levels, unable to interoperate. A UNDP project aims at progressive integration of the identification systems, starting from the digitised civil registry. UNDP frames SIREC as the foundation for future interoperability with the ID card, the voter register, social protection and the courts.

In March 2023 the reformulated Sistema Integrado de Informação Criminal was inaugurated under the West African Police Information System programme. It exchanges information between police and investigation agencies, and runs at the Polícia Judiciária. It is to extend to the Guarda Nacional, the Polícia de Ordem Pública and the migration services. When the CFE formalises a company, it passes the details electronically to the tax authority, which issues the taxpayer number without a separate application.

Customs went live on ASYCUDAWorld on 26 January 2026, with interfaces to external national systems built in. Its automated valuation module became mandatory on 1 May 2026. On 17 September 2026 an air cargo electronic manifest module, built with the air transport association, went live and gives customs advance cargo data.

Digital Identity and CRVS

In January 2026 an 18th card-production centre opened at Safim; the Ministry of Justice runs civil identification through its Directorate General of Civil Identification. The national identity system depends on UNDP and Swiss Confederation funding, and on Semlex under a revenue-sharing arrangement. UNDP's evaluation identified no dedicated domestic budget line. It reports that the World Bank rates progress on biometric ID as slow and at risk.

The 2025 national digital transformation strategy provides for a technical and legal framework for digital identity, with a matching authentication platform. It calls them an essential tool for every process of digital transformation. It sets 24 months, July 2026 to June 2028, to build them within a foundational identity system tied to the digitised civil registry. Lei n.º 11/2013 makes voter registration permanent, single and compulsory, and issues a manual or biometric voter card against it.

A 2025 World Bank operation carries a prior action on ECOWAS-compliant biometric identity cards. Under it the government planned a campaign to issue them free to all civil servants and pensioners. The Bilhete de Identidade is mandatory from age 15 and requires a birth certificate, so no credential is issued at birth or in early childhood. Guinea-Bissau is one of six ECOWAS states to have fully operationalised the regional biometric identity card for cross-border movement and trade. It is not among the countries financed under the World Bank's WURI identity programme. The ECOWAS Commission handles its part in WURI through its Directorate of Free Movement of Persons and Migration.

About 46 per cent of children under five had their birth registered (2025). Civil registration scored 25.0 of 100 in 2023, 50th of 54, having fallen 12.5 points over the decade. During 2025 Candonga di Justiça took mobile justice and civil registration services to remote communities. On one mission a registry officer called for identity cards to be issued on the islands, to spare islanders long trips.

Digital Payments and Fintech

In July 2026 every institution authorised on the regional instant-payment scheme in Guinea-Bissau was a bank. No mobile-money operator appears for the country, while Benin's includes Moov Money and MTN and Burkina Faso's Orange Money. Its five participants tied with Niger's as the Union's smallest cohort; Senegal had 24 and Côte d'Ivoire 23, of 104. The scheme went live in all eight WAEMU states on 30 September 2025. Orange and MTN both offer mobile money there, each with a partner bank (2022). The central bank's register lists one payment institution in Guinea-Bissau, of thirty-one across the Union.

That single licence is the joint-smallest payment-institution sector in the monetary union, level with Niger's and set against eleven in Senegal, nine in Côte d'Ivoire and three in Togo.

Règlement n° 15/2002/CM/UEMOA sets the Union-wide framework for payment systems. Instruction n° 001-01-2024 binds banks, payment institutions, microfinance and e-money institutions alike. The BCEAO oversees the infrastructures and the means of payment itself. It runs SICA-UEMOA for retail clearing and STAR-UEMOA for gross settlement, with a guarantee fund, intra-day advances and the Treasury as a participant. Opening a bank account requires the national ID card, ECOWAS card or passport.

In May 2024 the Ministries of Finance and Public Administration launched a blockchain platform monitoring bank payment of public salaries and pensions in real time. The public wage bill fell from 84% of tax revenues in 2020 to 50% by October 2024, against a WAEMU criterion of 35%. A Treasury Single Account regulation approved in March 2025 requires all public funds to be held in it, at the BCEAO. Tax can be paid from a phone through a bank or mobile operator, without an account or prior declaration.

Access to banking services scored 4.3 of 100 in 2023, 47th of 54 and the second-worst of 96 indicators. Close to 43 per cent of the population remained outside the formal financial system (2021). Fewer than one in ten respondents to the 2021-22 household survey used mobile banking. An estimated 5 to 15 fintech firms operate, with no national fintech strategy (2026).

Registries

UNDP reported in 2025 that all of Bissau's civil birth registry books had been digitised, with 230,967 records organised. By 2025 more than 7,500 registry books, holding over 1.3 million civil records from five regions, had been catalogued.

SIREC, the integrated citizen registration and statistics system, went live at the 1.ª Conservatória de Bissau on 22-23 January 2025. It is designed as a single database for births, paternity acknowledgements, marriages, deaths and other civil acts, with a unique number for each citizen.

Civil registration runs on the Código de Registo Civil of 1967, carried over at independence. The national vital-statistics yearbook calls it old and ill-adapted, and records a draft revision by Portuguese Cooperation held at the Ministry of Justice. Until 2009 the network was one central conservatória, eight regional delegations and 28 posts across 36 sectors. By 2022, 42 registry posts had opened inside hospitals under a protocol with the health ministry.

The Centro de Formalização de Empresas takes company applications online, its platform counting 7,666 firms created (2026). Women were 7.0% of promoters in 2012 and 13.9% in 2024. The electoral roll held 966,152 registered voters for the November 2025 elections, against 893,618 at the legislative election of 4 June 2023. The electoral commission had adopted a biometric voter card before the 2018 legislative elections. A Social Registry begun in 2015 held 6,200 individuals in 2,250 families at the end of July 2017.

The Lei da Terra of 1998, Lei n.º 5/98, vests land in the State; private use rights need a written contract registered at the Conservatória do Registo Predial. Decreto n.º 6/2018 created the framework for a national land cadastre, its atlas and a Livro de Tombo. Spatial Dimension won the tender for a World Bank-funded Geodata and Mining Cadastre System in April 2015. The postal address format places a four-digit postcode to the left of the locality name (March 2005). Bissau city council began placing street-name plaques and numbering houses in March 2019.

Sectoral management information systems

On 27 August 2026 the health ministry announced a five-year memorandum with Zenysis to extend the NIA, the national health data warehouse, towards a national health control and intelligence room. The NIA, built since a 2023 letter of intent, integrates child health, tuberculosis, malaria, HIV, facility-readiness and population data from several DHIS2 instances and other systems. DHIS2 has carried national health information for over a decade, including routine data, disease surveillance and bednet campaigns. Its tracker programmes serve as registries for maternal and child health and immunisation. Its climate-health tooling runs on a testing instance, and three of four named use cases await funding. About 2,500 community health workers in UNICEF's WASH programme enter data on tablets outside it. UNDP reports strengthening DHIS2 and implementing an eLMIS joining service-delivery and supply-chain data (2025).

PESIDE, a project of the NGO FEC and the education ministry from May 2021 to November 2026, equips and trains the SIGE education data system. It is installing the statistical platform in every Regional Directorate of Education. It works with 2,097 school directors, 215 inspectors, 11 regional statistical technicians and 16 central planning staff.

In 2023 UNDP reported that the Ministry of Justice's ICT director had built SIGDAJ, its justice information and data management system. It was tested on paper records from the Buba tribunal, the criminal register and the Centro de Acesso à Justiça. Cases entered into it and a justice version of DHIS2 gave real-time statistics in place of counts in Microsoft Word.

Five domains have a named management information system — health, education, justice, criminal information and civil registration — and each rests on a donor project, a regional programme or in-house ministry work rather than on an establishing decree or law; the one sector with a founding instrument, land, has no evidence of a running system. The public health institute's yearbook counted 1,747 of 2,073 expected health-centre monthly reports entered for 2022, 82.2%. The platform's developers signed an agreement to support the public health institute in strengthening the national health information system, with new HIV and tuberculosis patient-tracking tools (2026).

Other GovTech and e-Gov

The national digital strategy approved in 2025 provides for a Portal do Cidadão, a single entry point to state services, in the second half of 2025. A mobile platform mirroring it is scheduled for 2026. A Carta de Serviços Públicos was to inventory every state service and its processes between January and September 2025. It is the precondition for re-engineering services against an annual target for services digitised. A whole-of-government SMS notification service is scheduled for the first half of 2026. Every new public service is to be designed digital-first. Free access to services on the gov.gw domain is to be paid for by the operators, as consideration for their licences, or by the Universal Access Fund. The country ranked 170th of 193 on the UN E-Government Development Index in 2024. ITMA, the digital transformation agency, has responsibilities that overlap in part with the telecoms regulator's (2023).

A December 2025 service instruction made Kontaktu, the tax authority's portal for declarations and payments, the only system for issuing taxpayer numbers. Its operating instruction lists fourteen taxes and contributions that still require a prior declaration. Customs replaced ASYCUDA++, in use since the early 1990s, with ASYCUDAWorld across all computerised offices on 26 January 2026. On 8 October 2026 the customs directorate put the migration at more than 80 per cent executed. The Centro de Formalização de Empresas is a one-stop shop for company registration, joining the notary, commercial and taxpayer registration and business licensing. The Judiciary Police runs an online complaint portal.

The Electronic Transactions Law was still a draft out to public consultation by the regulator in May 2026. No statute giving electronic filings and payments to the state their legal effect has been found.

The steering committee of the Integrated Criminal Information System heard in April 2023 that the other forces had sent no staff, the Judicial Police citing a 2011 law as its basis. A second service opened at the Guarda Nacional command in Bissau in August 2024, linking the police forces' criminal data.

Digitalisation

Digitalisation of sub-national government

Bissau city council launched CMB+ on 22 September 2026, a digital modernisation project with a municipal portal for residents' access to council services. No budget or timetable for it has been found. A one-stop digitalised transport licensing counter opened at Gabú in March 2026, the first integrated service counter outside the capital. SIGFIP for public financial management and Kontaktu for tax run at national level (2025). The PESIDE education data system is being implemented at central and regional level, with equipment for the Regional Directorates of Education (2025).

Rural digital data capture

In 2024 Zenysis reported that health data relied on manual, error-prone entry across more than twenty fragmented systems. Civil registration had only begun moving onto the digital SIREC system in 2025, with birth registration below half of children under five. The criminal information system was inaugurated at the national headquarters of the Polícia Judiciária (March 2023). The justice-sector systems are the ministry's own, with a prisoners' database and the Judiciary Police's forum website beside them (2023). Customs computerisation runs at the port, the airport and the offices of São Domingos, Gabú and Bafatá (January 2026).

A vehicle for mobile civil registration in remote areas was handed to the civil registry directorate at SIREC's launch in January 2025. Registry officers were trained on its enrolment kits (2025). Mobile justice and civil registration services reached remote communities during 2025. A registry officer on one mission called for identity cards to be issued on the islands themselves. A GPE-backed nationwide mapping of schools built a database of schools, teachers and students for every region (February 2026). Electricity access outside Bissau was under 5% in 2023. No national backbone links the main cities, and international connectivity is confined to the coast (2025).

The Rural Economy fell 22.4 points over 2014-2023 to 26.0 of 100 in 2023, 43rd of 54. It was the largest decline of any of the index's 16 sub-categories. Within it, rural land and water access fell 49.0 points to 25.9 of 100, the largest ten-year fall of any of the country's 96 indicators. Rural representation and participation fell 13.3 points to 24.5 of 100.

Technology

AI

On 14 July 2026 the Ministry of Public Health spoke at the WHO global conference on AI for health in Lisbon. It said the country is at an initial stage of AI adoption in health. It said the country is building the basis for responsible implementation, and named digital transformation a strategic priority for universal health coverage. A five-year memorandum between the health ministry and its health-data platform's developer was announced on 27 August 2026. It commits them to explore natural-language AI querying of the platform.

The IMF reported in October 2024 that the blockchain wage-bill and pensions platform was not integrated with any AI model. The IMF frames its payment data as training material for future models able to flag wrongful or misdirected payments. The World Bank's Digital Economy Diagnostic calls the digital sector embryonic (2022).

The ECOWAS digital roadmap of January 2026 commits the bloc to a regional AI governance framework for West Africa. In August 2026 the World Bank-financed WARDIP project backed an AI-themed competition for 35 young people in four teams, with three months of mentoring.

ICT Industry

Guinea-Bissau ranked last in Africa for economic diversification in 2023, 54th of 54, with 16.2 of 100 after a fall of 2.1 points over the decade.

In June 2025 the regulator ARN found both operators to hold significant market power. It warned that Telecel's residual share of investment could seriously compromise competition. Orange Bissau accounted for the large majority of subscribers, sector turnover and capital spending in the same quarter. No private-sector provider of meteorological observations or data services operates in the country, leaving the state service as the whole of supply (July 2024). Commercial eKYC vendors offer electronic verification against the national ID by matching a document photo against a selfie.

In 2017 the WTO Secretariat found the expansion of foreign trade hindered by the lack of operational quality control mechanisms and a weak industrial fabric. The national standards body, DSNPQ, is a directorate of service inside the Ministry of Commerce, Industry, Tourism and Handicrafts. The national digital transformation strategy of 2025 provides for a phased Centro Nacional de Dados. It would consolidate the small processing centres scattered across ministries and serve as a test-bed for private-sector projects. The strategy makes its viability conditional on reliable electricity, which it places outside its own scope.

A 2026 survey put the fintech population at an estimated 5 to 15 firms and found no standalone national fintech strategy.

Innovation ecosystem

Kau Criar, the country's innovation hub, came out of Hack4SDGs and UNDP's PALOP Innovation Journey from 2020, with InnovaLab and the World Bank. It took shape in 2021 under UNDP's economic resilience work. A taskforce of AIGB, InnovaLab, CIDE, Impact Hub Bissau, the UNDP Youth Sounding Board and Youth Conekt steered it (2023). The hub occupies the former DECOMEL building in Bissau, chosen over three other sites. It offers hot desks, meeting rooms and capacity building under Impact Hub Bissau's temporary management.

InnovaLab, a think tank offering investment, incubation, acceleration, capacity building and consultancy, has worked in Guinea-Bissau for more than ten years (2026).

UNDP's five-country PALOP E-voluir journey carried three ventures towards an MVP pitch to jurors on 27-28 May 2021. Its technical partner was Fábrica de Startups Brasil and its strategic partner Djassi Africa. The ventures were Djemberém, a business incubator; Ebantaba de Conhecimento, in e-education; and ABIPTOM, a digital information platform for farmers. Ten further weeks of acceleration and a pitch to prospective investors were to follow. The initiative is built to strengthen a Lusophone network of innovation and entrepreneurship.

In October 2024 the IMF described the wage-bill platform as an early use of blockchain to improve government operations in sub-Saharan Africa.

A digital-economy entrepreneurship fund backed by the WARDIP programme selected its first cohort of 30 projects in March 2026. An operator's digital centre launched a structured incubation and acceleration programme in April 2026.

Capacity

Literacy

32% of primary teachers could not answer the Year 2 Portuguese paper set for their pupils, and 54% the mathematics paper, the education ministry's sector plan reported in 2017. By Year 5, the 2017 plan reported, 95% of teachers did not master the Portuguese they teach and 98% the mathematics. Year 2 pupils master less than half of what the programme requires, and Year 5 pupils about a third. Results vary by a factor of up to thirty depending on which school a child attends. Secondary education has no uniform curriculum. The sector plan calls that absence a graver problem than the level’s high drop-out.

Over 2014–2023 completion rose 17.2 points to 30.8 of 100 in the Ibrahim Index. Education quality fell 16.6 points to 16.7 of 100 in 2023, 47th of 54. It is among the country’s ten worst-scoring and ten most deteriorated indicators. Upper-secondary completion was 11.7% for girls and 15.8% for boys in 2022. Education took 10.4% of the effective budget in 2023, against a 20% target.

Decreto n.º 10/2025 adopted the Estratégia Nacional para a Transformação Digital in March 2025, with digital literacy as one of its ten pillars. Its objective OE5 commits the state to raise the population’s digital literacy and to train the specialised technical staff the public sector will need. Its interventions include a compulsory introductory ICT subject in every secondary and higher-education course and computer-literacy content for schools and adult training. They also include repeated campaigns on secure and responsible online behaviour and free public wifi squares, framed as an instrument of literacy as well as connectivity. In 2023 the World Bank judged the civil service low in digital skills. UNDP records that several hundred girls were enrolled in a digital-literacy programme in 2025.

The digital-literacy half of OE5 comprises six numbered interventions with scheduled windows running from July 2025 to December 2029; what has been delivered against them so far was financed and run by a donor, not by the ministry that owns them.

Training and skills

In March 2023 a UNDP survey found that 92% of justice-sector staff had never received any digital training in their workplace. The outcome reported for the training that followed was self-reported: staff rated their own digital skills and work-process efficiency higher. In 2023 the World Bank found digital skills low in the civil service and political bodies, and little done on the government’s stated commitment to them. The 2025 digital strategy’s commitment to train specialised technical staff for the public sector postdates that assessment.

In January 2026 365 customs officers, licensed agents, shipping agents and others had been trained over five months for the ASYCUDAWorld rollout. 63 civil registry officials were trained on digital registration systems in 2025. A committee set up by joint decree in May 2026 supervises an operator-government programme to train 1,000 young people a year for four years. The programme covers programming, data analysis and cloud computing. In 2021 the regulator ARN entered WATRA’s Sida-backed SPIDER training programme and sent five technicians on a benchmarking mission to the Nigerian Communications Commission. The regulator’s internet-connected multifunctional centres in three Bissau secondary schools and one in Buba have operated since February 2015. 26 regional planners and civil-society staff were trained on the public plans-and-projects information system in September 2026. In August 2026 the World Bank-financed WARDIP project backed an AI-themed competition for a few dozen young people through the third Orange Summer Challenge, with three months of mentoring.

The education ministry’s 2017 sector plan found teachers’ subject competence below the level being taught. In 2022 the ILO found that NEET rates do not fall uniformly as educational attainment rises. Young adults with upper-secondary education are at least as likely to be NEET as those with only basic education, if not more so.

Research institutions

Universidade Lusófona da Guiné-Bissau runs a Licenciatura em Engenharia Informática of eight semesters. The Ministry of Education accredited it under Decreto-Lei n.º 1/2008.

Inclusion

Access to services

Cash transfer programmes reached around 66,000 people in 2021, 7.6 per cent of the population in poverty, and under 7,000 in 2022, or 0.7 per cent. A National Social Registry is still being established, under a World Bank project approved in January 2025.

In 2021 close to 43 per cent of Bissau-Guineans remained outside the formal financial system. Access to banking services was one of the country’s worst-scoring governance indicators in 2023. In 2023 informal employment accounted for over 90 per cent of the active workforce.

Equal access to public services scored 30.9 of 100 in 2023, 35th of 54 and 1.5 points below its 2014 level.

ENTD.GW, adopted in March 2025, includes Intervenção 13: operational norms safeguarding full access to digital public services for people with special needs. It was scheduled for January to September 2025. The strategy also provides for a Portal do Cidadão as a single point of entry to state services. Guinea-Bissau is bound by the disability convention but has not accepted the inquiry procedure by which a failure of its obligations could be examined.

Decreto n.º 7/2018 records the refugees it covers as 98 per cent Senegalese from Casamance. Many of them had lived in Guinea-Bissau for more than twenty-five years. The decree definitively approves their local integration as naturalised citizens. UNHCR began registering the nearly 10,000 Senegalese refugees for national identity cards in 2018, cards that also open the way to a passport.

Digital divides

In May 2023 the regulator’s field measurement in the Bijagós archipelago and Bolama found no mobile signal in 18.6% of MTN’s readings and 26.2% of Orange’s. Over a third of attempted calls failed or dropped, and about half of data tests could not complete. In 2025 the country had no national backbone linking the main cities. Electricity was concentrated almost entirely in the capital in 2023.

The NEET rate for 15–29 year olds was 17.0 per cent in 2022, significantly down on 2019. Over the same period the gaps between young people with and without disabilities widened. Young men with disabilities were out of employment, education or training at around double the rate of those without in 2022.

ENTD.GW’s Intervenção 15 commits the country to free citizen access to every digital public service on the gov.gw domain between July 2025 and March 2026. The cost is to be negotiated with the telecom operators as consideration for their licences, or borne by the Universal Access Fund. In 2022 more than three in five workers were in agriculture.

Data

National statistics

The fourth population and housing census went to the field in June 2026. Its preliminary results, released on 23 July 2026 as the country’s first fully digital census, put the population at 2,191,202. The capacity of the statistical system scored 21.3 of 100 in 2023, 52nd of 54. That index has no data for Guinea-Bissau on 18 of its 96 indicators. Data sources was the weakest of the five pillars of the World Bank’s Statistical Performance Indicators in 2023, under 20 per cent. In the twenty years to 2023 the country held a population census but no agricultural or business census. It held no agricultural or business survey in the ten years to 2023.

The 2007 statistics framework law names in its preamble the failure of the Conselho Nacional de Informação Estatística, created in 1991, which never functioned. The same preamble identifies excessive decentralisation, with no coordinating function and a proliferation of ministerial statistical services. The successor body, the Conselho Superior da Estatística, was recorded as non-functional in 2018.

The first statistics development strategy, ENDE-1 for 2015–2017, was executed at 24.13 per cent. Its successor blamed a human-resources deficit, insufficient state financing and political instability. That successor, validated in October 2018, programmed a population census and an agriculture and livestock census for 2019. Its first operational objective was a statistics decree-law approved in Council of Ministers in September 2017 and unpromulgated a year later. The statistics law’s regulation and the institute’s organic statute were promulgated in March 2023.

In 2018 the strategy described sectoral statistical production outside education, fisheries, health and agriculture as “practically non-existent”. The education ministry’s last official statistical yearbook covers 2014–2015, with only partial data released since. The regulator’s 2025 market observatory computes penetration against a World Bank 2021 population figure projected at 2.2 per cent a year. In 2017 the WTO Secretariat recorded that the economic indicators are no more than estimates, given an informal sector the authorities put at 70 per cent of the economy. INE has since published a vital-statistics yearbook covering 2018–2022.

Open data

Disclosure of public records rose 12.5 points over 2014–2023 to 25.0 of 100, 38th of 54. Accessibility of public records, whether citizens can obtain government records and data, fell 6.0 points to 6.7 of 100 in 2023, 50th of 54. It is among the country’s ten worst and ten most deteriorated indicators. In October 2024 Guinea-Bissau had no access-to-information law in force.

On 8 July 2024 Guinea-Bissau launched its National Summary Data Page under the IMF’s enhanced General Data Dissemination System. At launch it published national accounts and prices, government operations and debt, the monetary and financial sector and the external sector. It publishes in human-readable and machine-readable form, with data from INE, the Ministry of Finance and the regional central bank BCEAO. The page is hosted on a national Open Data Platform provided by the African Development Bank, and participation in the standard is voluntary.

In September 2026, 26 regional planners and civil-society staff were trained on the Plans and Projects Information System. It lets citizens consult regional development plans, ongoing projects, the bodies responsible and the sums involved. Entry is reserved to regional planners and authorised civil-society staff.

The Assembleia Nacional Popular’s Dados Abertos page carries a stated last-modification date of 1 April 2015. It exposes four JSON datasets over an API: institutional, ombudsman, legislative-process and transparency data. An endpoint serves all published site content in the same format. It is built on Interlegis and takes its definition of open data from Brazil’s federal portal and the Open Knowledge Foundation.

In the 2024 Open Data Inventory government finance scored 7.0 out of 10, balance of payments 6.5 and price statistics 5.5. These are the only three categories above the midpoint. Education and health facilities, at 1.5 and 2.5, are the country’s two lowest assessed categories.

Use of satellite data

Between 21 and 25 August 2026 the national meteorological institute installed and accepted a CLIMSA/PUMA2025 receiving station. The African Union supplied it with European Union support under the ClimSA programme. It takes Meteosat Third Generation data from EUMETSAT in near real time, at intervals of up to 10 minutes and 1 km resolution.

A peer review in July 2024 found that the institute operated no automatic weather stations. Very few of its meteorological, climatological and agrometeorological stations were fully operational. It rated the institute level 1 overall, the bottom of the Country Hydromet Diagnostics scale, with four elements at level 2. It found technical infrastructure not adapted to digitalisation. No private-sector provider of meteorological observations or data services operates in the country. INM-GB is a semi-autonomous body under the Ministry of Transport, Communications and Digital Economy, which holds the digital portfolio. It has been a World Meteorological Organization member since 13 January 1978. FAO’s Global Information and Early Warning System publishes satellite-derived agricultural indicators for the country, from METOP and ECMWF inputs over the latest 36 months. Three seasonal indicators cover two crop seasons: the Agricultural Stress Index, Drought Intensity and the Weighted Mean Vegetation Health Index. Five non-seasonal ones include NDVI anomaly, vegetation and health condition indices and estimated precipitation, over cropland and grassland masks. FAO states that its seasonal indicators may diverge from local conditions because of limits in its crop phenology and crop mask data. Climate-health tooling drawing on global temperature, humidity, heat-stress and precipitation data sat on a DHIS2 testing instance in July 2026.

Geopolitics

US / hyperscaler activities

Starlink began commercial service in Guinea-Bissau on 19 June 2025. It was Starlink’s seventh West African and twentieth continental market. At launch hardware cost about XOF 228,000, roughly $400, and service about $63 a month.

ARN-TIC granted Starlink Guinea-Bissau a general provisional authorisation on 10 April 2025. It followed a provisional licence issued in December 2024. On 14 September 2026 the regulator extended the authorisation to the end of the year.

China activities

China handed over the ECOWAS Headquarters Building it had built as aid in Abuja on 28 April 2026. Beijing presented it as an outcome under the Forum on China-Africa Cooperation.

EU activities

A civil registration system launched at the first conservatória in Bissau in January 2025 was financed by the European Union. It was built with Portuguese public bodies and the Universidade de Aveiro under the GESTDOC project. PESIDE, a project running to November 2026, is equipping the education ministry’s statistical platform and training and advising its staff. The European Union finances PESIDE.

The EU’s Multiannual Indicative Programme 2021–2027 for Guinea-Bissau sets three priority areas: human development, a green and inclusive economy, and good governance and stability. Digital is not among them. The same document records that development partners had not achieved joint programming in the country.

In March 2024 ECOWAS held a certified electronic signature workshop with the EU Digital for Development Hub. The EU delegation framed it under the Global Gateway strategy and the EUR 150 billion package agreed at the February 2022 EU-AU Summit. The workshop was named a first step towards a harmonised regional digital environment.

Gulf/UAE activities

No Gulf or Emirati involvement in Guinea-Bissau's digital sector had been recorded as at September 2026.

India activities

No Indian state or commercial involvement in Guinea-Bissau's digital sector had been recorded as at September 2026.

Digital sovereignty

UNDP reports that construction of a National Data Center was launched in 2025. In 2022 the World Bank had found no government data centre and no shared services. In March 2024 the prime minister said the building for ITMA, the administration’s modernisation agency, would house the country’s data centre. Japan financed the building with EUR 1.93 million through UNDP, the prime minister said in 2024.

The Council of Ministers approved a draft data governance policy in July 2025. The WARDIP-financed interoperability framework of July 2024 names data sovereignty, residence and localisation among its principles. It sets avoiding dependence on suppliers among its aims. No instrument fixing where state data is held, or under whose law it is processed, had been found in force as at September 2026.

Semlex has supplied the national system for managing and issuing biometric identity cards since 2006. It financed the national production centre in Bissau. No record has been found of whether the state holds the identity system’s source code and operating skills, or could exit the Semlex contract. The wage-bill blockchain platform was designed over four years with the IMF, with Ernst & Young as technological advisor.
