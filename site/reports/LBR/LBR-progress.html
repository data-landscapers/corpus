Of 71 systems and instruments on this place's ledger, 40 changed position between 2025-08-01 and 2026-08-26, 8 did not, 14 carry no stated baseline, and 9 are Not held at both ends.

Shape check, run before the comparison: 97 sources for this place in the window — 22 in the earlier half (2025-08 to 2026-02), 75 in the later (2026-03 to 2026-08). The earlier half of the window is thin: this is a shorter comparison wearing a longer label, and the movement below rests mostly on the later half.

Movement values. Advanced — a system entered service, a stage was completed or an instrument was made. Stalled — a stated target passed without delivery. Regressed — an instrument was withdrawn or neutralised, or a measured position worsened. Closed — the programme ended. No change — the position at both ends is the same. Baseline not held — the base carries no position at the start of the period, so no movement can be stated. A value may carry a qualifying clause after a comma, as in Advanced, slipped.

Summary of the period

The clearest movement of the twelve months to 9 August 2026 was in payments, where an interoperable instant payment system went from not existing to running across both mobile-money networks with a published fee schedule. The clearest regression was in the system the state depends on most: national identity enrolment stopped, with about 640,000 prior registrants locked out over a US$1.7m unpaid balance, and the remedy adopted was to concede the register to a foreign operator. Around both, the state built coordination machinery — a whole-of-government digital office, a five-year strategy, a reformed licensing regime — while the data-protection Bill stayed where it was a year ago.

The data-protection position is identical at both ends of the window: the Personal Information Act was before a House committee in June 2025 and remains before the legislature, with no data-protection authority in existence, over a period in which the state contracted a foreign firm to hold the population's biometric records. What appeared instead was sectoral enforcement: the first privacy penalty on record, LRD 4m against Orange Liberia in June 2026, imposed under telecom regulation.

Policy was made and, in one case, unmade. The ministry published a five-year digital strategy in February 2026 to replace a framework already past its period, and the regulator announced a licensing overhaul in July 2026 that is not yet published as instruments. In the other direction, the African Development Bank's new country strategy cut its sectors from four to three and left no digital lending line.

Regional standing was recovered where domestic transparency was not. Liberia's African Telecommunications Union vote was restored on a US$361,151.36 arrears waiver, while the regulator's own revenue went from unpublished to expressly withheld.

Redundancy moved from absent to agreed but not built. At the start of the period international capacity rested solely on the Monrovia landing; by June 2026 there was a regional agreement to commercialise spare fibre on the CLSG grid, and by July a stated second landing at Buchanan and a national data centre, neither with a partner, cost or date. 5G moved the same way, from nothing on record to plans with investors still being engaged.

Two positions worsened. The traffic-monitoring concession went from operating under a legislature-ratified agreement to suspended, under renegotiation and the subject of a corruption complaint, leaving the state without its own read on operator traffic. And the market lost a licensee: Starcell's licence and spectrum were revoked after six years without operating, which removes a dormant competitor rather than adding one.

Cybersecurity gained an investigation and no strategy. The House ordered a probe into an alleged breach of about 2m citizens' data in February 2026; no findings have been published.

Payments is the section that moved. The instant payment system launched on 16 December 2025 in 73 business days, ending the separation of the two mobile-money networks, and its fee schedule was published in August 2026. A national switch remains provided for and undated.

Identity moved backwards and then sideways. Enrolment stood at about 740,000 people, under 15% of the population, at the start of the period; by March 2026 issuance had stopped and about 640,000 registrants were locked out of a system whose builder was owed US$1.7m, with a US$2m World Bank tranche on hold. The resolution was structural rather than financial: a concession under which OSD International finances enrolment of all about 5.3m people in 18 months, operates the system and transfers it, still unratified and with terms unpublished. No updated enrolment figure has been issued.

Government systems gained a coordinator and two platforms. Executive Order 163 created the whole-of-government digital office in April 2026; the Civil Service Agency's purchasing platform went live in December 2025; and e-procurement went from a February 2025 launch to more than 1,200 registered vendors and an export to Sierra Leone, still without award data.

Artificial intelligence went from absent to acknowledged. It was not a stated government agenda item at the start of the window; by July 2026 the President had accepted a youth council proposal in principle with no instrument attached, and the first working system had launched from the private sector rather than the state: a legal chatbot over 165 years of Supreme Court opinions.

The technology sector's other movement was a memorandum: the port authority's non-exclusive smart-ports agreement with HAROPA PORT, which commits no money and names no system.
