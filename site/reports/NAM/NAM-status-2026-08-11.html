Status values. Implemented — in operation or in force. Piloting — running with a limited user group or in a controlled environment. In development — build or drafting under way, not yet operating. Planned — announced or provided for, no build or draft on record. Enacted — an instrument passed into law; pair with a qualifying clause for its in-force date. Under review — a law or policy currently being reconsidered. Discontinued — closed or superseded. Not held — the base carries no reliable statement of status; these are the gaps to fill and are listed again at the end.

Summary of position

Namibia is issuing an electronic identity card in September 2026 into a country with no data protection statute. The card is chipped, carries biometric and biographical data and a digital identity component for remote authentication, and is free for first-time applicants, renewals and amendments (ministry account). The Data Protection Bill has been in drafting for about twelve years, was announced for tabling in September 2025, and had not been tabled at 6 August 2026 (tabling announcement).

The statutory basis for the card is the Civil Registration and Identification Act 13 of 2024 (the Act), which officials read as permitting institutions to accept either the physical card or its digital version with disclosure conditional on the holder's consent. Existing identity documents stay valid until withdrawn by a gazette notice that has not been issued. An Access to Information Act was gazetted on 28 December 2022 and has not been brought into force (the Act).

The regulator refused a satellite operator's licence twice inside the window, on ownership, national security and compliance grounds, and dismissed 624 reconsideration requests (regulator account). The regulator's published sector and market series stops at 2023 data, so every later connectivity figure in this report is the operator's, the ministry's or an international body's.

The 51% Namibian ownership requirement is the operative instrument in this section. It was applied to refuse a satellite operator's combined service and spectrum licence on 23 March 2026, on three of six statutory criteria — ownership and control, national defence and public security, and compliance history — and the regulator held that wholly foreign ownership raises questions of jurisdiction, enforceability of compliance obligations and effective oversight (refusal account). Of 624 reconsideration requests, 622 failed the legal threshold and two were refused on merits.

Networks were built by others. Commercial fifth-generation service launched at the end of August 2025 in Windhoek, Swakopmund and Walvis Bay (cabinet account); the country's first privately owned mobile network launched on 2 September 2025 with N$600m invested in the preceding year (launch account); and 25 towers were inaugurated across nine regions at N$40m in August 2025 (tower account). The regulator's own market series puts fourth-generation population coverage at 85% in 2022, with six of Namibia's 14 regions below 80% and Kunene lowest at 41% (regulator series); the 2023 edition puts coverage at 88.4% and Namibia's African 1GB-affordability ranking at 8th, up from 46th (regulator summary). CRAN's 2024 determination names Telecom Namibia dominant in all four defined markets, with NamPower in national data transmission and MTC in wireless end-user access (determination).

Cyber incidents are published quarterly. The response team recorded 513,921 vulnerabilities and 161,547 events in April to June 2026, up 39.8% and 56.7% on the prior quarter, a quarter after incident management guidelines were issued (regulator account). These are detections on the regulator's own sensors, not an incidence rate. NAM-CSIRT, launched 14 April 2025, states it completed Phase 3 of its implementation plan in 2025/26 and cannot fully execute its mandate for want of a cybersecurity law (landscape report).

The identity card is the country's largest live programme and it has not issued a card yet. Rollout is set for September 2026, first-time applicants receive a randomly generated number with the legacy number retained on the reverse (ministry account), and the card is valid for 10 years against the indefinite lifespan of the document it replaces, with a first duplicate at N$150 and a second at N$300 (ministry account). Regional outreach ran through Omaheke from 27 July to 5 August with a development agency and the regulator (outreach account).

The document backlog beneath it is measured. Between 1 April 2025 and 30 June 2026 the ministry printed 166,237 identity documents and 125,999 were collected, leaving 40,238 uncollected (ministry figures) — about a quarter of the print run. A school-based registration drive had reached 24 of 55 target schools by 23 July 2026.

Payments moved from build to live. The instant payment solution became operational in June 2026 with three participating institutions, on a phased national rollout with government-to-person social grants the first use case (central bank account). The quick-response standard is finalised and the open banking specifications complete, both known only through press reporting of an annual report the wiki does not hold (report account).

Windhoek's council adopted a People-Centred Smart City Strategy 2026-2036 on 15 June 2026, naming commercialisation of the city's own fibre-optic network as the digital backbone; no funding requirement, implementation timetable or institutional owner is published (council account). A National Digital Health Policy 2026-2036 was launched on 6 February 2026, succeeding the electronic health strategy whose period expired at the end of 2025 (regional office account).

Namibia's statistics system rests on parallel plans. The National Strategy for the Development of Statistics 2023/24-2026/27 costs ten sector plans at N$58,995,000 over four years, drafted with African Development Bank, Paris21, UNDP and UNFPA support (the strategy); the agency's own, separate Strategic Plan 2022/23-2026/27 records that funding was adequate only until about 2016 and that the census could not proceed in August 2021 or 2022 for want of funds (the plan). The agency also runs a NADA microdata catalogue of 16 studies dated 1992-2023, agency-run data dissemination rather than a government-wide open data policy (data portals).

Two bills carry the section and neither has been tabled. The Data Protection Bill was announced for tabling in September 2025, about twelve years after drafting began (tabling announcement), and was still described as being finalised on 14 July 2026 (ministerial account). The Cybercrime Bill is named as being finalised alongside it, with no draft published and no tabling date. In the interim, law enforcement relies on the Electronic Transactions Act 2019, the Communications Act and the penal code.

The 2020 draft carries a Part on the rights of the data subject at sections 8 to 14; the version the ministry published for comment in March 2022 does not, and access and correction survive only as controller obligations (the 2020 draft).

The March 2022 draft was published for a five-week public consultation closing 30 November 2022 (consultation notice). The ACTION Namibia Coalition submitted on the closing date (submission), and the Institute for Public Policy Research published the same argument on 14 December 2022, calling the Bill "not fit for purpose" and its Supervisory Authority's independence provisions in need of substantial redrafting (IPPR paper). No ministry-published respondent list is held.

A Corporate Laws Bill was at Draft 3 and out for public comment in October 2025; its clause 114 would require every company to keep a register of beneficial owners in Namibia recording each owner's date of birth and identity-document number (the draft). The European Union's country programme for 2021-2027 sets no digital priority area: digitalisation is a cross-cutting priority, and the 2025-2027 allocation fixed at the mid-term review is EUR 26,000,000, with nil for foundational skills education (the programme).

Regulations for appointing an Information Commissioner and Deputy were gazetted 9 December 2024, signed 15 November 2024, setting five-year Selection Committee terms and a five-member quorum — machinery made under the Access to Information Act 8 of 2022 before that Act itself is in force (the regulations).

The national artificial-intelligence strategy is in consultation and the legislation is sequenced behind it. The ministry stated on 3 August 2026 that consultations had begun under the research council and that government has prioritised a strategy before introducing legislation (ministerial account). The council's own chief executive and colleagues wrote in February 2026 that Namibia lacks a national strategy and lacks comprehensive legislation for data protection and cybersecurity (council paper).

The readiness work is done and the compute is not. A readiness assessment was completed in 2025 with an international body, and the council's working group runs the strategy process; a national institute recommended by a 2022 task force is proposed and not established, and no national high-performance computing facility exists in the base. A smaller, separate object does: a five-node PARAM cluster specified for NUST under the India-Namibia Centre of Excellence in Information Technology, with no operator, cost or opening date published, and a university research cluster rather than a national facility (specification).

The technology sector's one first-of-its-kind object is the satellite data-receiving ground station handed over by China on 12 February 2026, stated to be wholly Namibia-run by fourteen trained technicians (handover account). Cabinet approved drafting a space bill in 2025 and no text has been published.

Namibia's opening move with the Gulf is a proposal, not an instrument: at a Namibia-UAE Business Forum on 18 February 2026, the foreign minister proposed a Bilateral Economic Commission with technology transfer in its remit, the UAE side raised investment protection and double-taxation agreements, and no Gulf-state digital, data-centre or artificial-intelligence agreement is published (forum account).

Financial inclusion rose from 78% in 2017 to 86% in 2025 on the central bank's own account, which also named continued cash reliance, limited rural access, informal-sector barriers and the digital divide as persistent constraints (central bank account). A second held source describes the same 78% as current in July 2025, and the two accounts date it eight years apart.

Connectivity commitments are stated as targets rather than delivery. The ministry set 100% telecommunications coverage by 2030 in June 2025, against about 360,000 people outside fourth-generation coverage at February 2024, and reported 339 schools and 50 health centres without adequate connectivity (ministry account). Schools and clinics under towers built in the previous two years receive free internet for seven years, with no list or count of covered sites published (ministerial statement).

The universal service fund launched on 19 March 2025 with N$145,000,000 of government seed funding over three years, and priority goes to the five regions where fourth-generation coverage is below 80% — Kavango West, Kavango East, Kunene, Ohangwena and Oshikoto — with 16 schools and four clinics selected for uncapped Wi-Fi (launch account). No disbursement or balance figure has been published since; the recorded output is 100 handsets distributed to learners in Kunene in February 2026 (fund account).

Information and Communication is a compulsory, non-promotional but assessable subject for Grade 4 to 12: the Grade 12 syllabus was published in 2018 for 2021 implementation, and the Grade 8 and 9 syllabus followed in December 2024 for phased implementation in 2025 and 2026 (grade 8 & 9 syllabus). No standalone national digital-literacy strategy or programme exists outside this school curriculum.
