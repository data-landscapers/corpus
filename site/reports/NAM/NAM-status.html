Status report

This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

Namibia has no dedicated government entity responsible for data governance or data management, and no data governance strategy or policy (2025).

The Office of the Prime Minister's Digital Government Strategic Roadmap 2024-2026, "Digital First Services for All", carries the connectivity programme alongside the National Digital Strategy. The sixth National Development Plan targets internet penetration of 90 percent by 2030. The Ministry of Information, Communication and Technology's Strategic Plan 2025-2030 sets the digital agenda, including a national data centre and measures to close the urban-rural divide. Cabinet approved a revised National Broadband Policy and implementation plan in October 2026, aimed at rural-urban gaps in access, usage, affordability and skills. The revised text has not been published. A National Digital Health Policy 2026-2036 was launched in February 2026. It is a ten-year framework covering electronic health records, interoperable infrastructure and wider access for underserved communities. The headline commitments are to raise access to digital technology to 70 percent and to digitise 100 percent of government services by 2029/30.

The World Bank's 2025 assessment finds no GovTech or digital transformation strategy, a whole-of-government approach only planned or in draft, and nothing published on progress or spending. It finds no open source policy for the public sector and no national strategy on disruptive or innovative technologies. A GovTech entity and a coordinating body exist, but nobody monitors or reports digital spending across government and the GovTech institution publishes no annual progress report.

Of the 96 indicators in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, Namibia improved on 35 between 2014 and 2023 and declined on 54. Public administration was among the largest contributors to the decline.

In July 2026 licensing and supervision of payment service providers passed to the Bank of Namibia under a new Payments Association of Namibia framework. A national cybersecurity strategy and awareness-raising plan has been in place since 2022. Cabinet approved proposals in November 2025 for a government-sector incident response team and security operations centres, with the Office of the Prime Minister coordinating.

Legislation and regulation

The Access to Information Act 8 of 2022 was not in force as at May 2026. It would give a right to request information from public bodies. A Cybercrime Bill was still in draft in January 2026 and went as a final draft to a ministry validation workshop in April 2026. It was still being finalised in July 2026. Until it passes, enforcement rests on the Electronic Transactions Act, the Communications Act and the Penal Code. The bill would designate and protect critical information infrastructure, which no law yet provides for.

The Civil Registration and Identification Act 13 of 2024 was gazetted in December 2024 and had not been brought into operation as at December 2025. It would supply the legal basis for the electronic identity card, for the biometrics taken on application, and for authenticating the holder against them. The Identification Act 21 of 1996 remains the governing law for the population register. In November 2026 Cabinet put the electronic identity card's launch back until regulations under the 2024 Act are made. The electronic-signature provisions of the Electronic Transactions Act 4 of 2019, section 20 and Chapter 5, came into operation on 15 June 2026.

The Payment System Management Act 14 of 2023 gives the Bank of Namibia power to regulate and oversee the national payment system. The bank also approves its rules and issues directives. The Financial Institutions and Markets Act and a new NAMFISA Act came into operation on 1 May 2026. The Electronic Transactions Act empowers the minister to issue guidelines on system interoperability, but its Chapter 4 has never been commenced. No Namibian law requires one part of government to share data with another. No statute addresses artificial intelligence. The ICT minister told Parliament in September 2025 that an AI Act was being developed. In November 2026 the communications regulator put a sandbox framework for testing AI, satellite broadband, blockchain and digital-identity services to stakeholder consultation.

Regulations under the Communications Act requiring SIM registration came into force on 1 April 2024. They require communications data to be kept for up to five years.

Data protection

Namibia has no comprehensive data protection legislation and no data protection authority (2026). The Data Protection Bill that would create both was still being finalised in July 2026. Drafting began about twelve years before August 2025, when the bill was reported to be returning to Parliament. There is no entity monitoring compliance with data protection rules, and nothing is published on privacy complaints (2025). Namibia sets no data localisation requirement and does not regulate cross-border personal data flows (2026).

The 2020 draft would establish a Data Protection Supervisory Authority and set obligations on data controllers and processors. Its data-subject rights part, from access and erasure to a right not to be subject to automated decision-making, does not survive into the text published for comment in 2022. The uncommenced Civil Registration and Identification Act 13 of 2024 would give every data subject access to their own record. It would require a warrant or court order for law-enforcement access. It would also require a signed agreement before civil-register data goes to an organ of state, an access register and an Appeals Tribunal.

SIM registration and five-year retention of communications data have been in force since 1 April 2024. Biometric enrolment for the national identity system and SIM registration against a national ID or passport both run without a data protection statute. The Office of the Prime Minister names internal misuse of e-ID data as a larger risk than external attack. In December 2024 Telecom Namibia lost 626GB of data to the Hunters International ransomware group. The data included records of eight ministries, five regional councils and ten municipalities. In October 2025 the Namibia Students Financial Assistance Fund published the personal details of more than 7,000 students on its own website. The head of the national incident response team then called for urgent finalisation of the data protection law. Only national payment system participants must report a successful cyberattack, to the central bank within 24 hours. In refusing Starlink a licence in 2026, CRAN held that 100 percent foreign ownership undermines jurisdiction and the enforceability of compliance obligations.

Regional collaboration

Since 2023 the national ID has been accepted as a travel document for crossing into Botswana under a bilateral agreement. Citizens move between the two countries without a passport.

In March 2026 Namclear, the national clearing house, signed an agreement with PayInc to connect Namibia to TCIB, the SADC regional instant cross-border payment scheme. Low-value push payments to Common Monetary Area countries are being introduced in phases. A central bank directive of 31 March 2025 makes the scheme mandatory for low-value transfers within the Common Monetary Area from 1 April 2027. Customs data has passed to South Africa's revenue service since customs-to-customs connectivity went live in February 2021. In November 2026 the revenue agency signed an agreement with Russia to exchange customs data on the value, classification and origin of goods, not yet in force.

Regional integration is one of Namibia's ten most improved governance measures in the 2024 IIAG, up 14.8 points over 2014-2023 to 64.7 out of 100 and 4th of 54. Namibia ranks 6th of 54 for overall governance, on 63.9 against an African average of 49.3 (2023). Its own score has fallen 3.3 points since 2014 while that average rose.

In June 2026 the ICT minister said Angola's sovereign data centre in Luanda had encouraged Namibia's own data-centre ambitions.

An assessment of the legal, regulatory and institutional framework against the continental Protocol on Digital Trade went to a validation workshop in Windhoek, reported 5 October 2026. It is to feed a national digital trade action plan.

Standards

Namibia has no government interoperability framework, no data quality framework, no guidance for replacing legacy government information systems and no system for monitoring their uptime (2025). The e-procurement portal publishes tender notices and contracts, but not to the Open Contracting Data Standard, and exchanges no data with other government systems (2025).

By July 2026 the Namibia Interbank Settlement System had moved to the ISO 20022 messaging standard. A national QR payment standard, NamQR, had been settled and open banking specifications were complete (2026). No go-live date, merchant count or participant list has been published for NamQR or open banking.

CRAN states that it acts as Namibia's Root Certification Authority, regulating digital-certificate and certification-service providers (2026). At the end of August 2026 the regulator and a German identity vendor signed an implementation agreement connecting the home affairs ministry to the national trust framework. The ministry becomes the first certification service provider through its issue of electronic identity documents. On 30 July 2026 CRAN signed a three-year memorandum with the Namibia Chamber of Commerce and Industry covering public key infrastructure, cybersecurity, e-trade, regulatory standards and legislative reform.

The national standards institution launched a strategy in October 2025 targeting 1,200 new standards in three years. It had published 235 since its establishment in 2008, against 1,600 in Botswana, 6,500 in Zambia and more than 10,500 in South Africa. Four national standards were adopted in June 2026, covering chilled seafood, dates, laundry process management and refrigerating-appliance energy performance. None of the four covers government information systems. The institution is the international standards organisation's member body, participating in 21 technical committees and observing 23 (October 2026). In January 2024 it was an observer, not a participant, on the information-security standards committee.

Public debate and participation in policymaking

There is no government platform for citizens to lodge complaints, suggestions or information requests about service delivery (2025). No service standards such as response times are published, and there is no evidence of services changing in response to feedback. No national online platform lets citizens or businesses take part in policy decisions, submit petitions or give anonymous feedback.

In mid-August 2023 the ICT ministry held a further consultation on almost the same Data Protection Bill draft it had put out for comment in October 2022. The most recent draft had not been published as at August 2025. In February 2026 the ministry took the draft Cybercrime Bill to regional consultations, with written submissions due by 10 February. The final draft was circulated the week before a validation workshop in April 2026. Many civil society groups, earlier submitters among them, had little or no notice.

In June 2026 residents of the Omusati region protested on the claim that the electronic ID card would microchip and track citizens. In August 2026 the Ministry of Home Affairs rebutted social-media claims that the card exposes citizens' data, on the record and by name, at Gobabis. In July 2026 undocumented Namibians petitioned for the rollout to be suspended until a years-long backlog of citizenship and identity documents is cleared. They argued that a digital layer on an incomplete register would entrench exclusion. In August 2026 an opposition member asked Parliament for the agreements, contractors, data architecture and lifetime costs of a Chinese-funded smart city. He also asked for public hearings before the build proceeds.

Namibia has the freest media in Africa on the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 1st of 54 with a score of 83.1 out of 100 (2023). That score has fallen 10.7 points since 2014. Digital freedom scores 79.6, 11th of 54, and has risen slightly over the decade (2023).

The Access to Information Act 8 of 2022 had not been brought into force as at May 2026. Nothing is published on information requests received or granted (2025).

Finance

Financial sustainability

Namibia's universal service levy, up to 0.5 per cent of every licensee's annual turnover, has been in force since September 2024. The Universal Service Fund's first accounts cover the 18 months to 31 December 2025. They record N$68.0 million of grants received and N$278,153 of revenue from services (2025). Its 2025/26 operating deficit of N$6.5 million was met by a temporary loan from the regulator's reserves.

The 2026/27 estimates appropriate N$128.2 million to the Prime Minister's e-governance division, against N$73.6 million a year earlier (2025/26). The 2026/27 estimates print no financing source against their digital lines (2026/27).

Across the fifteen digital lines with a published outturn, unaudited spending in 2024/25 came to N$776.7 million, 90.7% of the N$856.1 million voted (2024/25).

The 2026/27 estimates print that outturn line by line: the e-governance division spent N$61.6 million of N$80.0 million voted, and the Information Commission N$5.5 million of N$19.9 million (2024/25).

The ICT ministry lists a national data centre among its flagship 2026/27 projects within a N$639.2 million budget. N$625.7 million of that budget is operational and N$13.6 million is for development (2026/27). Who carries the running costs of the data centre Huawei is to deliver under the Chinese smart-city grant had not been published as at September 2026. The electronic ID card is free to first-time, renewing and amending applicants, with a fee charged only for replacements (July 2026).

MoUs and other agreements

CRAN and the Namibia Chamber of Commerce and Industry signed a three-year memorandum of understanding in Windhoek on 30 July 2026. It covers public key infrastructure, cybersecurity, e-trade facilitation, regulatory standards and legislative reform, under a joint steering committee.

Namibia and China signed a Smart City arrangement at the end of June 2026, with the pilot forming part of the Windhoek People-Centred Smart City Strategy (2026-2036). China committed CNY 98 million to the pilot (2026).

In July 2025 Namibia and India agreed a cybersecurity memorandum at State House, between President Nandi-Ndaitwah and Prime Minister Modi. It sits alongside cooperation on UPI-based payments and digital governance. On 4 June 2026 the Namibia Institute of Public Administration and Management signed with the Global Cybersecurity Centre to train public servants in cybersecurity.

New investments

Paratus put NAD 600 million, about US$33.7 million at 2025 rates, into building its new Namibian mobile network (2025). About a third of it went on a technology stack bringing all the group's services onto one platform. It adds to Paratus investment in the country since 2018 (September 2025).

China has committed CNY 98 million, about US$14.4 million at 2026 rates, to Namibia's Smart City pilot. It pays for digital infrastructure, artificial intelligence capability and skills development in the capital (2026). In September 2025 Cabinet approved a US$20 million ICT venture capital fund for SME founders, women among them. No manager or disbursement for the fund has been found as at November 2026.

Namibia's digital-sector financing from 2016 to 2026 runs to 38 commitments, worth roughly US$79 million once the announcement currencies are converted (November 2026). Four of them carry about US$72 million (November 2026). The three from 2025 and 2026 are different kinds of money — an operator's capital spending on its own network, a commercial bank's loan to the state operator for network modernisation, and a foreign state's contribution to a municipal pilot — so their sizes say little about who funds Namibian digital. The fourth is China's completed 2018 grant of meteorological equipment. The other 34 are grants from 14 donors, together about US$7 million (November 2026).

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

In a decision gazetted on 23 March 2026, the Communications Regulatory Authority of Namibia refused Starlink a Class Comprehensive telecommunications licence and the spectrum it needed. It found the company met only three of six statutory criteria. The applicant is wholly foreign-owned against a rule requiring 51 percent Namibian ownership, and no ministerial exemption was granted (2026). Starlink said it would appeal. In June 2026 CRAN upheld the refusal; a fresh application had been filed on 8 June.

On 2 September 2025 Paratus launched Namibia's first privately owned mobile network, a data-only LTE and 5G service. The majority state-owned MTC then held more than two million subscribers and over 90 percent of the market, against TN Mobile's roughly 336,000 (September 2025). MTC switched on 5G in Windhoek, Swakopmund and Walvis Bay in August 2025. By September 2026 it had reached Grootfontein, Mariental, Okahandja, Otjiwarongo and Tsumeb, with a second phase of 19 sites across nine regions under construction.

Telecom Namibia brought the country's first submarine cable, WACS, ashore at Swakopmund in 2011. Paratus activated the second, Equiano, at Swakopmund on 12 June 2023. A study for Equiano's owner put used international bandwidth at 131 Gbps in 2020, 114 Kbps per internet user.

4G covered about 88 percent of the population in 2024, against a government target of full coverage by 2030. At 3.76 people per square kilometre, 5G is phased and city-first while rural areas stay on 2G and 3G (February 2026). In February 2026 the regulator proposed tax breaks on 4G-capable handsets, after an operator reported low use of its 4G towers in Kunene. In November 2025 the regulator approved MTC data bundles from N$25 for 1.5GB to N$699 for 50GB. Internet and computer access is Namibia's most improved of all 96 IIAG indicators, up 38.1 points over 2014-2023 to 49.6 out of 100. It ranks 13th of 54, while mobile communications ranks 21st (2023). In October 2026 Paratus launched the OneWeb low earth orbit service countrywide, with residential and commercial packages.

Data Storage

There is no shared cloud platform available to all government entities, no policy on where government cloud data may be hosted, and no monitoring of government cloud usage, security or savings (2025). The country's single Tier 3 facility is privately operated (2025). The nearest hyperscale regions are Amazon Web Services' Cape Town region and Microsoft Azure's South Africa North and West (2025). Namibia sets no data localisation requirement and does not regulate cross-border personal data flows (2026). In 2015 the Office of the Prime Minister's IT department ran a unified government data centre and a disaster recovery centre. In August 2023 the government had no fully operational data centre of its own. For 2024/25 the Auditor-General found no approved IT disaster recovery plan at the transport department. In 2013 the central bank built its own off-site recovery site at Prosperita to keep settlement running after a disaster.

Paratus's Armada data centre outside Windhoek has operated since September 2022, with multi-carrier connectivity including the Equiano submarine cable and Trans-Kalahari fibre. Its co-location space, with room for 104 client organisations, was 62% occupied in the year to June 2025. Armada, a 1MW carrier-neutral facility, is one of only two data centres in and around Windhoek; the other is Telecom Namibia's smaller Infinitum (May 2025).

In September 2025 Cabinet directed the ICT ministry and the Office of the Prime Minister to finalise the concept note and proposal for a national data centre, so that national data could be hosted locally. The 2025-2029 digital transformation strategy frames local hosting of government data as technological sovereignty. In September 2026 the ICT minister said construction had begun beside the new AI institute at Kenya House. No capacity, cost or completion date has been published. In June 2026 the minister cited Angola's sovereign data centre in Luanda as encouragement. Under a smart-city pilot funded by China, Huawei is to deliver smart-city systems and a national data centre with the home affairs and ICT ministries (July 2026).

Energy

Only 56.7 percent of the population had access to electricity in 2023. Renewables supplied 93.37 percent of domestic generation in 2021, principally from the Ruacana hydro plant. In 2023, 75.0 percent of the urban population had access against 34.1 percent of the rural population.

Only around 37 percent of Namibians said in 2024 that their electricity supply is reliable. Dissatisfaction with government performance on electricity provision is rising (2024). The national utility reported transmission availability of 99.81 percent for 2024/25, with no load-shedding or power outages. It put 4,856 GWh into the system that year, 44.6 percent of it generated locally (2024/25). In June 2026 it inaugurated a N$394 million digital substation outside Swakopmund as the main transmission supply point for the coastal network. Access to energy scores 50.3 out of 100 in the 2024 IIAG, 17th of 54 and up 5.5 points over the decade. About 12.6 percent of the population was served off-grid in 2023, under an off-grid electrification policy.

More than 180 rural schools had no electricity at all in 2023, and off-grid solar had reached 15 of them.

Namibia has over 3,000 hours of sunshine a year, and a green hydrogen and solar development near Walvis Bay is put forward as the power base for a data-centre industry (February 2026).

Technical Capacity

The Nam-X interoperability layer was built with Estonian partners, the Integrated Border Management System runs on Thales technology, and the e-ID is being prepared with UNDP support (2024). UNDP describes in-country capacity to build or replace these systems as partial.

Effective administration scores 77.2 out of 100 in the 2024 IIAG, 4th of 54 and up 10.9 points since 2014. A ministry leads public sector digital transformation, with a cross-government forum of senior digital officials (2025). The statistical system scores 50.9 out of 100, 25th of 54 (2023). Budgetary and financial management fell 30.5 points over the decade, the fifth sharpest deterioration (2023). There is no government strategy or programme to improve digital skills in the public sector (2025).

In 2025 the Prime Minister conceded that many ministry websites and email systems were outdated; the Office of the Prime Minister's IT department runs them centrally. In June 2025 the finance ministry put support, maintenance and development of its financial management system out to a five-year consultancy tender. The state oil company's enterprise resource planning contract rose from N$23 million in 2020 to N$68 million by May 2023 for a system not fully functioning, and was placed under internal investigation (January 2024).

The police operate a central digital backend of case and biometric systems (2026). In 2022 rural stations still opened dockets, occurrence book entries and statements by hand and stored them in physical cabinets. Officers were willing to digitise but lacked the funding and infrastructure.

Cybersecurity

NAM-CSIRT, the national incident response team, sits inside the communications regulator and publishes its threat figures quarterly (2026). CRAN appointed an executive to lead it from 1 October 2026. NAM-CSIRT counted 1.7 million cyberattacks against Namibian targets in 2025, 37 percent more than in 2024, and 2.23 million detected vulnerabilities. Its July-September 2026 quarter logged 506,143 vulnerabilities and 163,053 cyber events, vulnerabilities down about 1.5 percent on the second quarter and events up 0.93 percent. It named internet-exposed systems as the principal cause (2026).

Namibia had neither a cybercrime statute nor a data protection law in July 2026; both bills were still being finalised. Enforcement rests meanwhile on the Electronic Transactions Act, the Communications Act and the Penal Code (2026). A Cybercrime Bill that would designate and protect critical information infrastructure was still in draft in January 2026. Under determination PSD-12, in force from 1 July 2023, payment system participants must report a successful cyberattack to the central bank within 24 hours. A full impact assessment of financial, data and availability loss must follow within a month.

In December 2024 Telecom Namibia lost data to the Hunters International ransomware group, exposing records of ministries, regional councils and municipalities. On 19 September 2026 the ransomware group RansomHouse listed the Namibian Defence Force as a victim and published what it called an evidence pack. NAM-CSIRT confirmed unauthorised activity on the defence ministry's network, without saying what was accessed (September 2026). Namibia scored 37.93 out of 100 in the ITU's Global Cybersecurity Index 2024, in the index's fourth tier of five, the "evolving" band. In November 2025 Cabinet approved a government-sector CSIRT, security operations centres and cyber-skills training for public servants, coordinated by the Office of the Prime Minister.

DPI

Data Exchange

In 2025 Nam-X linked the population register to e-birth and e-death notification, to passports and to the statistics system. Built on the Estonian X-Road stack, it connects civil registration, the population register and some government databases, but full inter-agency coverage has not been achieved. Memoranda to bring in the ministries for gender and child welfare and for finance, the Electoral Commission and the poverty eradication programme were still being finalised in 2025. Nam-X connects the population register to other public and private registries so that an identity can be checked. It was used to validate applicants for the COVID-19 emergency income grant. It is run from the Office of the Prime Minister, with regional points of presence.

The financial management, tax, customs and social insurance systems each pass data to other government systems (2025). The same assessment records no government interoperability framework, no data quality framework and no monitoring of whether government systems are up. No law mandates cross-government data sharing.

In 2025 the immovable property registry and the cadastre were interoperable neither with each other nor with other public services. The automated exchange between the beneficiary registry and the wider platform is not operational (2023/24). In February 2024 the revenue agency and the business registry agreed to design a link between tax and company records. The trade single window's implementation date has moved three times, now to 30 June 2028, with the mandate passed to a new agency. A phased rollout is expected from late 2026 or early 2027.

Digital Identity and CRVS

The Ministry of Home Affairs printed 166,237 national identity documents in the fifteen months to 30 June 2026, and 125,999 were collected.

At the 2023 census 85.4% of Namibians aged 16 and over held a national identity card. Citizens' sense of how easily an identity document can be obtained fell 29.9 points over the decade to 2023, to 37.3 out of 100.

Civil registration and identity management run on one system, so vital events update the identity record through a single unique identity number and cards are issued out of the population register. Enrolment captures all ten fingerprints and a facial photograph. The card covers citizens and permanent residents, compulsory from 16. Every mobile subscriber must register a SIM against one, or a passport, a requirement in force since 1 April 2024 after the original deadline was extended by three months. Grants to 15,785 beneficiaries were suspended in 2026 when their records did not match the population register; by 8 September 10,066 had been reinstated and more than 5,700 were still waiting.

Government approved the e-ID and interoperable digital identity systems in August 2023. The rollout was carried in the 2026/27 budget. The launch slipped from July to September 2026. On 3 November 2026 Cabinet put it back again until regulations under the Civil Registration and Identification Act 2024 are made. The current card cannot be used for digital or remote authentication. In June 2026 the minister told the National Assembly that implementation will comply with the 2024 Act. Under that Act chip use is not compulsory and disclosure is consent-based, with further safeguards left to data protection legislation that has not been enacted. At the end of August 2026 the communications regulator and a German identity vendor signed an agreement connecting the ministry to the national trust framework. The ministry becomes the first certification service provider through its issue of electronic identity documents.

Digital Payments and Fintech

In June 2026 the Instant Payment Solution became operational after a live pilot, with Bank Windhoek, Nampost and Letshego Bank Namibia the first participants. The Bank of Namibia runs it through a subsidiary, Instant Payments Namibia, with India's NPCI deploying the system inside the central bank's own environment (2025).

On 17 June 2026 the first cohort of marginalised beneficiaries was paid over the new rail through Bank Windhoek, with pensioners and other grant recipients to follow. Only the first phase, government-to-person grant payments, has been introduced (2026). In March 2026 Namclear agreed to connect the country to TCIB, the SADC regional instant cross-border scheme. Bank-to-bank transfers already run on NamPay, the national electronic funds transfer system. Its near-real-time credit stream carries person-to-person, salary and supplier payments.

The Payment System Management Act 14 of 2023 lets the Bank of Namibia approve payment system rules and membership criteria, and carries a part on consumer protection. A determination effective 15 February 2024 licenses non-bank payment service providers and requires them to file audited annual statements. Operators must submit scheme rules on governance, access, risk, settlement finality and fees, on forms marked confidential (2024). A 2025 directive requires every e-money issuer to interoperate through the instant payment switch by 30 November 2026, a deadline moved from 26 February.

Access to banking scores 45.9 out of 100 in the 2024 IIAG and ranks 6th of 54. It is among Namibia's ten weakest measures and its ten most improved (2023). The Bank of Namibia's 2025 annual report put banking fraud losses at N$73.9 million for the year, against roughly N$8.7 million in 2020.

Registries

Namibia scores a perfect 100.0 out of 100 on civil registration in the 2024 IIAG, one of only two such measures, ranking 1st of 54 and unchanged across the decade to 2023. Birth registration fell to 62.0% in 2021, from 89.6% the year before. The National Population Registration System links 62 decentralised registration centres for real-time recording of births, deaths and marriages. Certificates are issued to the applicant on the spot. Electronic birth and death notification now reaches 153 health facilities, private hospitals, police mortuaries and old age homes. These feed directly into the register.

Company registration is only partly digital, and an integrated registration system targeting incorporation within 60 minutes was kicked off with its developer in January 2026. Name reservation, an application-status check and a login portal are online. Registration still requires notarised memoranda and articles of association lodged in person. The Deeds Registry remains substantially paper-based while a computerised system is implemented (2025). The World Bank's 2025 assessment found no digital title certificates, no digital cadastral plans and no public online identity-checking database. It found no interoperability between the property registry, the cadastre and other public services.

An integrated beneficiary registry and an upgraded social assistance database were both completed in 2022. Neither is yet fully deployed across all schemes, and the automated exchange with the wider government platform that was to follow is not operational. The Integrated Social Assistance System, linked to the population register, is in active use. It verifies beneficiaries' identity and life status and pays them under a March 2026 Cabinet decision on the Conditional Basic Income Grant. Coverage across the individual schemes is highly uneven. The Auditor-General found 3,350 duplicate child beneficiary records, with N$28.8 million paid twice, and over N$43 million paid to deceased recipients in 2024/25. The same audit found 8,099 of 488,753 grant beneficiaries unmatched to the national population register. The Electoral Commission's fingerprint matching removed 15,560 duplicate entries before the final 2024 roll of about 1.4 million voters. Registration ran on its upgraded in-house mobile biometric system from 3 June 2024.

Sectoral management information systems

Only 18 clinics nationally were digitally connected as of Namibia's 2025/26 health budget. In March 2026 the health minister named fragmented information systems, paper records and uneven rural and urban access as the continuing obstacles. A National Digital Health Policy launched in February 2026 runs for ten years. District officers key facilities' monthly paper summaries into DHIS2 (2026).

In 2018 most schools recorded attendance in paper registers, marked daily and summarised weekly. Education regions collect data twice a year from all schools for the ministry's EMIS division (2024). The police have run a central digital backend since 2010, with case dockets and crime statistics across linked databases and a biometric system added in 2016. In October 2026 the police said the E-Policing system suffers slow processing and interruptions, and that an overhaul has begun with no budget or timetable disclosed.

The financial management information system covers central government only and supports treasury execution, not the full public financial management cycle (2025). There is a debt management system but no public investment management system. In mid-2026 faults crippled the tax agency's portal, and the filing deadline moved from 30 June to 31 August while functions were restored. Version 4.4.1 of the customs platform went live on 5 May 2026, ending paper documents for transit and warehousing.

None of the five core administrative systems in operation — financial management, the treasury single account, tax, customs and e-procurement — has governance arrangements covering compliance, security and audit trails.

There is no human resources management information system with employee self-service, and no payroll system linked to one (2025). The automated payroll deduction system was shut on 30 November 2025 when its operator's contract was not renewed. Deductions now run through a manual helpdesk, and a December 2025 High Court order bars the ministry from interfering with them pending review.

Other GovTech and e-Gov

In 2025 Namibia's online public service portal was informational, publishing information without completing transactions. It offers omnichannel access, and a business can be started through it. Transactional services beyond that are confined to a handful. The national digital strategy's target of a portal carrying ten essential services is not operational.

Of the 32 core public sector digital capabilities the GovTech Maturity Index checks for in Namibia in 2025, 15 are in place, 16 are absent, and one — a whole-of-government approach to digital transformation — is in draft.

The tax portal takes registration and filing with omnichannel access (2025). Faults left it running slowly through mid-2026. In November 2025 the revenue agency launched Onyofi, a customer service and feedback system built in-house, across 11 service points. The social insurance and pension portal takes registration and benefits transactions. The e-procurement portal publishes tender notices and contracts. It does not publish to the Open Contracting Data Standard or exchange data with other government systems. Fewer than 5% of public entities used that portal in May 2025, on the World Bank's count. An end-to-end procurement platform is left to the second phase of the e-GP strategy for 2023-2027.

There is no government job portal (2025). There is no mobile app for public services, no e-residency and no involvement of citizens or businesses in designing e-services. Nothing is published on service delivery performance or user experience.

A ministry leads public sector digital transformation, and a cross-government forum brings senior digital officials together (2025). An established GovTech entity and a coordinating body exist. Nothing monitors digital spending across government, and the institution publishes no annual progress report. No GovTech or digital transformation strategy has been adopted. The government's stated target is to digitise all government services by 2029/30. Public administration as a whole fell 8.4 points over the decade to 2023. The Social Security Commission's mySSC portal takes employers' returns, contributions and certificate requests online (June 2025).

Digitalisation

Digitalisation of sub-national government

In September 2025 Cabinet directed that a legal framework be developed for a Windhoek smart city, working with the Ministry of Urban and Rural Development. The Windhoek People-Centred Smart City Strategy runs from 2026 to 2036. In July 2023 the urban and rural development ministry found the financial systems at most local authorities obsolete. In July 2025 the City of Windhoek confirmed billing faults on 387 accounts.

On 4 August 2026 Rodney Cloete of the Independent Patriots for Change argued against accepting a Chinese smart-city grant without enforceable safeguards. He is the party's shadow minister of international relations and trade. He put the grant at N$245 million and noted that no data-protection statute is in force to bind a foreign supplier (2026).

Nam-X is managed from the Office of the Prime Minister, with regional points of presence giving access to government services outside Windhoek (2024).

Rural digital data capture

In March 2026 the health minister named paper records, fragmented information systems and uneven rural and urban access as obstacles. A 2022 study of the Omusati region found police officers opening case dockets, occurrence book entries, affidavits and statements on paper at the scene and filing them in cabinets. The officers were willing to digitise but had neither the funding nor the infrastructure. In 2018 most schools recorded attendance in paper registers. Education regions collect data from every school twice a year (2024).

In March 2025 the Universal Service Fund made its first priority the five regions where 4G coverage falls below the National Broadband Policy's 80 percent threshold. They are Kavango West, Kavango East, Kunene, Ohangwena and Oshikoto, and 16 schools and four clinics there were selected for uncapped Wi-Fi. Rural households are far less likely than urban ones to have electricity (2023), and many rural schools have none at all.

Namibia carries no score on any of the four rural-economy indicators in the 2024 Ibrahim Index. Its statistical programme has gone without an agricultural survey for a decade (2023).

Technology

AI

In September 2025 a deepfake video showed the Prime Minister, her deputy and ICT minister Emma Theofelus endorsing an investment scheme promising N$35,000 a week, which Theofelus disavowed. In November 2025 the Presidency denounced a viral deepfake audio and video impersonating the President. By 2025 NAM-CSIRT had catalogued AI-driven frauds against Namibians, from deepfake investment scams to phishing that impersonates delivery services.

Namibia had no national strategy for disruptive or innovative technologies in 2025. In September 2025 the research commission began developing a national AI strategy under the education ministry, with technical advisory committees of AI experts. In October 2026 Cabinet directed the Office of the Prime Minister and the ICT ministry to write an internal policy on civil servants' use of AI. On 23 September 2026 the commission launched a national AI institute with development, datasets, AI-innovation and research centres. It says its youth fellowship reaches about 2,000 tertiary students (2026). The 2026 Ayre Index placed Namibia 36th of 56 Commonwealth states for youth AI readiness, at 42.7 of 100 against a readiness threshold of 53.

The central bank and the communications regulator run a donor-funded AI citizen-complaint pilot (2026). In September 2026 they launched a programme for agencies to buy AI hardware and agents jointly. The health ministry has screened for tuberculosis with AI-aided chest X-rays since March 2025. Under a Chinese-funded smart-city pilot, Huawei is to deliver an "AI-ready" national data centre (2026). In July 2026 the President pressed Huawei at its Shenzhen headquarters to create AI jobs and skills in Namibia.

ICT Industry

Namibia ranks 4th of 54 African states for its business and labour environment in the 2024 IIAG, on 58.7. It ranks only 27th for business and competition regulations, which scored 54.5 after a 10.8-point fall over the decade to 2023. It has 2.6 million people and GDP per capita of USD 4,742.8 (2023). Mobile is dominated by the majority state-owned MTC (2025). The first privately owned network, built by Paratus with Nokia and Cerillion as partners, launched in September 2025.

In 2025 Namibia had no policy to support GovTech startups or private investment in government technology, and no public financing to startups or SMEs for innovation. No procurement policy prioritises bids from startups and SMEs, and nothing is published on progress in the area.

Innovation ecosystem

At least four innovation hubs are active: ScaleUp Namibia at the investment promotion board, Impact Tank, the Afrisource Innovation Center and RLabs Namibia (2026). The largest venture round raised by a Namibian startup is JABU's US$3.2 million for B2B e-commerce and retail distribution in 2022. Namibia ranked 102nd of 133 economies in WIPO's Global Innovation Index 2024. No start-up act had been gazetted or tabled as at August 2026.

A public sector innovation entity and strategy exist, but there is no collaboration with the private sector and no published performance or results (2025). In September 2025 Cabinet approved an ICT venture capital fund for SME founders, with women founders a target group.

Capacity

Literacy

Equality in education ranks 34th of 54 African countries and has slid over the decade to 2023, one of Namibia's ten worst governance measures. Namibia sits 6th on the continent overall (2023). The teaching workforce is one of the ten best measures Namibia has, 6th of 54 and sharply improved since 2014. The quality of the education it delivers ranks 13th (2023). Enrolment rose 11.4 points in an education score that ranked 12th of 54 in 2023 and was otherwise flat across the decade, with completion also up.

Rural primary schools keep attendance, enrolment and learning outcomes on paper registers (January 2024). They send them twice yearly on forms to regional offices for the ministry to digitise centrally (January 2024). Many rural schools have no electricity at all, which with thin rural mobile coverage and few devices keeps computers out of those classrooms (2023). Outside school, the ICT ministry's free digital literacy programme had certified more than 900 people across all 14 regions by January 2025, eighteen months after its launch. Its regional offices were running two-week courses for 20 people at a time in July 2026. The first AI tutoring labs in a government school, privately funded, opened at a Lüderitz secondary school in September 2026. Six more schools in the region are scheduled for 2027.

Training and skills

The World Bank's 2025 GovTech assessment found Namibia with no government strategy or programme to raise digital skills in the public service, none for citizens or schools, and no published results. In November 2025 Cabinet approved proposals that included cyber-skills training for public servants. In June 2026 the Namibia Institute of Public Administration and Management signed a memorandum with the Global Cybersecurity Centre to train public servants in cybersecurity. On 12 May 2026 the institute held its first executive seminar on AI in the public sector, for more than 100 participants. It plans three-to-five-day AI courses for public servants (2026). In July 2026 President Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah pressed Huawei to create AI jobs and skills in Namibia. Other digital training runs in the tertiary institutions and the country's innovation hubs (2026).

Research institutions

On 23 September 2026 the national research commission opened a national AI institute, with a research centre among its four. The Namibia University of Science and Technology hosts several UNESCO research chairs (2024).

Inclusion

Access to services

In 2024 the Old Age Grant covered about 97% of its target population, the Disability and Vulnerable Children's grants about 75% each, and the Conditional Basic Income Grant about 14% of targeted households.

In September 2026 the Vice-President set out new guidelines and digital systems for disability assessment and access to the disability grant. No system name, supplier, timetable or accessibility standard was given. A national identity document is mandatory for a grant, and beneficiaries are re-verified against the population register (2026). By 8 September 2026, 10,066 of 15,785 beneficiaries suspended in that check had been reinstated, and more than 5,700 were still waiting. Much of what the ministry printed in the fifteen months to June 2026 was never collected. Namibians' reported ease of obtaining an identity document fell sharply over the decade to 2023.

Permanent residents hold pink identity cards and refugees yellow ones; non-nationals staying less than a year cannot hold one (2024). In 2025 UNHCR paid cash assistance to around 6,000 people at the Osire settlement through MTC's mobile money service.

From October 2025 cash grants moved to NamPost's branches and mobile teams. Government grants began moving onto the national instant payment system in June 2026. In July 2026 the Bank of Namibia conceded that transaction costs need industry-wide action. It reset its objective from access to effective use, naming cash reliance, thin rural provision, informal-sector barriers and the digital divide (2026).

The Universal Service Fund launched in March 2025 with N$145 million of government seed funding over three years. N$31 million went first to MTC for nine network sites (2025). In January 2026 the ICT minister said schools and clinics under nine towers built with N$80 million would get free internet for seven years. The public service portal publishes information but completes no transactions, and there is no accessibility support for disabled users (2025).

Digital divides

Inclusion and equality is one of Namibia's three worst-scoring governance sub-categories, at 58.3 out of 100 in 2023. It fell 4.5 points over the decade, to 16th of 54 African states. The equal access to public services measure inside it is lower still, at 53.0 and also falling. Both sit far below the country's 6th place on the continent overall.

Namibia's population density is among the world's lowest (February 2026). This makes towers and backhaul expensive, and the 5G rollout is city-first while rural areas stay on 2G and 3G. Rural households are far less likely than urban ones to have electricity (2023), and many rural schools have none at all (2023). The Universal Service Fund's priority regions are Kavango West, Kavango East, Kunene, Ohangwena and Oshikoto, where 4G coverage falls short of the national broadband target (March 2025).

Many of Namibia's schools, and a smaller share of its health facilities, had no 4G service at all (2022).

MTC has reported that much of its 4G capacity in the Kunene region goes unused (February 2026). CRAN has proposed tax breaks on 4G-capable handsets (February 2026). In April 2026 the regulator committed N$42.1 million under the second phase of the Universal Service Fund. N$32.3 million of it went to MTC for nine rural sites (April 2026). Mobile data is expensive by international comparison, one gigabyte averaging US$10.52 (2022). Namibia ranked 12th from the bottom of 233 countries (2022).

Data

National statistics

Namibia launched its first National Strategy for the Development of Statistics, running from 2023/24 to 2026/27, on 5 July 2023.

In the decade to 2023 Namibia ran no agricultural survey and no business survey, one health survey and one household survey, and three or more labour surveys. All three census types the World Bank tracks, population, agricultural and business, were conducted in the same decade. In August 2026 the statistics agency released a public-use microdata sample of about 20% of the 2023 census records, on registration.

Birth registration completeness fell sharply in 2021, on the statistics agency's own reporting. The agency compiles mortality and cause-of-death statistics from civil registration records, the latest edition covering 2018-2021.

Data products score in the 70-89% band and data use, services and infrastructure in the 50-69% band (2023). Data sources, from censuses and surveys to administrative and geospatial collection, fall to the 20-49% band (2023). The statistical system scores 50.9 out of 100 and ranks 25th of 54 in 2023, after a gain of 4.4 points over the decade. It is among the weakest of Namibia's public administration measures, in a country ranked 6th for governance overall (2023). In September 2025 the statistics agency issued five national statistics system standards, on quality indicators, metadata, leave no one behind, presentation and dissemination. They govern how statistics are described and published, not how they are collected.

There is no dedicated government entity for data governance or data management, and no data governance strategy or policy (2025). The Bank of Namibia publishes national payment system figures quarterly, each edition within days of quarter-end (2026). In August 2025 it launched Datasphere, a free online dashboard of economic data.

Open data

The national open data portal carries only basic information and datasets and is updated mostly by hand, not through APIs (2025).

On the 2024 Open Data Inventory, population and vital statistics score 5.5 out of 10, poverty 4.5, gender statistics 3, education and health outcomes 2.5, and education facilities and reproductive health 2. Government finance scores 7, balance of payments, trade and price statistics 6 each, national accounts 5.5, and labour and money and banking 4.5 (2024).

No category in that 2024 profile scores above 7 out of 10, the mean across the 19 scored categories is 4.2, and 13 of the 19 sit at or below 4.5 — an open data estate that is uniformly mid-to-low rather than strong in parts.

The Access to Information Act 8 of 2022 was gazetted in December 2022 but had not been brought into force as at May 2026, and no Information Commissioner had been appointed. The National Assembly invited applications for Information Commissioner and deputy in February 2026. Nothing is published on how many information requests are received and granted (2025). Accessibility of public records rose 15.4 points over 2014-2023 to 54.6 out of 100, ranking 5th of 54. Disclosure rose 8.9 points to 53.4, ranking 6th (2023).

The e-procurement portal does not publish tender notices or contracts to the Open Contracting Data Standard (2025).

On all 16 transparency measures the 2025 GovTech assessment applies — results, progress or spending for everything from cloud, data protection and digital transformation to right to information, online services and digital skills — Namibia is recorded as publishing nothing.

Geospatial data

In February 2026 a Chinese-supplied satellite ground station receiving remote-sensing data was handed over at the Telecom Earth Station outside Windhoek, operated by trained Namibian staff. Control of the data it takes down has been raised in Parliament (2026). Since November 2022 a donor-funded partnership has fed remote-sensing crop-yield forecasts to the agriculture ministry, with field data from seven northern regions. In 2018 Chinese aid gave the meteorological service a FengYun-3 satellite data receiving and processing system. In April 2023 Namibia reported to the WMO/UNEP ozone research managers that it had satellite equipment and data but lacked the technical capacity to use them. Geospatial collection sits inside the weakest of the five pillars of Namibia's statistical performance (2023).

The regulator refused Starlink a licence in March 2026 and upheld the refusal in June. The gazette that carried the refusal also approved a licence transfer for another satellite operator (2026). The regulator's chief executive framed the question as compliance with the statute.

Geopolitics

US / hyperscaler activities

No United States government programme held an operating position in Namibia's digital infrastructure as at August 2026. No American platform or hyperscaler held one either.

China activities

In July 2026 Huawei was named to deliver smart-city systems and a national data centre with the ministries responsible for home affairs and ICT. The work rests on a Chinese grant signed at the end of June 2026, within the Windhoek People-Centred Smart City Strategy running to 2036. In February 2026 a Chinese-supplied satellite ground station was handed over at the Telecom Earth Station outside Windhoek, run by a trained Namibian team. An aid package of 2018 had given the meteorological service a FengYun-3 satellite data system and weather stations.

In July 2026 President Nandi-Ndaitwah pressed Huawei at its Shenzhen headquarters for AI jobs and skills in Namibia. The company pitched the partnership as strengthening Namibian data sovereignty (2026). In August 2026 an opposition shadow minister argued that the grant should not be accepted without enforceable safeguards, because no data-protection statute is in force. He asked Parliament for the agreements, contractors, data architecture and lifetime operating costs. He also asked for a Data Protection Act before the build proceeds.

EU activities

UNDP described Namibia in 2024 as running its identity systems with only partial in-country capacity. Nam-X was built with Estonian partners, and the border management system runs on Thales technology. An Estonian-funded project is building further Nam-X services (2025).

The digital components of the EU's Multi-Annual Indicative Programme for Namibia fund parliamentary work: an e-records database of debates, an online library, an e-consultation system and members' e-skills (2021). They sit within a governance envelope of EUR 2.5 million out of an indicative EUR 37 million for 2021 to 2024. The programme names data protection once, as "possibly data protection and cyber security", with no allocation (2021).

Gulf/UAE activities

No Gulf state and no Gulf-based company held a position in Namibia's digital infrastructure as at August 2026.

India activities

India's NPCI deployed Namibia's instant payment system inside the Bank of Namibia's own environment, and the central bank operates it through a subsidiary (2025).

Namibia and India signed a cybersecurity memorandum of understanding at State House in July 2025. President Nandi-Ndaitwah and Prime Minister Modi agreed it alongside cooperation on UPI-based payments and digital governance.

Digital sovereignty

In August 2026 officials said a review through the attorney general's office found that a draft US health agreement gave the United States extensive unilateral access to Namibians' medical information. The review cited the constitutional right to privacy and national sovereignty over biological resources (2026). In November 2025 the US had confirmed it would withdraw the specimen-sharing agreement from funding negotiations. A joint statement of 4 September 2026 set US$45 million for 2027 as a transition, after which Namibia funds its own HIV response.

In August 2026 Cabinet had the agriculture ministry terminate a N$40 million satellite and AI crop-monitoring agreement with the US firm 6th Grain. Cabinet found it did not meet the legal and procedural requirements for government contracts. The ministry had said the agreement kept agricultural and land data in Namibian ownership and transferred the software, models and skills (2026). The firm demanded N$13 million in September 2026.

The national root certification authority, run by the communications regulator, went live after a key-signing ceremony (2026). The ICT ministry set it up with N$10.5 million (2026). The electronic-signature provisions of the Electronic Transactions Act came into force on 15 June 2026. In September 2026 home affairs became the first certification service provider accredited under it.

In 2026 the Prime Minister said his office and the ICT ministry had begun assessing who owns government data and digital infrastructure, after a question on a foreign-run online visa system. The ICT ministry names a national data centre among its flagship 2026/27 projects, and the only build with a vendor is Huawei's under the Chinese grant. No instrument fixing where state data is held, or under whose law it is processed, had been found as at September 2026. The grant's terms on data jurisdiction, portability and exit had not been published as at September 2026.
