This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

Opening the Fourteenth National Assembly on 9 October 2026, President Hakainde Hichilema set out a five-year Grow Zambia agenda that includes faster digitalisation and the adoption of artificial intelligence. He named internet access across the country, the country's own data centres and trained people as what that requires.

In the 2024 Ibrahim Index, public administration is one of only three of sixteen sub-categories in which Zambia went backwards over 2014-2023. It edged down 0.2 points to 58.4 out of 100, 19th of 54. Budgetary and financial management is the fifth most deteriorated indicator of all, down 18.1 points to 56.9 and 24th of 54. Effective administration rose 3.6 points to 55.4. Tax and revenue mobilisation fell 8.3 points to 41.5, 31st of 54. Overall governance scored 56.6 out of 100, 17th of 54 and 7.3 points above the African average of 49.3. Zambia is above the continental average on 72 of the index's 96 indicators.

The National ICT Policy 2023, published with an implementation plan by the Ministry of Technology and Science, is the overarching instrument. It covers broadband, cybersecurity, data centres, digital identification, digital payments, e-waste, spectrum, quality of service and universal access. The ministry's National Digital Transformation Strategy spans broadband, data protection, digital payments and the first, second and last mile of connectivity. SMART Zambia Institute's National e-Government Plan 2023-2026, issued under the Electronic Government Act, has a thematic area on standards and compliance with phased milestones. The second National Financial Inclusion Strategy, 2024-2028, targets 85 percent financial inclusion and 80 percent formal inclusion by 2028, from 69.4 percent in 2020. Digital financial services are one of its thematic areas.

In 2025 the GovTech Maturity Index recorded a dedicated digital government institution, with machinery for coordinating government technology across ministries. It also recorded a current digital transformation strategy in force, with a second instrument in the same family still planned or in draft.

Legislation and regulation

Enforcement of the Cyber Crimes Act commenced in July 2026. The Ministry of Information and Media said the Act "applies equally to every individual without exception". Public servants were singled out for a duty to protect confidential information held in the course of their duties.

The Cyber Security Act No. 3 of 2025 builds the interception and agency architecture. The Cyber Crimes Act No. 4 of 2025 carries the offences. Both were gazetted on 15 April 2025.

The Data Protection Act No. 3 of 2021 regulates the collection, use, transmission and storage of personal data and establishes the Data Protection Commissioner. The Electronic Government Act No. 41 of 2021 mandates the digitalisation of public services. The Electronic Communications and Transactions Act No. 4 of 2021 makes ZICTA the national root certification authority. Its amendment went to stakeholder consultation in September 2026. The National Payment System Act No. 5 of 2026, dated 8 April 2026, licenses payment service providers and sets market-conduct and consumer-protection rules. It had not come into force at August 2026.

In 2024 there was no dedicated digital identity law, digital ID being governed by the National Registration Act, the Electronic Government Act and the Data Protection Act. The government began amending the e-government law in January 2026. The Bill gives legal standing to electronic submissions and digital authentication so services can run end to end digitally.

The UN Special Rapporteur on freedom of expression found in June 2025 that the 2025 Acts' vague definitions of "critical information" and sweeping interception powers create a risk of surveillance and censorship. The Law Association of Zambia said in April 2025 it would mount a legal challenge to them. An opposition running mate campaigned in July 2026 on repealing both. Over an alleged election-period messaging campaign, civil society cited section 83 of the Electoral Process Act No. 35 of 2016 and the Anti-Corruption Act 2012.

Data protection

Zambia's Cyber Security Act No. 3 of 2025 obliges every service provider to keep an interception capability and to store call-related information and internet connection records. The manner of that capability is set by presidential statutory instrument.

Under the 2025 Act a law enforcement officer applies ex parte to a High Court judge for an interception order effective for three months, renewable once. The one route without a prior order is an oral request to a provider where a court order would be too slow. The officer must then be before a judge within two days. The officials who may seek interception, from police and anti-corruption to drug enforcement, intelligence and counter-terrorism, can be widened by presidential statutory instrument.

The Data Protection Act No. 3 of 2021 obliges a controller to notify the Commissioner within twenty-four hours of a breach and affected data subjects as soon as practicable. The Office of the Data Protection Commissioner was declared fully operational in April 2025. Controllers and processors had to register by 30 April, with statutory penalties for non-compliance. The office now takes part in the digital identity programme (May 2026). The GovTech Maturity Index rates the law effective and the authority in place, but finds that neither publishes anything about its work (2025).

Civil-society organisations asked the Commissioner in July 2026 to examine the source of the contact list behind an alleged bulk-SMS campaign to social cash transfer beneficiaries. They asked ZICTA to trace the messaging channel. For the refugee register transfer, the project's own assessment of December 2024 names data privacy in capturing refugees' biometric data. A breach of confidentiality could leave some at risk of exclusion.

Digital freedom is Zambia's ninth most improved Ibrahim Index indicator, up 19.7 points over 2014-2023 to 76.4 out of 100 and 14th of 54. It measures online censorship, interference with online content and users' rights, on 2023 data that precede the 2025 Acts. The technology ministry's permanent secretary said the country is implementing a national child online protection strategy for 2025 to 2029.

Regional collaboration

Lusaka hosted a COMESA and World Bank regional AI workshop in July 2026. It was launched as a first step toward a COMESA Regional AI Strategy, framed against policy fragmentation across the bloc.

In 2025 Zambia was piloting cross-border mutual recognition of national IDs and ID verification with COMESA and neighbouring IDEA countries including Angola, the DRC and Malawi. Formal cross-border interoperability of the digital ID was not yet implemented. Zambia has an identity memorandum of understanding with Ethiopia, drawing on the Fayda system, chosen because it is domestically developed.

In 2023 cross-border payments ran mainly at wholesale level, through the regional SADC-RTGS and COMESA settlement systems. Instant low-value transfers from South Africa went live on the SADC TCIB scheme in February 2024. In June 2026 the regulator said roaming charges had been cut by 3-93% across six SADC states under the One Network Area. On 11 November 2026 COMESA's central bank governors were told that the bloc's digital retail payment platform was approaching user trials. Live transactions are to be piloted between Malawi and Zambia.

Zambia, landlocked and a member of both COMESA and SADC, scores 47.8 out of 100 on the Ibrahim Index's regional integration measure, 26th of 54 and down 1.6 points over 2014-2023. The measure covers trade, movement and infrastructure integration, with no digital or data-flow component.

Standards

Officials pursuing the e-government law amendment in January 2026 named fragmented, vendor-locked systems as a governance, policy and legal alignment problem, not a technical one. SMART Zambia Institute's Public Service ICT Data Interoperability Standards are mandatory for all ministries, provinces and spending agencies. They carry an annual compliance audit schedule. With the Electronic Government Act, they set the data management and governance requirements for government data.

In 2025 the GovTech Maturity Index recorded a government interoperability framework and a data-exchange platform extensively used across government. It also recorded an electronic government procurement system in use for online tendering and contract management. The Bank of Zambia is both system and technical operator of the interbank payment and settlement system under the publicly issued ZIPSS Rules 2024.

The World Bank's 2025 digital acceleration project designs the identity system as homegrown, built on open standards and existing investments. The aim is for government to manage its infrastructure independently and reduce reliance on external vendors. Privacy, security and interoperability are stated priorities. In March 2026 SMART Zambia was procuring an international system integrator for a MOSIP-based register. It insists the application layer be built by Zambians on open standards to avoid vendor lock-in.

ZICTA said in February 2026 it would take visible action against mobile network operators over poor quality of service. Quarterly network-performance reports were announced in May 2026 as the accountability measure on operator quality. ZICTA put revised type-approval guidelines for equipment out for comment from 21 October to 20 November 2026. The draft replaces the 2022 set with a high- and low-risk classification of equipment.

Public debate and participation in policymaking

Deliberative and participatory governance is Zambia's fifth most improved Ibrahim Index indicator, up 32.7 points over 2014-2023 to 79.1 out of 100 and 3rd of 54. Civil society space is the third most improved, up 34.0 points to 76.8 and 11th of 54. The expression measures did not move with them. Media freedom stands at 56.7 out of 100, 22nd of 54 and up 1.3 points over the decade. Freedom of expression and belief stands at 71.6, 18th of 54 and down 1.1 points. All are expert-coded assessments with a 2023 data year, which precedes the 2025 cyber laws. A civil-society digital-rights index released on 28 October 2026 scored Zambia 37 of 60 for 2025, down from 40, and sixth of 29 countries.

Zambia has a central citizen feedback and grievance-redress platform for public services, with five of the six mechanisms the GovTech Maturity Index tracks in place (2025). The channels exist and are partial. A government e-participation platform also carries citizens' petitions. Three of the five further participation mechanisms the index tracks are absent. The cyber bills show how far consultation reaches. They were deferred at Second Reading in December 2024 for talks with civil society, and returned to the House on 25 March 2025. In March 2025 the ministry said 98% of the concerns raised had been resolved. Civil society said many of its key recommendations were not adopted. A right to information law is in force and rated effective (2025).

The Human Rights Commission raised the alarm over hate speech and online abuse ahead of the August 2026 vote. On 11 September 2026 the Lusaka Magistrates' Court convicted a vlogger under the Cyber Security and Cyber Crimes Act No. 2 of 2021. The offence was passing to a WhatsApp group a screenshot making allegations about a presidential aide. A ZNBC journalist held from 17 July 2026 on cybercrime and sedition charges was freed after 14 days when the Lusaka High Court ruled his detention unconstitutional. MISA Zambia launched its 2025 Digital Rights Report on 30 July 2026.

Finance

Financial sustainability

Zambia's 2026 budget carries a K1 billion World Bank grant for the Digital Zambia Acceleration Project inside the Smart Zambia Institute's standards and regulation line, where the 2025 budget footnoted no donor money against any digital line; the project's grant window runs to 2030.

About 66 percent of the K2.93 billion appropriated on the digital lines for 2026 is the state's own money, the rest being that grant.

The lines that keep systems running are on the state's own votes: the 2026 budget gives the institute K119.7 million for ICT infrastructure and K102.7 million for ICT security, and the finance ministry K40.8 million for SAP licences, the same sum as in 2025. Money voted is not all spent. In 2025 the institute's standards and regulation line spent K152.3 million of K288.0 million authorised, and the technology ministry's ICT infrastructure line K107.7 million of K204.1 million, while ICT security spent K114.3 million of K115.8 million.

Own-source financing of connectivity is in law. Regulations of 2012 require licensees to pay into the Universal Access Fund at a rate not exceeding 1.5 percent of gross annual turnover, capped at half their annual operating fees and payable in advance, with the Fund account run by ZICTA.

The state's infrastructure company earns its keep commercially. INFRATEL, wholly owned by the government through the Industrial Development Corporation, owns three Tier III data centres and more than 1,200 towers and is funded from tower services, data-centre revenue and investments; it leases shared tower sites to MTN under a five-year contract from January 2024 at a monthly fee per site, and was owed K362.9 million by customers at the end of 2024. The finance minister said in May 2026 that hundreds of new towers were going up through public-private partnerships, with no partnership terms published.

MoUs and other agreements

The Zambia Revenue Authority signed both a memorandum of understanding and a non-disclosure agreement with the International Centre for Tax and Development in July 2026, covering research, capacity building and innovation for data-driven tax administration and domestic resource mobilisation. The non-disclosure agreement is the binding half of it, and no value or duration is attached to either.

Huawei signed twice within a month. A memorandum of understanding with the government and the SMART Zambia Institute in May 2026 covers a National AI Data Centre, and a "sovereign AI" memorandum at the June 2026 Zambia Mobile Congress covers AI infrastructure, a sovereign cloud and a co-invested national data centre keeping critical data in the country. Both are memoranda rather than contracts, with no published value, co-investment split or delivery date.

The identity and payments work runs on the same kind of paper. Zambia has an identity memorandum with Ethiopia's National ID Program, drawing on a system chosen because it was built domestically, and a 2023 memorandum with the Centre for Digital Public Infrastructure at IIIT-Bangalore on the design of its DPI use cases. On payments, the government and the Payments Association of Zambia have agreed a compact to accelerate digital financial services and reduce cash dependency, with quarterly regulatory coordination between the Ministry of Technology and Science, the Bank of Zambia and ZICTA, and the fintech Zoyk has entered a partnership with Visa to bridge local payment rails to global networks for SADC cross-border trade. None of these is a procurement, and none of them commits either side to a sum.

New investments

Chinese policy banks are much the largest source of external digital finance: four commitments from China Eximbank and China Development Bank worth about US$878 million (2015-2026). That is nearly three-quarters of the roughly US$1.22 billion recorded across 116 commitments (November 2026). IFC and MIGA follow with US$105 million between them, the World Bank with US$103 million and British International Investment with US$66 million (2015-2026). It is overwhelmingly debt — six loans or buyer's credits worth about US$956 million against 108 grants worth about US$171 million — and connectivity takes about US$876 million, with US$178 million going to a single urban surveillance loan (2015-2026).

A US$65.5 million China Eximbank concessional loan of March 2015 covered a Huawei contract in full for Phase I of Smart Zambia. It delivered the three government data centres, in Lusaka and at Zamtel's Roma and Kitwe switches. A US$302 million China Eximbank concessional loan for communication towers followed in August 2017. The largest single commitment dates from December 2017: a China Eximbank credit against a US$392 million Huawei contract to extend ZESCO's national fibre backbone. A former ZESCO board chairman says he was pressed to proceed with the deal.

The World Bank's Digital Zambia Acceleration Project was approved in 2025 with a window to 2030. Its IDA grant is committed but not yet disbursing. IFC lent US$75 million to IHS Zambia in 2021 to refinance, not to build. MIGA's US$30 million guarantee insures a private-equity stake in Airtel Money's Zambian arm, not the wallet (2022). Sida committed SEK 25.1 million and SEK 16.9 million over 2020 to 2025 for financial inclusion and digital finance.

K255 million was approved in May 2026 for digital infrastructure and network quality, rural areas first. The Smart Zambia Institute asks private investors to fill the gap. The regional programme's report for January to June 2026 records a second market engagement for the digital acceleration project on 22 April 2026. It covered 300 cell towers, 2,000 km of fibre and connectivity for 500 public institutions.

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

ZICTA revoked the network and service licences of Beeline Telecoms, the fourth mobile network operator, with effect from 14 September 2026. Beeline was licensed in 2021 and had traded as ZedMobile since 2024. ZICTA cited missed rollout, quality-of-service and fee obligations.

4G signal reached 91.2 percent of Zambians in 2023. 27.0 million mobile connections were active in late 2026, equivalent to 119 percent of the population, a count of SIM cards, not of people. 6.73 million people used the internet at the end of 2026, 29.7 percent of the population. A low-consumption mobile data and voice basket cost 3.5 percent of GNI per capita in 2022. The technology minister said 40 of 152 towers in a rollout with Airtel and IHS were operational in September 2025, the rest due by end-November 2025.

Zambia's mobile communications score rose 35.6 points over 2014-2023 to 80.1 out of 100, eleventh of 54 African states. It was the country's second most improved indicator of 96. The measure combining internet use, computer access and affordability reached only 34.0, twenty-fifth of 54, on a gain less than half as large (2023). Public perception of infrastructure fell 18.7 points to 41.3 out of 100 (2023). An 11.0-point administrative gain lifted the infrastructure sub-category to 42.8 (2023).

263 of 15,600 targeted government sites were on the Government Wide Area Network in December 2022. Twelve Western Province sites were reported complete in February 2025. SMART Zambia's provincial ICT officer said the network could not be used until ZAMTEL finished the last-mile links. By July 2026 every local authority and post office in North-Western Province had been connected over fibre, reaching schools and health facilities. GovLink, a government-owned private 4G network for public institutions, launched on 15 July 2026 with healthcare as its flagship use.

Domestic traffic meets at one exchange, LSK-IX in Lusaka, twelve peers over twelve connections and 76G of capacity, ten of the peers open (August 2024). Airtel Zambia is buying about 1,300 km of fibre from its related company Airtel Telesonic Zambia for K324.9 million (September 2026).

Data Storage

Section 70(1) of the Data Protection Act No. 3 of 2021 requires controllers and processors to store and process personal and sensitive personal data on servers or in data centres in the country, subject to limited exceptions. In September 2025 there was no direct cloud on-ramp for AWS, Azure or Google Cloud. Johannesburg is the nearest hyperscale hub, reached at latency above 100 milliseconds. The commercial market is small and telco-dominated, limited to Tier II facilities such as Paratus's carrier-neutral site and the state-linked National Data Centre operated by Infratel. The first more advanced entrant, Africa Data Centres, was not expected before 2026-27. Aggregate commercial capacity was under 3 MW in May 2025, most facilities below 500 kW of live load. It was projected to at least double within five years.

The 2023 national e-government plan records government data centres at Tier I to Tier III, with a Tier III facility targeted at the Integrated Command and Control Centre site. It makes cloud computing infrastructure a pillar of the national digital transformation strategy. The government cloud itself was still a strategy, not a running platform, in 2025. Drought left towns without power for up to 72 hours at a stretch in October 2024.

Zambia, the SMART Zambia Institute and Huawei signed a memorandum of understanding in May 2026 for a National AI Data Centre. A sovereign-AI memorandum followed at the 2026 Zambia Mobile Congress, covering AI infrastructure, a sovereign cloud and a co-invested national data centre keeping critical data in-country. Huawei's 2015 contract built the national data centre in Lusaka and the two backup facilities behind it.

Energy

INFRATEL, the tower company, invested US$5 million in power resilience at mobile sites, deploying high-powered thermal batteries and upgrading solar plant (August 2026). A severe El Niño drought left one of the six turbines at Kariba running and 7 percent of its 1,080 MW generated (October 2024). Towns went up to 72 hours without power. The energy minister promised at least three hours a day once the country's only coal plant returned to full output. With load-shedding at 12 hours a day in June 2024, the main tower company reported generator run-time and fuel spending rising. It was moving its towers onto solar, hybrid power and battery banks. Renewables, overwhelmingly hydropower, account for close to 90 percent of Zambia's electricity generation (July 2025).

Electricity access reached 51.1 percent of the population in 2023, up from 47.8 percent in 2022 and 46.7 percent in 2021. The 2023 National Energy Access Survey records 80.3 percent in urban areas against 34.0 percent in rural areas, a gap of roughly 46 percentage points. The grid dominates and solar is the main alternative. In March 2026 the rural electrification authority published a master plan for 2025-2030, costing universal access by 2030 at about US$1.52 billion. Access to energy is the second worst-scoring of Zambia's 96 IIAG indicators at 26.7 out of 100, 35th of 54, despite a 7.0-point gain over 2014-2023. Off-grid electrification is an established policy line, with results-based financing for off-grid solar and mini-grids under the Beyond the Grid Fund. An Off-Grid Energy Taskforce and a mini-grid regulatory framework sit alongside it.

In 2025 the government's electronic health record was concentrated in facilities where solar power and internet connectivity had been secured. The Ministry of Health, the Smart Zambia Institute and UNDP agreed in October 2025 to extend solar power and connectivity together to rural and peri-urban health facilities that have neither. The Smart Zambia Institute has a partnership with ZESCO to extend digital access to schools, health facilities and public administrations. It reported in March 2026 that solar power had been provided to every council so they can operate around the clock.

Technical Capacity

Zambia's government systems are bought rather than built: of the eight whose software type is on record in 2025, six run commercial off-the-shelf products and only two — tax management, and the public investment management system under implementation — are custom.

In 2025 the human resource management information system and the payroll system were both commercial off-the-shelf products, with most HR services online or digitised. The mines ministry's cadastre licensing portal shut on 1 June 2025 when its contract with the South African developer Spatial Dimension expired. It reopened under interim measures. SMART Zambia Institute e-government developers have taken MOSIP training so the identity platform can be run in-country (2025). Development and capacity building still depend substantially on external partners. The institute said in March 2026 that the digital identity system would be developed by Zambians, with an international partner used only for technical support.

In 2025 a dedicated digital government institution was established, with machinery for coordinating government technology across ministries. A public sector innovation institution focuses on digital skills and public service innovation. A digital skills strategy and a training programme for public servants cover basic digital skills and data literacy, including fee-based training. The Smart Zambia Institute launched a decentralised version of that training for government staff in December 2025. A training-of-trainers programme for 80 mainly public-sector trainers followed in January 2026.

The University of Zambia's Department of Computing and Informatics dates from September 1998 and admitted its first computer science degree students in August 2000.

Cybersecurity

Zambia scored 92.58 out of 100 in the ITU's Global Cybersecurity Index 2024, up from 68.88 in 2020. That places it in the second of the index's five tiers, advancing, with Benin, South Africa and Togo, behind the five African states rated role-modelling. A National Cyber Security Policy was approved in January 2021. The Cyber Crimes Act No. 4 of 2025 was signed by President Hakainde Hichilema on 8 April 2025 and gazetted a week later. It criminalises unauthorised access, computer fraud, malware and the knowing spread of false information that damages another person's reputation. Its section 10 makes it an offence to record a private conversation without notifying the parties to it. The offence applies whether or not the recorder is one of them. Its investigatory powers are judicially gated throughout Part III, from search and seizure on a warrant to an ex-parte judicial order for real-time traffic-data collection.

The companion Cyber Security Act No. 3 of 2025 is a separate instrument. It places the Zambia Cyber Security Agency in the Office of the President, administering the Act under the President's general direction. Its remit has since widened. The Ministry of Home Affairs has placed AI-enabled human trafficking and online scams inside its response (July 2026).

Enforcement of the Cyber Crimes Act commenced on 18 July 2026, weeks before a general election. Parliament's parastatal committee found ZAMTEL's real-time billing system running on an unpatched database, its vendor advising against available security patches, with access controls as the interim safeguard (2025).

ZmCIRT, the national response team set up under the 2021 Act, took the first chair of the SADC regional response teams' steering committee in September 2026. ZICTA deactivated 43,500 SIM cards linked to mobile money fraud between January and June 2026, securing K19.8 million. The cases reach it through consumers reporting scammers on the *707# short code, under its consumer-protection mandate in the ICT Act. The Digital Zambia Acceleration Project (2025) provides for a centralised national Security Operations Centre. It also provides vulnerability assessment tools for the national Cybersecurity Incident Response Team.

DPI

Data Exchange

Zambia's Government Service Bus carried 290 deployed services (July 2024), and the World Bank's maturity index records the country as having both a government interoperability framework and a data-exchange platform in extensive use (2025).

What crosses it is more than a catalogue of endpoints. The land administration system runs on the bus and checks National Registration Cards against the Ministry of Home Affairs and retrieves company profiles from the company register while a land transaction is in progress (2023). The customs administration and the central financial management system exchange data with other government systems through the bus and through dedicated interfaces (2025). The Revenue Authority agreed in 2022 to interface its TaxOnline platform directly with the bus, and company, tax and pension registration data have been shared across three agencies through the One Stop Shop Integrated System since around 2015, longer than the bus itself. Civil registration is being wired in now: birth and death notifications are being automated end to end through the registration system's API manager so that population records update in real time (December 2025). Inside health, the patient record has exchanged laboratory orders and results with the DISA laboratory information system since June 2024.

The reach stops well short of the whole state. Government's own electronic government plan calls the platform landscape fragmented, interoperability partial and ICT systems still built in silos (2023); the identity estate remains spread across agencies collecting their own fingerprints, facial images and iris scans for their own services, with its integration a project commitment rather than a finished one (2025); and the electoral register is not connected to the exchange at all (June 2025). The data interoperability standards are mandatory for every ministry, province and spending agency (2024), and officials pushing the amendment of the Electronic Government Act name fragmented, vendor-locked systems as a governance and legal problem rather than a technical one (January 2026).

Digital Identity and CRVS

By 2026 the system carrying Zambia's digital identity had been deployed in 11 of the country's 116 districts. Around 1.3 million people were enrolled on it, and more than 500,000 births and deaths recorded (December 2025).

The World Bank operation carrying the work targets 4 million people holding a digitally verifiable identity by October 2030. Its baseline was 1.5 million in December 2022. In July 2026 the implementing institute still maintained that digital identity cards will issue by the end of 2026. The registrar general puts population-scale coverage at the end of 2027 (June 2025). Responsibility is split between the registration department, which issues the credential, and the institute, which owns the system.

15 percent of adults were registered biometrically in September 2025. The paper card is the legally recognised proof of identity. The great majority of adults hold one (2024). Registration is mandatory from age 16, with no earlier enrolment pathway.

In 2024 the National Registration Act, on which the credential rests with the Electronic Government Act and the Data Protection Act, was flagged as needing to be brought into line with digital identity standards. The Auditor General found that the vendor of the register's 2020 biometric upgrade still had access to the live database in August 2024, with no agreement authorising it. Zambian civil society organisations report that government does not explain what the biometric data required for services is collected for, or how it is shared (October 2025).

In March 2026 the institute was procuring an international system integrator for a MOSIP-based system integrated with civil registration. The initial goal is 2 million digital IDs (March 2026). Re-evaluation of the bids to deploy and integrate the identity and civil registration modules was complete and awaiting the lender's review at June 2026. Enrolment is free of charge. It collects name, date of birth, address, gender, ten fingerprints and a photograph, expressly excluding ethnic and other sensitive data. Refugees are being enrolled into the citizens' register, not a parallel one. UNHCR's registration database is transferring to the Ministry of Home Affairs under the same project (September 2024).

Digital Payments and Fintech

The Bank of Zambia counted 5.08 billion payment transactions worth K5.1 trillion in 2025. Volume rose 24.7 percent on 2024 and value fell 5.4 percent. 22.1 million mobile money wallets were registered by the end of 2025. Mobile money reached 76.1 percent of adults in the Bank of Zambia's FinScope 2025 survey, up from 58.4 percent in 2020. The payments rail runs well ahead of the foundational identity system meant to underpin it (2025).

In 2023 the National Financial Switch was operated by the Zambia Electronic Clearing House under central bank oversight. It was live nationally for instant payments between banks, non-bank financial institutions and payment service providers. MTN Mobile Money, Airtel Money and Zamtel Kwacha were integrated as members for person-to-person transfer. The switch now also carries wallet-to-bank, bank-to-wallet and merchant payments, with an industry QR project under way (July 2026). Cheques are being phased out.

In June 2026 the community development ministry said 92 percent of social cash transfer households were paid through mobile money or bank accounts. In 2021 urban payments were moving to payment service providers, and rural payments were still made by hand.

Non-bank payment providers had no seat on the clearing house board in 2023, and put their views through an association. Instant low-value transfers from South Africa went live on the regional TCIB scheme in February 2024, the scheme's first corridor. COMESA's retail payment platform is to pilot live transactions between Malawi and Zambia (November 2026). The interbank system's 2024 rules provide for the central bank to process Revenue Authority transactions. They point to integration with the financial management system and the treasury single account. That account is in use across ministries, departments and agencies, settling over both gross-settlement and fast payment rails (2025).

Access to banking services scores 35.9 out of 100 on 2023 data, 11th of 54 African states.

The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission resolved 1,477 consumer complaints in 2024 on its own count. 13.60 percent were in ICT, chiefly mobile-money transactions not reversed. The central bank is looking to create an independent ombudsman office for consumers (2025).

Registries

Birth registration stood at about 14 percent in 2024, against a government goal of half by 2026. The registration department has digitised close to 81 percent of the legacy civil registration records (2024). Civil registration is the seventh most deteriorated indicator in Zambia's Ibrahim Index profile, down 12.5 points over 2014-2023 to 62.5 out of 100.

Of an estimated 1.2 million numbered properties, fewer than 450,000 held registered title deeds in January 2026. The cadastre is outdated and incomplete, with council valuation rolls behind it (2024). Lusaka's roll was last completed in 2019 and covers 82,265 properties. The land platform is fully digital, land applications running through the government service bus, not over a counter (2024). The Auditor General's report for 2025 found 982 property records from the predecessor system missing after migration. Users report outages lasting weeks (October 2026).

9,163 new companies were registered between January and May 2026. The business register offers online incorporation and a public company search. Companies formed since 2020 get a taxpayer identification number automatically once registration completes. The electoral roll is digital and inspectable by USSD and online. 8.79 million voters were certified for the August 2026 elections, 53.1 percent of them women. The roll is built on possession of the paper registration card and searched by its number.

In 2023 the social protection system was integrated in real time with a payment gateway connecting nine providers and banks. The 2025 National Social Protection Policy says the system covers all 116 districts but is not fully operational. Beneficiaries are checked against their registration card at the point of payment, which the system uses to find duplicate records across districts (2025). A unified National Social Registry was approved by Cabinet in April 2025 and is still in early implementation. In 2023 there was no national address register, the addressing project was still piloting street naming and no addressing policy had been approved. Councils kept disjointed address records.

Sectoral management information systems

SmartCare Pro, the national electronic health record, was deployed in more than 2,000 health facilities by July 2026. It is among the first systems moving onto the government's private 4G network. That is about 80 percent of facilities on the Smart Zambia Institute's count that month. About 90 percent of health facilities report counts, not patient records, into the national DHIS2 platform for disease surveillance (2025). The national education management information system still depends chiefly on periodic school census returns. Monthly digital reporting has been demonstrated in a single district (2025). Equipment for a redesigned system was installed at the education ministry's headquarters and in every province by June 2026. The redesigned system is expected in operation by May 2027.

All ten core back-office systems the GovTech Maturity Index tracks are in place, nine of them in use — financial management, the treasury single account, tax, customs, human resources, payroll, social insurance, electronic procurement and public debt — with public investment management still being implemented (2025). Of the eight whose software type is recorded, six are commercial off-the-shelf products; only the tax system and the public investment system are custom-built.

In 2025 the financial management system was extensively used at central government level, covering treasury execution and budget preparation. It exchanges data with other systems through dedicated interfaces and the service bus, and customs is connected the same two ways. The Auditor General found the payroll system on an unsupported Oracle database with no interface to the financial management system (2025 accounts). The health record system now accepts a fingerprint checked against the national identity system in place of a physical card, though not everywhere and not as a requirement (2026).

The judiciary launched an electronic case management system in October 2026. It pilots 24-hour e-filing, automated court calendars and case notifications in four registries at the Lusaka and Kitwe High Courts before a nationwide rollout. The chief justice opened a training-of-trainers workshop for it on 2 November 2026.

Other GovTech and e-Gov

The national services portal carried 572 services across 52 providers at 30 September 2026, on the finance minister's account. More than 400 government services — passports, government rent and road tax among them — were onboarded to the state's electronic payment gateway (July 2026). They are payable by bank transfer, card, mobile money or cash. Sign-in runs through ZamPass, the national authentication and access control service, with companion mobile applications (2026). The national service portal is rated at transactional maturity, meaning services can be completed through it and not only read about (2025). In the 2025 national ICT survey, disseminated in November 2026, 7.7 per cent of respondents knew of ZamPortal.

The past two years have added particular transactions rather than a general capability: road transport services — driving licences, road tax, vehicle registration — have been live on the service bus and ZamPortal since at least September 2024. The Ministry of Labour had 29 services on ZamPortal by April 2025. An online passport application and payment system opened in January 2026, with more than a thousand passports processed at that point. A property management system for rentals, valuation and housing services followed in May 2026. A contract tracking system for government contract approvals launched in July 2026.

Five of the seven citizen-facing channels the GovTech Maturity Index tracks are running — the national service portal, the tax portal, the social protection portal, the e-participation platform and the feedback and grievance platform. Two are absent: a government jobs portal and an open data portal (2025).

Zambia ranked 130th of 193 states on the UN e-government index in 2024, scoring 0.5424, with online services the weaker half of that score at 0.4858. The information ministry launched an integrated media reporting system. A portal for the public service funeral scheme was due to roll out in the first week of October 2026. The meteorological department and the agriculture ministry are building a national digital climate advisory service that sends farmers localised forecast and agronomic advice by text message (2026).

Digitalisation

Digitalisation of sub-national government

Councils became the first sub-national tier on Zambia's Government Services Bus in June 2026. Eight local authorities collected roughly K21.7 million in property rates, business levies and permits in the month after the rollout (July 2026). That is 8 of 116 councils on the state payment rail, which the Accountant General frames as revenue modernisation, not convenience (July 2026). Lusaka describes its own platform's purpose as minimising revenue leakage so it can mobilise more own-source resources (August 2026). The local government ministry had ordered every council in May 2025 to move from manual to digital collection and end cash handling. Its permanent secretary said most councils collected under half of their potential revenue.

Lusaka's e-Council platform lets residents and businesses transact without attending council offices (August 2026). It was introduced through the Ministry of Local Government and Rural Development and integrated onto the national Government Services Bus, not built as a standalone council portal. Lusaka was the first council onto the national services portal (May 2026). The revenue system behind it entered a proof-of-concept phase with a first group of councils in March 2026. It covers property tax, business and market levies, health permits and fire certificates before extension to the rest. A Constituency Development Fund system was in data entry across councils, to track spending transaction by transaction (March 2026).

In 2025 the government wide area network had been extended to all 116 local authorities. In Western Province, completed council and district office sites were still waiting on last-mile links before the network could be used (February 2025). The national financial management system reaches central government and not the levels beneath it (2025). Council valuation rolls are outdated, and paper records, ledgers and receipt books still run through the property tax cycle (2024).

Rural digital data capture

Smart Zambia installed Starlink kits in Moomba Chiefdom in the Kazungula Game Management Area, connecting one rural health clinic and two primary schools to online government services (September 2025). In November 2026 it began deploying 751 Starlink kits, financed by the Global Fund, to health facilities in all ten provinces. The national patient record had reached more than 2,000 health facilities by July 2026. It runs only where power and an internet connection have been secured. Police case books away from headquarters are kept in paper form. The school system still runs chiefly on periodic census returns, not facility-level digital reporting. Networking equipment began reaching 40 police stations in early 2026. No case-recording system has been found running on it.

ZICTA's Universal Access and Service Fund subsidises towers and last-mile fibre where operators see no return (September 2025). Its installed projects span Copperbelt and North-Western provinces. The programme is extending into Muchinga, Northern and Luapula, including ICT provision at Chinsali Special School (June 2026). Rural electricity access is far below the urban rate (2023). Poor mobile service in parts of the Copperbelt has cut residents off from social cash transfers and farmer input payments (February 2026). Electronic collection by councils has yet to reach rural districts (July 2026).

Rural Economy is the worst-scoring of the Ibrahim Index's 16 sub-categories for Zambia at 36.7 out of 100, 41st of 54 (2023). It fell 18.4 points over 2014-2023, and is the one sub-category the index declines to assign a trend. Two access measures inside it rose: rural market access by 11.8 points to 31.5, and rural land and water access by 12.9 points to 46.1. Market access remains among the country's ten worst-scoring indicators (2023).

Technology

AI

Zambia's National Artificial Intelligence Strategy describes the country's AI field as fragmented, with no centralised platform for AI data aggregation or model deployment (2024). It finds that the government data exchange incorporates no AI or advanced analytics for data quality, fraud detection or prediction. Policy trackers treat the 2024-2026 strategy as the current instrument for cloud services, broadband and digital payments, there being no dedicated policy for any of the three. At the June 2025 launch of the science, technology and innovation policy the minister allocated K8 million to AI and emerging technologies. The money is for regulatory frameworks, ethical guidelines, education and awareness.

The social cash transfer payment system added AI-based card scanning and biometric matching in a 2025 pilot. The system verifies beneficiaries against their national registration card. In October 2026 the technology ministry was piloting Njila.ai, an AI career-guidance platform in technical and vocational training expected to reach more than 17,000 young people. UNDP and the Lusaka water utility launched a pilot in October 2026 that brings the utility's production, metering, billing and network data into one platform. The pilot uses AI to locate non-revenue water and runs on an in-country compute node at the University of Zambia. The partnership memorandum was still to be signed. In health, a Finland-backed pilot of remote diagnostics and an AI drug-interaction tool reached 20 health institutions in 2025. The statistics agency tested a language-model pipeline that coded Labour Force Survey occupations and industries 10 to 17 points more accurately than enumerators (October 2025).

UNDP reports GPU capacity procured, installed and activated with public institutions in Zambia, oriented to inference (September 2026). Its lead use is the analysis of critical-minerals data with the mines ministry, for which data access was still to be confirmed. A national AI data centre rests on a memorandum of understanding signed with Huawei and the SMART Zambia Institute in May 2026. Lusaka hosted a COMESA and World Bank regional AI workshop in July 2026, opened as a first step toward a regional AI strategy. The workshop was framed against policy fragmentation across the bloc.

ICT Industry

The domestic sector has a listed tier as well as a contracting one: Dot Com Zambia, the fintech behind eToll and eLevy, is quoted on the Lusaka Securities Exchange (May 2026). Government digitisation work itself sits with the SMART Zambia Institute's own e-Government Division and with local ICT firms, with public services consolidated behind a single ZamPortal and ZamPay front end.

Government measures supporting technology startups were recorded as in place in 2025. The wider regulatory setting is one of Zambia's stronger governance results rather than a technology-specific one: business and competition regulation scored 81.0 out of 100 on the Ibrahim Index, sixth of 54 countries and up 9.8 points over the decade to 2023.

Innovation ecosystem

Team Zambia reached the global finals of the 2026 AI for Good Global Summit and Robotics for Good Youth Challenge in Geneva, among nearly 50 participating countries, and has been invited to the 2027 summit (July 2026). The delegation came out of a nine-month identification and mentoring cycle run by E-MARK Innovation Hub, a private hub rather than a state programme. On the standard comparative measure the system still sits low, 112th in WIPO's Global Innovation Index 2025, 90th on innovation inputs and 128th on outputs.

The hub layer is small but active, anchored by BongoHive in Lusaka alongside Ehub Zambia and Jacaranda Hub, all listed in the AfriLabs pan-African directory, with BongoHive running incubation and acceleration programmes including AI-focused cohorts (2025). Inside government there is a public sector innovation strategy with a programme attached, and an established public sector innovation institution whose focus areas are digital skills and public service innovation (2025).

Capital is the thin part. Zambia's startup funding was low in both volume and value in 2024, a year in which one Zambian startup raised a US$400,000 pre-seed round. Public money comes through a Technology Business Development Fund run by the National Technology Business Centre (2026), and the government said in October 2025 that it had finalised an Innovation and Inventors Bill creating a venture capital fund to be run independently of government; no capitalisation figure is published for either. The Ministry of Technology and Science says it will advance the field through a Startup Bill, which has no published text (July 2026). Firms are building in the meantime, and not always along the lines donors expect: the agritech Green Giraffe argues that smallholders already know soil, pests and weather and lack price and demand information, and so signs purchase contracts with farmers before planting rather than pushing advisory content afterwards (July 2026).

Capacity

Literacy

The statistics agency's 2025 national ICT survey found 17.7 percent of Zambians aged ten and over able to use a computer, up from 11.8 percent in 2022 and 6.8 percent in 2018. Fewer than one computer user in twenty performs advanced tasks such as programming (2025). The 2022 census recorded internet use at 15% of the population aged five and above. ZICTA's Universal Access and Service Fund reached Muchinga, Northern and Luapula provinces in 2026, including ICT provision at Chinsali Special School. The Smart Zambia Institute launched a three-week course for girls in cybersecurity, AI and data science with the Cisco Networking Academy in April 2025. It was designed to reach every province.

Education is Zambia's second best-scoring sub-category in the Ibrahim Index, at 65.2 out of 100 and 8th of 54 (2023), one of only two placing in the continental top ten. Education Enrolment fell 8.8 points over 2014-2023 to 60.3 (10th), and Education Quality 4.0 points to 51.3 (15th). The sub-category still rose 4.8 points over that decade. Human Resources in Education, at 88.3 and 19th, is among the country's strongest indicators of any kind (2023).

Equality in Education, whether educational opportunity is spread evenly across social groups and regions, rose 11.3 points over 2014-2023 to 54.4 out of 100, 23rd of 54 (2023).

Training and skills

The Smart Zambia Institute launched a decentralised digital-skills training programme for government staff in December 2025. In 2025 Zambia had both a digital skills strategy and a digital skills programme for public servants. They cover basic digital skills and data literacy, with some of the training charged for.

A Zambian delegation reached the global finals of the 2026 AI for Good Robotics for Good Youth Challenge in Geneva, among nearly 50 competing countries. It came out of a nine-month identification and mentoring cycle run by E-MARK Innovation Hub, a private hub. In July 2026 the Zambia Cyber Security Agency urged journalists to learn the country's cybersecurity laws ahead of the general election. It presented the legislation as protection for media houses against ransomware and disruption.

The University of Zambia has taught computing through a dedicated Department of Computing and Informatics since 1998. It opened with a postgraduate diploma and admitted its first degree students in 2000. SMART Zambia e-government developers took MOSIP training so that the identity platform can be run in-country. Development and capacity building still lean substantially on external partners. The judiciary began training trainers for its electronic case management system on 2 November 2026. The World Bank's Digital Zambia Acceleration Project carries a digital skills and capabilities component within its 2025-2030 operation. A June 2026 memorandum with Huawei commits to training several thousand young Zambians through the company's global academy.

Research institutions

In July 2026 the Zambia Revenue Authority signed a memorandum of understanding, and a non-disclosure agreement, with the International Centre for Tax and Development. It covers research, capacity building and innovation for data-driven tax administration and domestic resource mobilisation.

Postgraduate computing programmes at the University of Zambia date from 1998. ZAMREN, the research and education network, connected 167 institutions in the first half of 2025, among them every university and college of education. The chief executive of E-MARK Innovation Hub called in July 2026 for stronger national investment in AI infrastructure, digital skills and innovation support. Zambia ranks 112th on WIPO's Global Innovation Index 2025, and 128th on its innovation outputs.

CIPESA and Bloggers of Zambia analysed the 2024 cyber bills for the Zambia CSO Coalition on Digital Rights in December 2024. Strathmore University's CIPIT, with Canadian IDRC funding, studied digital ID in Kenya and Zambia (September 2025). It met limited participation from Zambian government and private-sector bodies.

Inclusion

Access to services

Access to Public Services for Women scored 25.2 out of 100 for Zambia, 40th of 54 African states, after a rise of 10.3 points over 2014-2023. It is the lowest-scoring of all 96 indicators the Ibrahim Index measures for Zambia. The index's measure of whether public services reach all social groups and regions alike sits at 52.1 out of 100 and 16th of 54, up 12.0 points over the same decade.

Financial inclusion reached 80.1 percent in 2025, up from 69.4 percent in 2020, on the Bank of Zambia's FinScope survey. Mobile money carried the rise. Inclusion runs well ahead of the biometric identity enrolment meant to underpin it. Industry accounts in July 2026 put the remaining barrier down to product design, not access. Products are built around salaried income in an economy where much of the population earns seasonally.

In November 2026 the technology minister set a five-year target of 10 million more smartphones, against 12.4 million smartphone connections. The social cash transfer programme operates in every district (February 2026). Its register reaches only part of the extremely poor population it targets (2022).

The Bank of Zambia and ZICTA recognised the Refugee Certificate as valid identification for mobile money and SIM registration in 2017. A central bank survey of June 2024 found 17 of the 60 responding financial providers serving forcibly displaced people. In 2024 1,361 alien cards were issued, and 30% of displaced people held one. Most ask for the refugee identity card. The World Bank's refugee project enrols refugees into the national citizens' register, not a parallel one. Urban refugees without a valid refugee document can be blocked from registering their children's births (2025). 14 percent of births were registered in 2024, and six percent of the children registered hold a birth certificate (June 2025). The state operator launched a self-care application and a short-code digital financial service in October 2026.

Digital divides

Internet use reached 48.3 percent of urban residents against 13.2 percent of rural residents in the statistics agency's 2025 national ICT survey, in which 29.7 percent of individuals aged ten and over had internet access, up from 25.2 percent in 2022. A compiler's estimate shows the same slow pace: 199,000 internet users added in the year to October 2025, a rise of 2.8 percent that left the penetration rate itself unchanged — growth that keeps pace with the population rather than closing on it, so the share of the country outside the internet altogether is where it was a year earlier.

The sharpest line is rural. The Ibrahim Index scores the rural economy as the worst-performing and by a wide margin the most deteriorated of the sixteen sub-categories it measures for Zambia over 2014-2023 — the only one it declines to assign a trend to at all. Underneath that sits the electricity gap: the 2023 National Energy Access Survey found rural access running at well under half the urban rate, which sets a ceiling on what any connectivity or device programme can reach. The index's own measure of internet use, computer access and affordability has improved far more slowly over the decade than its mobile measure, which is the difference between a phone signal and a usable connection.

Gender and skill are the other two lines. The 2018 measurement put women's internet use below men's, with phone ownership converging; the 2025 survey still separates the sexes by 4.5 points in internet use, 27.7 against 32.2 percent, while smartphone ownership is near parity at 39.2 and 40.7 percent (2025) — device access and use are separating. Place splits devices far more than sex does: smartphone ownership runs at 55.3 percent in towns against 21.0 percent in the countryside (2025).

What is missing is the means to see any of this clearly. Zambia's gender, education, health and food-security statistics score worse for openness in the 2024 Open Data Inventory than its population and price statistics do — the categories a targeting programme would need are the ones hardest to obtain.

Data

National statistics

Capacity of the Statistical System gained 10.2 points over 2014-2023 to 58.5 out of 100 and 16th of 54. That came against falls in civil registration, tax and revenue mobilisation and budgetary and financial management. The public administration sub-category around it went backwards over the same decade.

In the ten years to 2023 Zambia had run a population census, but no agricultural census and no business census within twenty. Across that decade it fielded three or more agricultural and labour surveys, two household surveys and two health surveys, but no business survey. The enterprise economy is counted only through a companies register not matched to any measure of the business base it covers. An economic establishment census was earmarked for August to September 2025 to underpin the rebasing of GDP, and no result from it has been found. The 2022 census counted 19,693,423 people on 8 September 2022 in its revised figures of February 2025. Its national analytical report was published in August 2025.

Data sources is the lowest of the five pillars in the World Bank's Statistical Performance Indicators, in the 20-49% band (2023). Data services and data infrastructure sit at 50-69%, data products at 70-89% and data use above 89% (2023).

Civil registration is among Zambia's most deteriorated Ibrahim Index indicators over 2014-2023. Birth registration remains one of the lowest rates in Africa. The vital statistics report for 2017, published in March 2020, put birth registration coverage at 19.2% and death registration completeness at 20.6%. Most of the legacy registration record has been digitised.

Open data

In 2025 Zambia had no government open data portal, so the measures that depend on one, such as catalogue coverage and update frequency, were recorded as not applicable. A right to information law is in force and rated effective.

Disclosure of Public Records stood at 34.6 out of 100, 22nd of 54 and down 2.1 points over 2014-2023. Accessibility of Public Records stood at 43.3, 14th of 54 and down 1.7 points. Access to public information is the weakest part of Zambia's Ibrahim Index accountability score. There is no data governance body and no data governance strategy for government (2025).

The pattern repeats: documentation of the systems government runs — financial management, tax and customs, the right-to-information regime, digital skills and public sector innovation — is recorded again and again as existing but unpublished.

No category in Zambia's 2024 Open Data Inventory assessment scores above 6 out of 10. Balance of payments, price indexes and population and vital statistics each reach 6, and agriculture 5.5. Five of the 23 categories score zero: money and banking, the digital economy, energy, pollution, and crime and justice. Core economic statistics sit low: international trade 5, national accounts and labour 4 each, government finance 3.

The finance ministry, the statistics agency and UNICEF launched a national research repository in September 2026. It brings research and evaluations once kept in hard copy or scattered across institutions onto one platform, with no count of holdings or licence terms stated. The extractive industries transparency secretariat launched an online portal of mining revenue data in April 2024. The statistics agency offers most of its products free through its website and data portal, with commercial use under separate terms. The Bank of Zambia issues an annual report on the national payment systems.

Geospatial data

The ZNSDI GeoPortal, a national spatial data infrastructure portal, is to map rivers, wildlife areas and land boundaries in districts such as Mwinilunga (July 2026). Artisanal mining licences are then to be issued against them. No national space agency or Earth observation programme had been established as at August 2026. The agriculture ministry uses a satellite drought-monitoring portal launched in February 2023 with a water research institute. A national crop-condition monitor was launched during the 2023/24 drought, with provincial teams trained on it (August 2024). The statistics agency and WorldPop released a 100-metre gridded population dataset from the 2022 census preliminary counts in June 2024. It carries a CC BY 4.0 licence. The meteorological department hosted a regional nowcasting testbed with Mozambique and South Africa from 30 January 2025. Forecasts and nowcasts went out every two hours.

Geopolitics

US / hyperscaler activities

Zambia and the United States signed a five-year health financing memorandum on 8 October 2026, which the State Department put at US$2.49bn, US$1.52bn of it from the United States and US$975m from Zambia. The text released that day by the advocacy group Health GAP as the final memorandum leaves out the earlier draft's clause tying the money to a minerals compact and its 25-year specimen-sharing term. In that text the two governments plan a specimen sharing arrangement for pathogens with epidemic potential, within seven days of detection, and a data sharing arrangement on the memorandum's performance, and the United States plans about US$32.2m for the SmartCare Pro patient record over the term, while Zambia commits to connect all 3,216 government health facilities to it by the end of 2030. A Zambian outlet reported a data sharing agreement signed beside the health memorandum, limited to aggregated and de-identified programme data (October 2026).

In April 2025 the US Embassy in Lusaka issued a security alert to its own citizens warning that Zambia's new cyber law required the interception and surveillance of electronic communications in the country. Zambia's Minister of Home Affairs and Internal Security, Jack Mwiimbu, rejected that reading, saying the laws are meant to protect citizens. The target of a national digital ID by the end of 2026 has been argued for domestically as a route off a US blacklist (December 2025).

China activities

Zambia's sovereign AI and cloud programme runs through Huawei (June 2026). Huawei is the same vendor whose 2015 contract built the country's three government data centres. What the sovereignty push localises is where the data sits, not who builds and runs the platform underneath it.

That programme rests on memoranda rather than contracts: SMART Zambia and Huawei signed a sovereign AI memorandum at the 2026 Zambia Mobile Congress (June 2026). It covers AI infrastructure, a sovereign cloud, digital public infrastructure for 25 ministries and a co-invested national data centre holding critical data in-country. It came weeks after a separate memorandum with Huawei for a National AI Data Centre (May 2026). Neither has been given a value or a delivery date. The June memorandum also carries a training commitment through the Huawei Global Academy. On 9 November 2026 the technology ministry asked China, Huawei included, to help build an AI-enabled data centre. The two sides agreed to meet within weeks on a roadmap.

The built stock behind the memoranda is a decade old: from 2016, Phase I of the Smart Zambia National ICT Development Project, financed by a China Eximbank concessional loan against a Huawei contract, delivered the country's three government data centres. They are the main national centre in Lusaka, with backup facilities at Zamtel's Roma and Kitwe switch offices. Phase I also delivered an ICT talent training centre. Phase II financed a Huawei build of communication towers and wireless stations in unserved and underserved areas. It was most of the way disbursed by July 2022. A further Eximbank credit for a Huawei extension of the ZESCO optical fibre backbone had not begun disbursing at that date. Chinese lending also stands behind the ZTE contract to install CCTV across Lusaka's streets, highways and bus stations for the Ministry of Home Affairs.

Chinese policy banks are the largest source of external digital finance to Zambia on record (2025), across four commitments and well ahead of the World Bank and every other financier.

EU activities

No European-built digital infrastructure, no European-supplied government platform and no standing EU bilateral digital agreement were on Zambia's record as at August 2026.

Gulf/UAE activities

No Gulf state or Gulf-based operator had built, financed or supplied digital infrastructure in Zambia, and no Gulf bilateral digital agreement was in force, as at August 2026.

India activities

Senior Zambian officials from the digital sector and line ministries joined a UNDP-hosted learning mission to India on digital public infrastructure, received in Bengaluru on 1 September 2026 alongside delegations from Ethiopia, Malawi, Rwanda, Sierra Leone and the African Union Commission; no agreement or financing was announced from it. Zambia signed a memorandum of understanding in 2023 with the Centre for Digital Public Infrastructure at IIIT-Bangalore to support the design of its digital public infrastructure use cases — help with how the systems are shaped, not money, equipment or a stack the government now runs on. No Indian-financed infrastructure or supplied platform was on Zambia's record as at October 2026.

Digital sovereignty

Zambia launched its national public key infrastructure in September 2026, its master keys held offline and split among several custodians so that no single body controls the country's root of trust, built for ZICTA by South Africa's Altron Security under a contract won three years earlier. The certificates it issues take legal effect under Zambia's electronic transactions law, and Zambia sits in the Africa PKI Forum working towards cross-border recognition.

Beyond the statutory rule that keeps personal data in the country, the state has moved on software. Cabinet directed ministries in June 2026 to adopt open-source technology, launching a national spatial data portal built on open-source software to avoid long-term vendor lock-in, and the Smart Zambia coordinator has said the country kept full national ownership of the 12 million patient records consolidated into its SmartCare Pro platform (August 2026). The government's own hosting is state-owned: INFRATEL, held through the Industrial Development Corporation, owns and operates three Tier III data centres in Lusaka and Kitwe (2024). Whether the state holds the source code and operating skills for the government data centre systems Huawei built, or can exit their support arrangements, was not on record as at September 2026.

The AI layer is being negotiated on stated conditions. At an August 2026 seminar on AI cooperation with China, the Smart Zambia coordinator called for regulatory sovereignty and for cooperation that does not impose a one-size-fits-all approach, and the technology ministry's permanent secretary named limited computing infrastructure as the main barrier to adoption. The terms on which the May and June 2026 memoranda with Huawei for a national AI data centre and a sovereign cloud govern data jurisdiction, portability and exit had not been published as at September 2026.
