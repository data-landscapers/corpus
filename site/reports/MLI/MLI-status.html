This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

In 2025 the government acknowledged that the Mali Numérique 2020 strategy had not been implemented successfully. A replacement National Digital Economy Strategy for 2024-2028 was still under development. Until one is adopted, the 2015 national strategy for the development of the digital economy covers the field. It reaches across broadband, universal access, cybersecurity, data centres, cloud, data protection, digital identification, payments, signatures, e-commerce and e-waste.

The Council of Ministers approved a national cybersecurity strategy for 2026-2030 in December 2025. A national financial inclusion strategy adopted in June 2022 targets rural populations, women, youth and smaller enterprises.

Mali scored 43.2 out of 100 for overall governance in the 2024 Ibrahim Index, 37th of 54 African states (2023). The African average was 49.3. Mali lost 4.6 points over 2014-2023, while the African average rose 1.0 point. Across the index's 96 indicators it improved on 43, declined on 51 and scored below the African average on 59. Its most improved categories are Human Development and Foundations for Economic Opportunity. The second covers public administration, infrastructure, the business environment and the rural economy, and scored 48.1 out of 100, 29th of 54, up 1.9 points over the decade.

Legislation and regulation

A decree of 25 February 2026 approved the national cybersecurity strategy and its 2026-2030 action plan. It was gazetted on 6 March 2026 without the annexed strategy text. Ordonnance n° 2011-023/P-RM of 28 September 2011 governs telecommunications, ICT and posts, including universal access. Loi n° 2016-012 of 6 May 2016 covers electronic transactions, exchanges and services. Loi n° 2019-056 of 2019 represses cybercrime. Loi n° 2016-005 of 24 February 2016 governs public statistics.

On 12 March 2026 the telecoms regulator reiterated the prohibition on importing, selling or using non-homologated Starlink and WiFi Zone equipment, and warned of sanctions. The reiteration rests on the existing homologation regime and cites no article numbers.

A justice ministry communiqué of 29 July 2026 lists penal offences already in force, creates no new offence and notifies no platform. It treats the staging, filming, publication and commentary of images of child depravity as offences falling "notamment de la cybercriminalité".

Law No. 2013-015 of 21 May 2013 restricts transfers of personal data to countries judged to offer adequate protection. It sets no general requirement to store data inside the country. A law of 13 December 2024 criminalises data breaches, and the constitution of 23 July 2023 recognises personal-data protection.

In 2025 no consolidated digital identity act was in force. The NINA number and the biometric card rest on Law No. 06-040 of 11 August 2006, Law No. 06-024 of 2006 on civil status and Decree No. 2022-0639/PT-RM.

Each of Mali's 2026 moves on the digital estate — an equipment reminder to importers and users, an instruction to prosecutors, a conviction in the cybercrime court — applies law already in force, and none of them creates a new statute.

Data protection

In October 2025 Mali's data protection authority had fined a Bamako clinic 5 million FCFA for obstructing a verification mission. It had issued more than 100 formal notices to data controllers. It had examined some 2,000 declaratory files from organisations registering their processing. In December 2025 the APDP opened a regional antenna in Ségou. By September 2026 it had regional offices at Kayes, Sikasso, Ségou and Mopti. On 23 September 2026 it signed a cooperation agreement with Togo's authority on information exchange, complaints, compliance checks and staff training.

The authority has operated since 2016 as an independent body of 15 members serving seven-year terms. It is funded from the state budget and may investigate complaints, demand evidence and impose sanctions. It was established under Law No. 2013-015 of 21 May 2013.

In September 2026 an AI provider's misuse report described Lakana 360, a platform built for the state security agency that monitors about 25 million SIM cards across all three mobile operators. It captures call records, messages and voice traffic, and a warrant requirement was removed from one component. No response from the authority and no legal basis for the interception has been found.

In 2023 no mechanism let a Malian view the data held on them in the national identity database or see an access log. In 2025 redress over the NINA and the biometric card ran through the ordinary courts, with no identification-specific mechanism. Mali's digital freedom score fell 22.7 points over 2014-2023 to 38.5 out of 100, 40th of 54 African states.

The justice ministry's July 2026 communiqué calls on parents, educators, building owners, landlords, managers of furnished flats, and bar and hotel keepers to watch for and denounce the conduct it targets. It gives that duty to no telecommunications or content regulator. On 28 August 2026 the health insurance fund CANAM closed a staff session on personal-data protection, covering cyberattack, leakage and unauthorised access.

Regional collaboration

Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger severed their ECOWAS ties in 2024. Mali's exit took effect on 29 January 2025, the day the three launched a common biometric passport. In March 2026 the three shared an Alliance of Sahel States agreement on identity credentials. Burkina Faso and Niger have launched regional biometric identity cards under it.

Malian banks and other financial institutions are bound by BCEAO Instruction n° 003-03-2025 of 18 March 2025 on customer due diligence, applied across the eight UMOA states. The central bank's instant payment platform for the eight WAEMU states, Mali among them, was launched on 30 September 2025.

In March 2026 the WURI supervisory committee, the ECOWAS Commission and the World Bank validated a cross-border interoperability strategy for the programme's participating states. The Commission names Benin, Burkina Faso, Côte d'Ivoire, Guinea, Niger and Togo as WURI's implementing states, and not Mali.

On 23 July 2024 the confederation's customs directors-general signed a memorandum at Niamey to interconnect their systems, starting with Togo. A pilot link between Togolese and Malian customs opened on the Lomé–Bamako corridor on 10 March 2025. In October 2026 they and Togo's customs set 31 January 2027 for a technical unit supervising interconnected customs data. They set 1 January 2027 for the company lists behind a regional identification code.

On 18 November 2025 the three data protection authorities signed a roadmap for a coordination framework. A convention creating a Council of Sahel Regulators, signed by the three telecoms regulators, was reported on 26 September 2026. Its statutes and first work programme were still to be adopted. On 5 November 2026 the three states adopted a Year II digital roadmap setting up a confederal network operations centre by the end of 2028. On 2 September 2026 the communication ministry convened digital and media actors on a common confederation framework for social media, modelled on a Burkinabè text.

The 2010 ECOWAS Supplementary Act on personal data protection applies by its Article 3(3) to processing in a UEMOA or ECOWAS member state. It requires each member state to set up an independent data protection authority. Under Article 48 it enters into force on publication in each member state's gazette.

Standards

In January 2026 the board of the standards agency AMANORM was submitted a draft 2026 budget of CFA 243,306,303, against CFA 318,369,036 in 2025, a fall of about a quarter. The agency was created by ordonnance n° 2012-016/P-RM of 19 March 2012. It is the international standards organisation's member body for Mali. No standard adopted or certification issued by the agency has been found.

In 2025 Mali had no dedicated data exchange or interoperability strategy and no published implementation roadmap. In 2023 none of its core registers — commercial, civil registration, land, tax, address and electoral — connected to a national data exchange platform.

Règlement n° 15/2002/CM/UEMOA recognises electronic signatures for payment-system purposes, and provides for netting and settlement finality. On 26–27 March 2024 an ECOWAS and EU expert meeting formulated guidelines for a certified electronic signature and left a roadmap being defined.

Public debate and participation in policymaking

On 3 August 2026 Mali's cybercrime court sentenced Chahana Takiou, publication director of the newspaper 22 septembre, to 12 months' imprisonment with 6 suspended. The charge was atteinte au crédit de l'État à travers l'institution judiciaire. He was convicted over remarks at the Forum panafricain des médias in Bamako in June 2026. There he argued that journalists should be tried under the press-offence law, not the cybercrime law. The sentence was the one the prosecution had sought on 27 July 2026.

On 14 September 2026 the same tribunal sentenced Abdrahamane Keïta, publication director of Le Témoin, to two years in prison. He was convicted of regionalist remarks, undermining the credit of the state and spreading false information through an information system. The case arose from a remark about Kidal on Renouveau TV on 4 June 2026. He had been held since June, and his defence said it would appeal. In August 2026 the International Press Institute said the authorities were using cybercrime provisions to target journalists.

Mali's media freedom score fell 9.1 points over 2014-2023 to 53.5 out of 100, 30th of 54. Its freedom of expression and belief score fell 24.4 points to 57.6.

The justice ministry's July 2026 communiqué calls the acts it targets "importés d'ailleurs" and contrary to "nos valeurs sociétales". At the end of July 2026 the minister instructed prosecutors and judges to act with rigour against those spreading depraved content on social media.

In August 2026 Women Tech Mali launched Citoyens 2.0, an application for countering disinformation, with a campaign titled "Le vrai avant le viral". A representative of the communication and digital economy ministry endorsed it. He named institutions, media, content creators and ordinary users as the four actors a response to disinformation requires.

Finance

Financial sustainability

In July 2026 the AGEFAU board cut the universal access fund's 2026 budget from FCFA 41.15 billion to FCFA 27.89 billion. By July 2026 the agency had collected FCFA 23.18 billion and spent FCFA 3.19 billion. The government told the UN that the fund mobilised US$72 million in 2025 for network extension and telemedicine. Under four-year conventions with the education and employment ministries, it has committed to connect every secondary, technical and vocational school by the end of 2027.

The 2025 credit-opening order carries FCFA 163.5 billion of external financing across 46 projects, none of them a digital line. In 2025 it gives the ICT agency AGETIC FCFA 2.49 billion and the data-protection authority FCFA 2.06 billion. The finance ministry's tax and financial IT unit receives FCFA 1.56 billion in 2025 (FY2025).

All of the FCFA 20.5 billion of digital lines in the 2025 order is the state's own money, against FCFA 19.6 billion in 2024 (FY2025).

The 2026 budget bill proposes FCFA 636.4 million to sustain RAVEC, the biometric civil census, and FCFA 1 billion to build the civil-status data-processing centre (FY2026). No outturn against the digital appropriations of the state budget had been published as at September 2026.

MoUs and other agreements

No memorandum of understanding or comparable digital-sector agreement involving Mali had been concluded as at September 2026.

New investments

In 2024 Sotelma, Maroc Telecom's Malian subsidiary trading as Moov Africa Malitel, paid XOF 160 billion to renew its telecommunications licence. It was the largest part of Maroc Telecom's licence and frequency costs across its subsidiaries that year.

In 2025 the International Finance Corporation disclosed a proposed loan to Orange Mali S.A. in CFA francs, recorded at about USD 79 million. It is for network modernisation, 4G expansion and mobile money infrastructure.

In December 2018 Sida committed SEK 25,000,000 to co-fund the fifth population and housing census, RGPH5, paid to the Institut National de la Statistique. An amendment added SEK 20,062,653, almost all drawn down between 2021 and 2025.

On 28 September 2018 the government signed a loan with the Export-Import Bank of China to finance the Mali Digital 2020 project. China International Telecommunications Construction Corporation is the commercial contractor. From 2023 USAID funded a Digital Ecosystem Country Assessment covering infrastructure, regulation and the digital economy.

In all, 106 external digital commitments naming Mali are on the record, spanning 2015 to 2033 and worth roughly USD 697 million, one of them an innovation fund grant with no published amount (November 2026). About 64% of the value sits in two transactions, a 2024 telecommunications licence renewal and a 2018 China Eximbank loan; the West African regional operations that do not name the country sit outside that figure (November 2026). USAID appears more often than any other financier, 67 times, and 100 of the commitments are grants, together about USD 102 million (November 2026).

Connectivity takes 56% of the value, about USD 388 million over four commitments (November 2026). The most frequently financed subsector is national statistics, 76 commitments worth about USD 64 million, 57 of them USAID's (November 2026).

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

Internet use stood at 35.1% of the population in 2025, against mobile connections equal to 94% of it. In 2021 at least a 4G network covered 47% of the population. In 2019 international bandwidth was about 7 kbit/s per internet user.

In March 2026 the ban on non-homologated Starlink and WiFi Zone equipment was reiterated during a SIM-card identification drive. The transitional authorities held that uncontrolled use of such equipment could expose the country to terrorist action. In August 2026 Orange Mali and Malitel dominated the retail market, and years of talk of a new operator licence had not changed that. In 2025 a high-consumption mobile data basket cost more than 10% of gross national income per capita.

Mali's internet and computers score rose 19.8 points over 2014-2023 to 31.8 out of 100, 29th of 54 African states. Its mobile communications score rose 19.9 points to 60.5, 37th of 54. The Infrastructure sub-category scored 36.4, 30th of 54, after a gain of 8.4 points. The postal and shipping network score fell 16.3 points to 15.6, 40th of 54. Malians' own rating of infrastructure provision rose 13.6 points to 46.7, 16th of 54.

MLIX, Mali's single internet exchange point, was established in Bamako in February 2018 and is managed by the regulator AMRTP. In November 2026 it was the one active exchange listed for Mali, with seven members. The Chinese-financed Mali Digital 2020 project was designed around intercity fibre from Kidal, Gao and Timbuktu to Mopti and on to the Guinean, Algerian and Burkinabè borders. On 5 October 2026 the Malian and Senegalese state fibre operators inaugurated the interconnection of their backbones at Kidira-Diboli.

Data Storage

Mali inaugurated a sovereign Tier III data centre in Bamako in early 2026. It is part of a gradual repatriation of data hosted outside the country. In 2026 the state transmission company sought a consultant to take its OpenStack cloud offer into commercial service. The offer is to run across two new national data centres at Sénou and Kati. In 2025 Malian users of the large cloud providers were served from distant regions.

Mali's 2013 personal data protection law sets no general requirement to store data inside the country. In 2023 data sources and data infrastructure were the weakest of the World Bank's statistical pillars for Mali, well below its scores for data use and data services.

Energy

In November 2025 households in Bamako had four to six hours of power a day, electricity having been rationed since late 2023. In March 2024 the utility EDM's director-general said thermal generation had reached about 70% of output, from 17% two decades earlier. He put this down to the fuel and financial crisis. In 2025 residential tariffs were among the highest in West Africa.

Access to electricity reached 54.5% of the population in 2023. In 2023, 91.5% of urban residents had electricity, against 22.7% of rural residents, a gap of 68.8 percentage points. Mali's access to energy score rose 10.2 points over 2014-2023 to 26.3 out of 100, 36th of 54.

In 2024 AMADER was running a rural mini-grid programme. In July 2025 a workshop on civil registration named poor connectivity, weak electricity access and insecurity as the barriers in rural areas.

Technical Capacity

In February 2024 Mali moved its national identity database to Mali Kura Biométrie, a sovereign platform built and maintained by Malian engineers. This followed a 2023 dispute in which IDEMIA was accused of withholding access to the RAVEC/NINA database. In 2024 a Malian company produced the biometric identity card, with a German supplier providing equipment and training. In July 2026 the digitisation of bank-channel pension payments was reported built with technical support from PAFEEM, the entrepreneurship and employment financing project. In April 2026 the mines ministry presented a mining cadastre rebuilt by public-administration staff, fully online and linked to the Treasury and tax systems.

Internet access across the structures of the national statistical system rose to 63% in 2024 from 49% in 2023. Intranet links and working websites remain limited across many of them. The capacity of the statistical system scored 32.5 out of 100 in 2023, 46th of 54, almost unchanged over the decade.

Public administration scored 46.6 out of 100 in 2023, 35th of 54, after falling 7.4 points over 2014-2023. Effective administration rose 5.6 points to 48.2, 32nd of 54.

Cybersecurity

In December 2025 the Council of Ministers approved a national cybersecurity strategy for 2026-2030. Mali scored 29.47 out of 100 on the ITU Global Cybersecurity Index 2024, in the fourth of five tiers. It was relatively strong on legal measures and weak on organisational and capacity-development ones. On 10 June 2026 the Council of Ministers approved the creation of a national information systems security agency. At the National Cybersecurity Days of 26-30 October 2026 the incident response centre ML-CERT launched a web platform for reporting incidents.

The criminal chamber of the Pôle national de lutte contre la cybercriminalité began hearing cases on 21 May 2026. In August 2026 it convicted Chahana Takiou, publication director of 22 septembre, of atteinte au crédit de l'État à travers l'institution judiciaire. On 14 September 2026 it sentenced a second editor, Abdrahamane Keïta, to two years in prison. In July 2026 the justice minister directed that networks trafficking in depraved content on social media be pursued under penal and cybercrime provisions already in force. The direction created no new offence.

In 2018 the Chinese-financed Mali Digital 2020 project was designed to include a state surveillance camera network and a secure internal communications network for the government. Both were to be financed under the same loan as the fibre backbone.

DPI

Data Exchange

In September 2025 Mali launched or expanded Sigui-Dolo for transport licensing, OpenClinic for health, SIGIP for territorial administration and TrésorPay for payments. They run as isolated sector platforms with no interoperability hub between them. In 2023 none of the core registers connected to a national data exchange platform, and the technical foundations for cross-agency interoperability were absent.

In 2024 birth declarations recorded through City-Web at civil status centres fed the MorphoCivis interface that issues the NINA number. In 2025 the one-stop investment shop API-Mali issued the commercial registration and tax identification numbers together when a business was formed. In 2018 an applicant for a tax identification number submitted a photocopy of the NINA card, with no interface or shared key behind it. The Registre Social Unifié, set up by decree in May 2022, can exchange with other national databases. It accepts the NINA number as one identifier among several.

In September 2025 the health ministry presented OpenClinic with its link to the national identification number still a plan. In 2019 an NGO prototype linked its community health workers' app to a facility medical record by patient identifier. In 2025 civil registration was largely paper-based, with the connection to the NINA number planned. The replacement national digital economy strategy is still under development, and no interoperability roadmap has been found. In 2023 sharing between government bodies ran case by case with no overarching data governance framework.

On 24-25 September 2026 the Ivorian and Malian customs reviewed SIGMAT, the transit interconnection system running on the Abidjan-Bamako corridor. They adopted a joint roadmap to correct the difficulties that persist.

Digital Identity and CRVS

In 2025 Mali's identity system supported neither digital know-your-customer nor authentication through a government portal. Identity is verified by physical inspection of the document. Since 1 February 2023 SIM registration has required the national identification number. In July 2025 subscribers found lines registered on their number without consent, and the regulator ordered operators to let every subscriber check the lines linked to it. Behind it sits a biometric register of national scale. By August 2023 5,733,428 biometric national identity cards had been produced against 8,463,084 people listed as eligible in the RAVEC database, roughly two-thirds.

The credential rests on Decree 2025-0509 of 25 July 2025, which instituted the Sahel confederation biometric identity card. The card is valid five years and carries the national identification number, and the decree repealed the 2022 decree on the secure biometric card. Enrolment captures fingerprints and a facial photograph, with no iris. The practice is unchanged from the 2009-2011 RAVEC census to the 2025 biometric passport. Malians nonetheless rate the ease of obtaining an identity document at 69.9 out of 100, 8th of 54 African states. The score rose 5.7 points over 2014-2023, while the civil registration score collapsed.

In February 2024 Mali migrated the register to Mali Kura Biométrie, a sovereign platform built and maintained by Malian engineers. The move followed a 2023 dispute in which IDEMIA was accused of withholding access to the RAVEC/NINA database. In 2024 card production was carried out by the Malian company M-SI, with Germany's Dermalog supplying equipment and training. Passport production went the other way, moving from IDEMIA to China's Emptech in October 2025. The government presented this as a sovereignty measure.

Mali launched a common Alliance of Sahel States biometric passport with Burkina Faso and Niger on 29 January 2025. That was the day its exit from ECOWAS took effect. It stands outside the ECOWAS foundational identity model, the WURI programme being implemented by six other West African states with the Commission.

In 2023 refugees enrolled through the National Refugee Commission, and displaced people and returnees through the National Social Development Directorate. There was no general provision for permanent residents or temporary workers. The finance ministry had no documented role in the system, which sat with territorial administration and security (2023). Whole-of-government coordination on digital identity was weak.

Digital Payments and Fintech

In the 2024 Global Findex round 59.8% of Malian adults made or received a digital payment, against 38% in 2021. In 2024 the number of mobile-money distributors fell 46.34%. The central bank put this down to distributors leaving as commissions were cut, and to the closure of inactive points.

In December 2024 Mali held 10.7% of UEMOA e-money transaction volume and 11.7% of value, down from 12.1% in 2023. It held 7.29% of the accounts opened in the Union, served by nine issuers (December 2024). It was the Union's third largest country for cross-border digital flows, with 17 million intra-UEMOA operations worth 1,054 billion FCFA in 2024. Access to banking services scored 19.2 out of 100 in 2023.

On 30 September 2025 six participants based in Mali were authorised at the launch of the BCEAO's PI-SPI platform. A list published in September 2026 put Malian institutions authorised to offer it at fourteen. Nineteen were on the list published as at 30 September 2026. In June 2026 the deadline to open the service was set at 30 September 2026 for banks and e-money and payment institutions, and 30 June 2027 for microfinance institutions. Orange Money's August 2026 terms let customers pay instantly from their wallets through the platform. The BCEAO runs clearing and settlement through SICA-UEMOA and STAR-UEMOA for all eight member states.

An interministerial instruction of July 2024 mandates digital payment for a first list of public services, including taxes, fines, identity documents and passports. Between July 2024 and May 2026 digital payment raised nearly FCFA 200 million for local authorities. In December 2023 the Jigisémèjiri mobile-money pilot in Mopti covered around 3,000 households. In July 2026 mobile payment of pensions, introduced in 2019, reached pensions below FCFA 50,000. In November 2023 digital financial services had no established mechanisms for consumer complaints.

Registries

Mali's civil registration score fell 37.5 points over 2014-2023 to 25.0 out of 100, 50th of 54 African states.

In 2024 Mali had 747 primary civil status centres, 292 secondary centres and some 8,000 declaration points. In 2024 a UNICEF-supported registration platform had been piloted in ten zones since 2022 and not extended countrywide. In July 2025 a workshop with members of the transitional council accepted that digitalisation remained insufficient. Birth registration completeness stood at 87% (2018). On 5 February 2025 the Council of Ministers created an Agence nationale de sécurisation des actes d'état civil. Its board was named on 21 July 2026, including the digital-economy ministry and the civil-status director.

The commercial register has been computerised since September 2017 under an OHADA-backed programme. A 2023 law set up a beneficial-ownership register for legal persons, giving existing companies until 28 December 2024 to declare. The NINACAD identifier, set by decree of 22 February 2019, is the single national identifier for every land parcel. In January 2026 NINACAD-Adresse, a digital addressing application, was still at its design stage. It covers about 1.5 million locations in Bamako district and five towns (2025). By July 2026 cadastral identifiers had been assigned to more than 1.5 million parcels, Sikasso and three communes near Bamako included.

On 1 October 2026 the annual revision of the electoral lists opened, to run to 31 December. The lists are drawn from the biometric civil-status database, with the NINA as each elector's unique identifier. The 2025 revision covered all 815 communes, Bamako's seven districts and 48 diplomatic posts. At the June 2023 constitutional referendum the roll held 8,463,085 voters.

A social register was established by decree in May 2022 for contributory and non-contributory schemes alike. It covered 1.2 million households in April 2024. In 2025 the World Bank's Sahel social protection programme spent its latest year strengthening the register's policy, systems and diagnostics. In August 2026 Mali was covered by BIC-UEMOA, the credit bureau for all eight UEMOA states.

Sectoral management information systems

The Code de Procédure Pénale in force since December 2024 requires judicial police officers to draft procès-verbaux on the spot and send the original to the prosecutor. It lets the custody register be kept in electronic form. In 2024 Mali took part in INTERPOL's West African Police Information System, which converts paper police records into a national digital database.

In 2021 community health workers reported on a paper-based system, DHIS2 having been adopted for facility-level aggregation in 2015. By the end of 2017 DHIS2 covered 1,208 of 1,358 health facilities. An evaluation of 2023-24 put its data completeness and timeliness at 91.7%, with internet problems the obstacle most cited. OpenClinic, launched and expanded in September 2025, manages clinical, financial, pharmacy, laboratory and statistical records in a subset of facilities. Since March 2023 DISC-Mali has given community health workers tablet-based capture for household visits. In July 2026 the Hôpital dermatologie de Bamako ran registration, laboratory, consultation and pharmacy on a single patient identifier, with consultations and medicines paid electronically. A national digital health strategic plan covers 2024-2028.

In 2017 the education management information system had no formal data-sharing protocol with INSTAT. In July 2026 the education ministry validated the specification of a portal for teachers' postings and attendance, with no build contract or launch date stated. By December 2018 the customs migration to ASYCUDA World had moved further offices into production, with links to the single window, treasury and ports pending. The SYGOCE foreign-trade system, presented in May 2025, automates import requests including bank domiciliation. In 2025 the AgData Hub and the farmer database for input e-vouchers were standalone systems with no link to tax, land or civil registration.

Other GovTech and e-Gov

In February 2026 the justice ministry extended its judicial records system with online platforms for criminal record extracts and nationality certificates. Their documents carry the same legal validity as those issued in person. The underlying case file system was launched with the ICT agency AGETIC in January 2025. On 5 November 2026 the justice minister said an e-Justice platform built with national expertise was being put into operation, its first services being those two documents.

Sigui-Dolo, launched in September 2025, issues biometric driving licences and vehicle cards and connects to TrésorPay and the SYDONIA customs system. On 9 November 2026 the trade directorate issued the first digital import certificate for a private vehicle, in a pilot with customs, tax services and licensed brokers ahead of generalisation. The tax authority's e-Impôt platform has carried online filing and payment for large and mid-sized taxpayers since December 2021. Since August 2023 tax clearance certificates have been issued online through the E-Quitus platform. BAARA-ANPE, launched in June 2025, registers jobseekers and matches them with employers. By October 2021 the SIGMAP procurement system ran in five regions and Bamako, with online bidding still in preparation. In 2025 the procurement directorate's head named its low use among the system's weaknesses. In May 2025 the Prime Minister presented N'GNA SÔRÔ, a microfinance refinancing platform credited with more than 642,000 beneficiaries, and a tanker-truck gauging system. A platform opened in February 2025 lets the diaspora request documents through consular missions. In 2025 the identity register offered no authentication through a government portal, so none of these services could check a user against it.

In August 2025 the finance ministry suspended the salaries of 36,151 public agents who had not presented for the biometric census of the integrated human resources system. The census had enrolled 122,166 of 158,317 agents.

In 2024 Mali scored 0.3005 on the UN E-Government Development Index, 173rd of 193 member states. In August 2026 the territorial administration ministry named digitalisation of public services among its priorities and asked UNDP for reinforced support.

Digitalisation

Digitalisation of sub-national government

On 30 July 2026, at a meeting with the communes of Bamako District and Kati, local authorities were ordered to report within thirty days on how digital payment is functioning in each état civil centre. The report is to cover the difficulties met, the corrective measures taken, the equipment needed and what would improve it. Local authorities must also designate focal points to supervise digital payment at those centres and pass technical and financial information upward. The territorial administration ministry frames generalised digital payment as the instrument for ending fraudulent practice in local revenue collection. The order came alongside publication of what the whole local-government tier had raised through the channel since July 2024.

A UNICEF-supported platform for consolidating birth, death and marriage records nationally has been piloted since 2022 without a countrywide rollout (2024). The network of civil status centres and declaration points administered by the DNEC is still being digitised (2024). In December 2025 the data protection authority opened a regional antenna in Ségou. In August 2026 the ministry of territorial administration named digitalisation of public services, modernisation of civil registration and dissemination of the new administrative map among its priorities. It asked UNDP for reinforced support to deliver them.

The local-authorities directorate trained seven staff on the software and its central server in September 2023. It was reported tested in 680 communes in July 2024. The directorate's 2025 action plan adds a financial-control module.

A FCFA 9.407bn contract for a secure state network reaching local authorities was reported in June 2022, for 12 months. The Prime Minister launched it on 18 December 2023. In April 2025 the universal access agency was seeking to revive the September 2022 convention. Bamako's Commune V signed a convention with a mobile payment operator on 29 October 2026 to collect municipal taxes and fees by phone application or USSD code.

Rural digital data capture

In 2024 Mali's digital services were concentrated in Bamako and the major cities. Rural areas were held back by very limited connectivity and by violence that hinders network investment. In 2021 community health workers reported on paper, with DHIS2 used for aggregation at facility level.

Since March 2023 community health workers have carried tablet-based capture for household visits. In 2025 Mali-Météo's agricultural data hub and the electronically registered farmer database for input e-vouchers were standalone systems. They did not connect to tax, land or civil registration.

Rural Economy is Mali's strongest Ibrahim Index sub-category, at 69.4 out of 100 and 13th of 54, up 11.1 points over 2014-2023. Rural market access reached 75.6, 10th of 54. Rural representation and participation reached 80.5, 8th of 54, up 17.4 points. In 2023 rural electricity access remained far below the urban rate. In 2021 4G population coverage was well short of universal.

Technology

AI

In November 2025 the tax authority described a planned data warehouse using business intelligence and AI tools to detect fraud, with no date attached. On 21 September 2026 an artificial intelligence and robotics centre was inaugurated at Kati Sirakoro Niaré. The 50-hectare, six-block site inaugurated in 2026 has a workshop for intelligent systems and was financed from the national budget at FCFA 3.3 billion.

In November 2025 the private lab RobotsMali released a 612-hour transcribed Bambara speech dataset with recognition models. In February 2025 it had released two open-source Bambara speech models trained on about 35 hours of audio.

In July 2026 the government told a UN dialogue in Geneva it would adopt a national AI policy before the end of the year. Mali ranked 46th of 54 on the Africa AI Governance Index 2026, scoring 0.68 of 4 in the Nascent tier. The index counted no national AI strategy.

ICT Industry

Mali's economic diversification score fell 28.5 points over 2014-2023 to 18.3 out of 100, 53rd of 54 African states. It is among the country's ten worst-scoring and ten most deteriorated indicators.

Business and competition regulations scored 51.5 out of 100 in 2023, 30th of 54, down 8.8 points over the decade. The business and labour environment sub-category scored 39.9, 38th of 54.

In 2023 no business or establishment census had been taken in twenty years, and no such survey in ten. No official count of firms has been found to size an ICT industry against. A plant assembling PCs, tablets and smartphones opened in Bamako on 17 July 2023, with about 50 staff hired. In 2026 the organised activity that was visible sat in a layer of hubs and incubators.

Innovation ecosystem

In August 2026 Women Tech Mali launched Citoyens 2.0, an application for countering disinformation, with a campaign titled "Le vrai avant le viral".

In 2026 Impact Hub Bamako, Jokkolabs Mali, Nyetaa Mali and Expert'labs Mali were among the Malian hubs on AfriLabs' membership list, with an Orange Digital Center and a university incubator. The Council of Ministers adopted a start-up bill in October 2019. In June 2024 its adoption was billed as imminent. In August 2026 a start-up law was recommended again. In April 2025 the ministry's Mali Digital Accélérateur, offering training, mentoring and funding, was reported launched. No cohort size or budget for it has been found. Mali placed 131st in WIPO's Global Innovation Index 2024, in the bottom quartile.

Capacity

Literacy

In 2023 Human Resources in Education was the one Ibrahim Index indicator of 96 for which Mali returned no data. Education quality fell 7.5 points over 2014–2023 to 19.0 out of 100, 46th of 54. Enrolment stood at 21.5, 47th of 54 and 2.0 points lower than a decade earlier. The education sub-category rose 2.8 points to 36.3.

In 2022 42% of Malian adults used the internet at some frequency, 25% daily, and 58% never. The 2023-24 demographic and health survey found 63% of men and just over four women in ten had ever used it.

In September 2026 the universal access fund was paying FCFA 200 million to train 504 elected officials and staff of 126 communes in office software. The first 84, from 21 communes, began that month. A Global Fund contract to August 2026 continues the health ministry's e-learning platform.

Training and skills

The quality of Malian schooling fell over the decade to 2023, to 46th of 54 African states. The private Université du Sahel in Bamako runs a dedicated faculty of computer science. In 2023, 5,713 of the 21,004 graduates of public higher-education institutions, or 27.2%, qualified in sciences, techniques and technology.

In August 2026 a first cohort of 1,000 young people began four months of bursaried digital training. It runs under a three-year convention signed on 31 January 2026 to train 15,000.

On 28 August 2026 the health insurance fund CANAM closed a staff session on personal-data management, cybersecurity and protection against leakage, delivered by a private firm. In 2023 card and payment operations training under a BCEAO programme was completed at all the Malian sites concerned.

In June 2025 the National Employment Agency launched a platform to register jobseekers and match them with employers.

Research institutions

Mali ranked 131st in the Global Innovation Index in 2024, in the bottom quartile of the ranked economies.

In March 2025 the Bamako Forum on digital technology and social cohesion published a comparison of cybercrime, data-protection and telecommunications law in Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger. It was written with a Bamako law faculty academic.

Inclusion

Access to services

Mali's score for equal access to public services fell 4.2 points over 2014-2023, to 30.4 out of 100 and 39th of 54 African states.

In June 2025 Mali adopted a statelessness law creating a pathway to registration and nationality. Since 2017 nearly 2,400 people at risk of statelessness have been naturalised, and more than 30,000 have received birth certificates and other civil documents. In 2023 refugees enrolled through the National Refugee Commission, and displaced people and returnees through the National Social Development Directorate. No general provision covered permanent residents or temporary workers.

In 2025 a high-consumption mobile data basket cost 51.5% of gross national income per capita.

In December 2023 mobile-money disbursement of social transfers in Mopti was still framed as informing a future scale-up. In November 2023 digital financial services had no established mechanisms for consumer complaints. In 2025 standardised digital addressing covered Bamako district and a handful of towns.

Digital divides

Women's access to public services in Mali scored 13.3 out of 100 in 2023, 47th of 54 African states. It rose 4.3 points over the decade and is among the country's ten worst-scoring governance indicators.

In 2022 58% of Malian adults had never used the internet, 42% used it at some frequency and 25% daily. Mali's mobile communications score rose 19.9 points over 2014-2023 to 60.5 out of 100, its second most improved indicator. Its internet and computers score reached 31.8 out of 100 (2023). In 2023-24 87% of men and 66% of women owned a phone, and 63% of men against just over four women in ten had ever used the internet. The 2024 national household survey found 73.6% of men and 67.8% of women owning a mobile phone.

In 2024 digital services remained concentrated in Bamako and the major cities. In 2023 electricity access was split sharply between urban and rural households.

Data

National statistics

On 9 October 2026 the Council of Ministers took note of a fourth statistical master plan and its action plan for 2026-2030. It is set on four axes, from management of the statistical system to the use of its output. The previous master plan covered 2020-2024. The public statistics law of 24 February 2016 underpins the plans and the national statistics council. A law of 7 May 2024 added a statistical levy to the statistics institute's resources, and a decree of 9 May 2024 gives the institute 15 per cent of it. The text of the 2026-2030 plan has not been found.

In 2023 Mali scored above 89 per cent on data use in the World Bank's Statistical Performance Indicators. In 2023 it scored 50 to 69 per cent on data services and products, and 20 to 49 per cent on data sources and data infrastructure. No business or establishment census had been taken in twenty years, and no such survey in ten. Household, labour force, health and agricultural surveys were each fielded three or more times over the decade.

The fifth population and housing census, planned for fieldwork in 2019, concluded in 2022, delayed by infrastructure and security conditions. It counted 22,395,489 residents, against 14,528,662 in 2009. Its directory of localities, giving population village by village, was published in January 2026.

The capacity of the statistical system scored 32.5 out of 100 in 2023, 46th of 54, 0.2 points below its level ten years earlier. Internet access across the structures of the statistical system reached 63 per cent in 2024, from 49 per cent in 2023. The 2024 statistical yearbook appeared in April 2026. Trade statistics are compiled each year from the customs directorate's computerised records.

In April 2026 the statistics institute trained staff across the system in tablet collection on CSPro and ODK. In 2025 rural clinics kept patient encounters on paper registers and sent aggregated monthly totals to DHIS2.

Open data

Mali's disclosure of public records score stood at 21.4 out of 100 in 2023, 42nd of 54, down 5.1 points over ten years. Accessibility of public records rose 3.9 points to 32.3, 24th of 54.

In the 2024 Open Data Inventory government finance, price indexes and agriculture each scored 7.5 out of 10. Health outcomes and money and banking scored 4.5, and trade 4. No openness score was recorded for energy, pollution or digital and communications.

In 2023 data sharing between government bodies was ad hoc, with no overarching data governance framework.

Geospatial data

In 2025 Mali-Météo hosted the AgData Hub, which brings climate, soil and crop data together. A separate farmer database drives input subsidy e-vouchers. Both are standalone systems with no link to tax, land or civil registration. On 10 March 2026 Mali-Météo commissioned receiving stations for Meteosat Third Generation data, under the African Union's PUMA 2025 programme. In 2025 a donor-backed scheme pairing SMS weather alerts with satellite-index crop insurance covered 6,322 farmers.

Geopolitics

US / hyperscaler activities

In 2023 USAID commissioned a Digital Ecosystem Country Assessment of Mali. It examined digital infrastructure, regulation and the digital economy, to shape USAID's own programming.

China activities

In October 2025 production of Mali's biometric passport moved from France's IDEMIA to China's Emptech. The government presented the switch as a digital sovereignty measure.

On 28 September 2018 Mali signed a non-concessional loan of RMB 1,134,000,000 with the Export-Import Bank of China for the Mali Digital 2020 project. It was ratified by Loi n°2018-066, with China International Telecommunications Construction Corporation as contractor. The 2018 scope covered intercity fibre linking Kidal, Gao and Timbuktu to Mopti and the border routes. It also covered an urban fibre network for government institutions and an electronic identity card system. It included a camera surveillance network with a secure internal communications channel for the government.

In January 2024 the Council of Ministers approved a USD 117,387,180.77 amendment to the works contract. It replaced the Mopti, Koro, Timbuktu, Gao, Ansongo and Labenzaga sites with western ones and redesigned the backbone route. It reserved the data centre and deleted the electronic-identity component.

The project carried a 2020 completion date, and China's ambassador visited the works in February 2019.

The Eximbank loan is one of the two transactions that dominate Mali's digital finance by value (September 2026), at roughly USD 187 million against about USD 256 million for Maroc Telecom's renewal of the Sotelma licence, and well ahead of the IFC's roughly USD 79 million.

EU activities

The European Union had no digital-sector financing commitment or cooperation programme in Mali as at September 2026.

Gulf/UAE activities

India activities

Digital sovereignty

The national Tier III data centre inaugurated in Bamako in February 2026 is to host the state's strategic data, e-administration platforms and public digital services. In the short term it is also to host shared infrastructure for Niger and Burkina Faso. In February 2026 the communications minister said it was financed and run locally, the three Sahel states having pledged to repatriate their data. No instrument fixing where state data is held, or under whose law, had been found as at September 2026. In August 2026 the fourth Semaine du Numérique recommended a data-management law. It also recommended making SMTD-SA the sole national operator of transmission and storage infrastructure. It proposed a confederation protocol on AI governance.

In June 2026 the state raised its stake in SOTELMA from 49% to 56%, leaving Maroc Telecom a minority shareholder. Passport production resumed in October 2025 under a new Malian-law contractor, SECU-PASS Mali. Nothing found as at September 2026 says whether the state holds the e-passport system's source code and operating skills, or can exit that contract. The data, portability and exit terms of the 2018 Mali Digital 2020 contract had not been published as at September 2026. The ICT agency AGETIC's 2026 procurement plan renews government messaging and collaboration licences at FCFA 240 million. It also renews Windows, Office, Linux server, Fortigate and SolarWinds licences.

In September 2026 the state intelligence service's Lakana 360 platform, built with a foreign commercial AI model, was reported running on premises on local models. The model vendor's closure of the builder's account did not affect it. In July 2026 Mali asked the UN's global dialogue on AI governance for binding rules on developing countries' sovereign right to govern their national data.
