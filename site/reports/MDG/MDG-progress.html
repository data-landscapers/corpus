Governance · Finance · ICT Infrastructure · DPI · Digitalisation · Technology · Capacity · Inclusion · Data · Geopolitics · Where the record is thin

This report asks the same set of questions of every country. The rows below are a fixed frame of indicators, one row each, chosen in advance and covering all thirty-eight subjects the base tracks — so what appears here is decided by the frame and not by whichever records happened to accumulate. Each row says what happened on that indicator during the period, with every claim linked to the source it rests on.

Where a row reads No evidence, the base holds nothing on that indicator. That is a statement about this base, not about the country — it does not mean nothing exists, only that nothing has been collected here yet. The rows that carry evidence say what it is; the rows that do not are left to speak for themselves.

The period is 2025-08-01 to 2026-08-27.

Progress values. Advanced — a system entered service, a stage was completed or an instrument was made. Stalled — a stated target passed without delivery. Regressed — an instrument was withdrawn or neutralised, or a reported position worsened. Mixed — the indicator's instruments moved in different directions in the period; the clause after the comma names which moved which way. No change — the base holds a standing position and nothing in the period touched it. No evidence — the base holds nothing on this indicator at all. A value may carry a qualifying clause after a comma, as in Advanced, regulations still pending.

Legislation and regulation Data protection legislation The data-protection law of 2014 has been in force since January 2015 and is recorded as unenforced.
Full recordThe law is in force and unenforced, and excludes part of journalistic practice from its scope (law).

Legislation and regulation Cybersecurity legislation A complete recasting of the 2014 cybercrime law is one of three digital bills before the extraordinary session opened on 25 August 2026.
Full recordThe bill was adopted by the Council of Ministers and would define security incidents and critical infrastructure organisations, revise electronic evidence and connection-data retention, and require operator cooperation with justice (adoption, account).

Revision was reported again in August 2026 alongside signature of the United Nations cybercrime convention, with findings that artificial-intelligence-enabled fraud is outpacing African states' response capacity given as the reason (revision).

Three of the seven bills on the session's order paper are digital: one on electronic communication and digital infrastructure, one recasting the cybercrime law and one authorising ratification of the convention (order paper). No bill texts are on file.

Digital Identity and CRVS Registration of entire population The first adult enrolment phase registered more than six million people against a two-million target, on 2,500 kits across 1,729 centres.
Full recordA steering committee validated the proof of concept and released 2,500 kits across 1,729 centres operating six days a week (kits); the January pilot had enrolled about 130,000 people across 116 centres in 26 communes (kits, pilot, enrolment).

More than six million adults were enrolled in the April to June 2026 phase, against a two-million target, with a second phase in preparation (phase, identity).

The counts are reported on different scopes and dates, from 2.6m in two months to over 5.6m in total (counts, review), and no cost figure for either phase is reported. The pilot ran through regime change, civil unrest and a cyclone.

Digital Identity and CRVS Interoperability of birth registration and digital id A law of 28 May 2026 lets births be registered en masse inside the biometric enrolment operation, against a de-duplicated database.
Full recordLate birth registration had been available only through a costly judicial process; the new law permits mass registration within enrolment, with over 2,000 teams and more than 19,000 local chiefs confirming identities against a de-duplicated database (law).

About six million adults, roughly 40 per cent, lacked a birth certificate (identity), so the interoperability here is not a technical link between two working registers but the use of one operation to populate both at once.
