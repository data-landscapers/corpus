Status values. Implemented — in operation or in force. Piloting — running with a limited user group or in a controlled environment. In development — build or drafting under way, not yet operating. Planned — announced or provided for, no build or draft on record. Enacted — an instrument passed into law; pair with a qualifying clause for its in-force date. Under review — a law or policy currently being reconsidered. Discontinued — closed or superseded. Not held — the base carries no reliable statement of status; these are the gaps to fill and are listed again at the end.

Summary of position

Niger has legislated most of an identity stack into being in a single burst. Four instruments of April and May 2026 created a civil-status regime, three interoperable registers and a new agency to run the population register; mass enrolment for the AES biometric national identity card began on 25 April 2026 and passport enrolment on 16 July 2026. No gazetted text of the four instruments is held.

The base underneath is thinner than the stack above it: 66% of births are declared nationally, and the ITU put internet penetration at 23.2% at the end of 2025.

Physical infrastructure arrived late in 2025 — 1,031 km of Trans-Sahara fibre and a Tier III data centre provisionally accepted in November, against a delivery date of 30 September 2025 — while the cyber institutions blueprinted in July 2025 have not moved since.

The telecommunications regulator is the most active institution on the record. It served formal notices on all four mobile operators on 10 June 2026 over voice and data quality, against thresholds this report cannot state because they are not published.

The data-protection regime is formal and its output is invisible. Loi n°2022-59 of 16 December 2022 replaced the 2017 statute, the Malabo Convention was ratified in May 2022, and the High Authority operates under it — but no caseload, decision or sanction figure is published, leaving the privacy backstop for biometric enrolment undescribed.

Telecommunications regulation is where enforcement is visible. Loi 2018-45's prior-authorisation regime was invoked in September 2025 against unauthorised resale of satellite connectivity through Wifi Zones, with a 3 October deadline and penalties of three months to a year and fines to 60m FCFA; in June 2026 formal notices went to all four mobile operators, with fines of 1 to 3% of prior-year turnover, licence suspension or withdrawal in prospect. ARCEP's own 2021 annual report names the underlying decision n°000012/ARCEP/CNRCEP/21 of 10 December 2021 and its function — quality-of-service, coverage and network-control rules — but not the threshold values, which ARCEP's own site still does not surface as of August 2026.

The national digital-development policy (PNDN 2026-2035), drafted by March 2025, went through a redrafting committee installed 2025-07-18 and a national validation workshop on 2026-03-11 for the policy and its 2026-2030 sectoral action plan, on three axes (governance/security, infrastructure/services, skills/innovation) and targeting a 2035 fibre-hub goal; not yet formally adopted. The ministry called the workshop the product of broad consultation among digital-sector actors, though no open public-comment channel is named.

Externally the pattern is neighbour-to-neighbour. Niger agreed telecommunications cooperation with Algeria in March 2026 and sent a technical mission to study Burkina Faso's identification stack in July 2026. Its EU country programme ended on 31 December 2024, replaced by a EUR 238m complex-settings envelope held inside the regional programme.

The physical layer arrived in two tranches. China Eximbank financed a 2,275 km domestic backbone across three sections — Niamey-Tillabery, Zinder-Diffa and Agadez-Tahoua under an RMB 623.7m loan signed January 2014, completed and handed over in Q1 2017. Niger then took provisional acceptance, on 14 November 2025, of 1,031 km of Trans-Sahara fibre on five cross-border axes — to Algeria, Chad, Nigeria, Benin and Burkina Faso — and of a Tier III data centre at Niamey, under an AfDB-financed project (about EUR 43m) launched under NEPAD in 2017, works from 2021. The data centre was targeted for delivery by 30 September 2025; no capacity figure has been published for it. A national internet exchange point opened at Niamey in August 2025.

The cyber half of the same blueprint has not been built. The critical-infrastructure committee of July 2025 enumerated a national public key infrastructure, a National Cybersecurity Centre (SOC and CERT), and a digital forensics laboratory; all three remain planned, and the National Cybersecurity Strategy is still in elaboration.

Satellite is the access story. Starlink has been commercially available since 13 March 2025 under an agreement signed with SpaceX in October 2024, and in January 2026 Airtel Niger commercialised a telco-integrated version in which the operator rather than the customer holds the satellite relationship.

The regulator has real-time visibility of the networks it supervises, through a monitoring platform inaugurated in January 2026, and it found voice and mobile-data quality deficient at all four operators in the April-May 2026 control, with a verification campaign running to 28 August 2026.

Identity runs on two tracks that have converged. The sovereignty track issues the AES biometric national identity card — mandatory at 15 and over, valid five years at 4,500 FCFA, capturing fingerprint, face, voice and iris — and the AES biometric passport in five categories to ICAO specification, both produced with a Libyan supplier. The donor track is the restructured WURI programme, which now carries the statutory register stack rather than running alongside it.

That stack is three registers and an agency. A National Civil Register and a National Nationality Register were instituted by ordonnance of 22 April 2026; a National Population Register is being built as the single authentication point for services conditioned on identification; and ANEI was created and chartered by two décrets of the same date with no budget, staffing or enrolment target stated. Population-register enrolment starts in Niamey's five arrondissements.

Civil registration is the constraint the design assumes away. Nationally 66% of births are declared. One region publishes a number: Diffa reports 173,109 birth certificates issued between July 2023 and July 2026 and timely declaration up from 56% in 2022 to 88.04% in 2025, on village chiefs trained as mobilisers and four computerised communes.

The administrative systems are payroll-first. The integrated state human resources and payroll system has 16,500 agent files digitised and 23,000 updated against a scope of about 200,000 public agents, replacing systems the responsible minister describes as ageing and unintegrated; student grants are to be routed through the Treasury's own application, stated as an intention rather than a live service. Health has a validated master plan with 27 structuring projects and the tax administration is rebuilding after sanctions.

Payments are thin and interoperating. Bank-to-wallet transfers went live in June 2026; the same operator runs the kiosks that take the identity-card fee. Niger holds 3.92% of electronic-money accounts opened across the eight-state Union.

Below the national identity stack, one municipal service is on record: Niamey's Naneye Yarda tax-collection platform, launched 27 January 2025, runs 50 field agents who have geolocated more than 2,000 taxpayers, with mobile-money payment via Al Izza and Nita and automated transaction auditing.

Niger's artificial-intelligence position is one training programme. The United Nations coordination office trained 140 cadres in AI applied to human security with the statistics institute, the digital agency and the interior ministry, and a multisectoral government and UN data platform is in build behind it. No national AI strategy or instrument is held.

The technology sector is a short list the digital agency names — Qwiper, E-Mobility Niger, CodeLoccol and Maria Server — with no funding or company count published. The one substantial industrial relationship is the supply of national identity documents, contracted to a Libyan firm under two memoranda of June 2025 and cast by officials as technological independence.

Two external relationships are now on record. Huawei — present in Niger since 1998 — met the country's newly arrived Chinese ambassador in September 2025 to discuss deepening digital cooperation; the relationship's only recorded financing is a 2014-2017 China Eximbank backbone loan. On the Gulf side, UAE operator e& pledged US$6bn (2024-2026) to the ITU's Partner2Connect coalition across 16 markets including Niger, undivided by country.

Inclusion runs through community infrastructure. The World Food Programme opened a second community digital centre at Tahoua in July 2026, with 100 computers and 50 kW of solar power, after one at Diffa.

Capacity-building follows the same route: Niger hosted the 13th AfricaCERT Handshake symposium at Niamey in July 2025, where the digital agency proposed an AES regional cyber-defence alliance.

Against the enrolment programme, an analyst warns of privacy breaches, state surveillance and systemic exclusion, with rural communities, migrants and those without foundational identity most exposed.
