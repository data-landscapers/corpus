This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

The national digital-development policy had been drafted but not adopted as of March 2025. A National Digital Development Policy for 2026-2035 and a sectoral digital development plan for 2026-2030 were still in validation as of March 2026. The plan carries an axis on digital governance and e-government. A national cybersecurity strategy for 2023-2027 was adopted by decree in December 2022.

The commerce ministry opened a pre-validation workshop on a national e-commerce strategy to 2035 on 26 October 2026. Niger has adopted a national e-Agriculture strategy whose costed 2023-2027 action plan carries 22 projects and 37 actions. It was developed with GIZ and FAO support. It aims explicitly at future interoperability between agricultural data platforms. Delivery committees have also been set up ahead of the strategies. Among them is a national committee installed on 23 July 2025 to deploy and operationalise the country's critical digital infrastructures.

On 27 July 2026 the public-service minister presented SIGRHEP, the human-resources system for state agents, to the Conseil Consultatif de la Refondation. The council is the transition's consultative body. Niger is governed by General Abdourahamane Tiani as President of the Republic.

Overall governance scored 47.9 out of 100 in 2023, 30th of 54 African states. That is below the continental average of 49.3. The score fell 2.5 points over the decade, and the rank slipped from 23rd to 30th. Niger sits below the African average on 50 of the index's 96 indicators. It declined on 49 of them between 2014 and 2023, against 43 improvements.

Legislation and regulation

Ordonnance n°2026-21 of 22 April 2026 sets Niger's civil-status regime. Décrets n°2026-242 and n°2026-243 of the same day create ANEI, the national civil-status and identification agency, and set its statutes. Décret n°2026-277 of 18 May 2026 is the ordonnance's application text. On 14 August 2026 a national awareness tour opened at Dosso to explain these texts to the population.

One ordonnance and three décrets, the whole identity stack, were enacted inside the 27 days from 22 April to 18 May 2026.

Loi n°2022-59 of 16 December 2022 replaced the 2017 data statute. Loi n°2023-31 of 4 July 2023 amended it. It governs the collection, processing and transfer of personal data, biometric data included. No data localisation requirement applies, so personal, government, financial and critical-infrastructure data may be held abroad. No dedicated law establishes cross-government data-sharing authority or governs a national data exchange. The nearest instruments are the 2018 Telecommunications Code, the 2019 law on electronic transactions and the December 2022 data protection law.

Loi n°2018-47 of 12 July 2018 constitutes the telecoms regulator and requires its director general to swear an oath before taking office. Moussa Zeinabou Seyni was appointed by décret n°2026-071/PRN/PM of 30 January 2026. She swore the oath before the Cour d'appel de Niamey on 25 February 2026. The court receives the oath and has no say in the choice. In September 2025 ARCEP served formal notice on commercial Wifi Zones reselling Starlink connectivity. It invoked articles 16 and 23 of loi 2018-45 and set a 3 October deadline to cease or regularise. In 2025 the penalties ran from three months to a year in prison and fines up to 60 million FCFA, plus 6 to 12 million per non-homologated terminal. SIM identification has been mandatory since décret n°2012-433 of 4 October 2012. In November 2013 some 1.7 million unidentified cards, roughly a third of subscribers, were deactivated.

Data protection

The government abolished the Haute Autorité de Protection des Données à Caractère Personnel by decree in June 2026. Its prerogatives passed to the ministries of justice and of the interior, in a wider administrative reorganisation. The law stands: loi n°2022-59 of 16 December 2022 replaced the 2017 statute and governs the collection, processing and transfer of personal data. Loi n°2023-31 of 4 July 2023 and ordonnances n°2024-16 and n°2024-29 have since amended it.

From its installation in 2020 until its abolition the authority conducted inspections, referred offences to the courts and imposed administrative and financial sanctions. Its decisions were appealable to the Conseil d'Etat. Article 57 of the consolidated law defines artificial intelligence and requires developers, manufacturers and service providers operating from Niger to declare to the authority. Article 52 restricts fully automated decisions and profiling. Individuals have a right of access to their data, including data held in identity systems. A controller must notify the authority of a breach without delay, and tell the data subjects where the risk is high. Data may leave the country only for states offering equivalent protection, with the authority's authorisation. A 2020 decree set the authorisation procedure, and the authority issued a form for transfers to third countries under articles 30 to 34 of the 2022 law. No procedure has been published since the authority's abolition.

On the Ibrahim Index, Digital Freedom fell 10.0 points over 2014-2023 to 38.5 out of 100, 40th of 54 African states. The measure covers online censorship, government internet shutdowns and the freedom to organise online.

Regional collaboration

Niger withdrew from ECOWAS in 2024 with Mali and Burkina Faso, leaving the bloc's shared regional digital rails. It builds the same interoperability through the Alliance of Sahel States. On the Ibrahim Index's Regional Integration measure, covering trade, movement and regulatory alignment, Niger scored 41.3 out of 100 in 2023. It ranked 32nd of 54 African states, down 2.9 points over the decade.

BCEAO Instruction n°003-03-2025 of 18 March 2025 governs customer identification across the monetary union, and Instruction n°001-01-2024 governs the supply of payment services. Leaving ECOWAS kept Niger in the monetary union, where most of its regional payment systems sit. The BCEAO operates the SICA-UEMOA retail clearing system and the STAR-UEMOA real-time gross settlement system. Niger's banks and Treasury use both, and there is no Niger-only instant retail payment system. Beyond the union, Niger reaches the PAPSS network for inbound payments only (June 2026). It does so through a licensed extended-network partner's mobile wallet, not as a direct participant.

Niger's national card is the harmonised AES biometric credential, taken up as the alliance's second member after Burkina Faso (March 2026). It allows passport-free travel between the three states. WURI, the World Bank identification programme, opened a pilot in May 2025 to issue birth certificates to 21,202 children under five at Filingué. It supports the 2026 civil-status and identification revisions in restructured form.

Niger hosted the 13th AfricaCERT Handshake symposium in Niamey in July 2025. On 18 September 2026 the confederation's media and communication regulators created a platform seated at Niamey, with its executive secretariat there. In September 2026 its telecoms regulators signed a cooperation convention, with its statutes still to be adopted. Its working files are border frequency coordination, mutual recognition of type-approved equipment, free roaming and coordinated 5G. The confederation's customs directors and Togo's set 1 January 2027 for exchanging company lists and 31 January 2027 for a technical unit on data flows (October 2026). On 5 November 2026 the three states adopted the digital deliverables of the confederation's Year II roadmap. A confederal network operations centre is due by 31 December 2028.

Standards

ARCEP inaugurated a permanent platform for continuous monitoring of mobile networks in January 2026. It runs on the TRAFSCAN, QUALCOP and NETFIX systems supplied by the Tunisian vendor INFOCOM. It gives the regulator its own measurement of service quality, independent of the operators' returns.

Decrees of 2019 created ANMC, the national standards, metrology and certification agency, within the UEMOA and ECOWAS quality framework. ANMC is an ISO correspondent member. Its catalogue of national standards, as published in 2020, runs from agri-food to electrotechnical, with no information-technology or data catalogue.

BCEAO Instruction n°001-01-2024 is the standing text for supplying payment services in the monetary union. It binds banks, credit finance houses, payment institutions, microfinance institutions and electronic-money issuers alike. It abrogates contrary earlier provisions. Surveillance of financial-market infrastructures and of payment means and services runs under Instruction n°001-03-2021. The operating rules of the systems themselves are published, covering settlement finality, priority processing, settlement-account checks and participation requirements.

As at April 2024 West Africa had no certified electronic-signature policy. An ECOWAS and EU Digital for Development Hub expert meeting, held online on 26-27 March 2024, worked from the Malabo Convention and the ECOWAS Supplementary Act on electronic transactions. It formulated guidelines and left the roadmap still to be defined.

Public debate and participation in policymaking

On 6 October 2026 prosecutors sought five years against a lawyer charged under the cybercrime law over a Facebook post on divisions in the army. The charge was spreading data likely to disturb public order. A journalist missing since 31 August 2026 was acknowledged detained only on 25 September. The media regulator suspended nine French outlets on 8 May 2026 for content it said threatened public order. Four journalists had been jailed since 2025.

On 10 September 2026 the council of ministers approved an overhaul of the media framework, tightening accreditation for foreign correspondents. A draft ordinance in it would require online press publishers to be identified. It would make digital newsrooms legally responsible for what they carry. The text has not been published.

A national tour on the identity reforms reached Agadez on 28 August 2026 as its fourth stage. It works region by region through administrative and traditional authorities. It covers the National Population Register, the biometric identity card, the WURI project and the new identification agency. No public consultation on the reforms has been found.

On the Ibrahim Index, Media Freedom fell 12.3 points over the decade to 59.4 out of 100 in 2023, 18th of 54 African states. Freedom of Expression & Belief fell 10.8 points to 64.2, 24th of 54.

Finance

Financial sustainability

The 2025 finance law carried a 14.7 billion CFA franc grant-financed line to connect 2,175 localities. The 2025 law also carried 6.2 billion for mass identity registration. The largest externally financed digital line in the 2026 law is 3.0 billion, to connect schools and health posts under the Smart Villages project.

The domestically financed share of the digital lines is 54.5% of 20.9 billion CFA francs appropriated in 2026, up from 19.5% of 69.3 billion in 2025; the external side fell to 9.5 billion from 55.9 billion, and the domestic side eased to 11.4 billion from 13.5 billion (2026).

The 2025 finance law created a special account for the digital modernisation of the tax administration. Since 2025 it has been credited with a 0.25% levy on the turnover of firms managed by the large-enterprise directorate, plus a state subsidy. Its 2025 ceiling was 8.22 billion CFA francs. The 2026 law caps it at 5.56 billion. The provisional year-end execution report for 2025 reports spending by economic title only. No outturn against the digital appropriations had been published as at September 2026.

The Smart Villages loan runs to 2026 and the regional identity programme's second phase to 2027. The 2026 law's own-budget identity line is 350 million CFA francs. It pays the civil-status directorates' running costs and registry centres. In March 2026 the Trans-Saharan backbone and data-centre project carried more than 30 billion CFA francs from the African Development Bank. In March 2026 that was 16.0 billion as credit and 12.76 billion as grant, against a national counterpart of 2.17 billion.

In June 2025 the government signed memoranda under which the Libyan firm Al Itissal Al Jadeed finances, builds and maintains the passport system. An ordinary passport cost 45,000 CFA francs in September 2026. The revenue terms of the partnership had not been published as at September 2026.

MoUs and other agreements

Algérie Télécom signed two conventions in Niamey on 8 September 2026. They cover a skills centre at the telecoms school, training for Niger Telecom's staff and donated equipment for a fibre link on the national backbone. Niger and Chad signed a protocol in June 2025 to implement their cross-border fibre interconnection. The text of neither instrument has been found.

New investments

Niger's largest live external digital commitment is the World Bank's USD 100 million Smart Villages for Rural Growth and Digital Inclusion loan, approved in 2020 and running to 2026. The figure is a commitment at approval, not money disbursed. The largest commitment of any kind was never a live one. China Eximbank's RMB 623.7 million concessional loan for the national fibre backbone closed in 2017. Repayment runs from 31 December 2020 to 28 December 2033. Niger will be servicing a network committed in 2014 for close to two decades, longer than the equipment's expected life.

As at November 2026, 47 distinct external digital commitments are on the record, with start years from 2015 to 2024 and end dates reaching 2026, together worth roughly USD 200 million. Three concessional loans account for about USD 135 million of that: two from the World Bank and one from the African Development Bank for the Trans-Saharan backbone (November 2026). Of the rest, 42 are grants worth about USD 43 million, 18 of them from USAID, beside a USD 20 million World Bank statistics loan and a USD 2 million political risk guarantee; no equity investment is recorded (November 2026). Rural digital inclusion (about USD 100 million) takes most of it, ahead of connectivity at about USD 48 million and national statistics at about USD 24 million (November 2026). Three are still running: two World Bank loans worth about USD 108 million and a USD 1 million European Union grant (November 2026). Seven have closed and the other 37 carry no status.

The Trans-Saharan fibre backbone and Tier III data centre project is financed at around 30 billion FCFA by the African Development Bank, the African Development Fund and the national counterpart (November 2025). Foundational digital identity is paid for from outside the state budget, under the World Bank's four-country WURI Phase 2 operation running to 2027. The digital payment work for urban cash transfers sits as a component inside the Adaptive Safety Net Project 2. It covers a feasibility study, a payment model and the system itself.

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

In November 2025 Niger took provisional acceptance of 1,031 km of optical fibre on five cross-border axes towards Algeria, Chad, Nigeria, Benin and Burkina Faso. The fibre was laid under the Trans-Saharan backbone project. On 6 October 2026 the Nigerien and Algerian ministers launched operation of the link between In Guezzam and Arlit-Agadez. Algeria's account gives a starting capacity of 100 Gbps. In August 2025 Niger inaugurated a domestic internet exchange point. It lets traffic between Nigerien networks meet without first leaving the country. On 20 July 2026 the regulator opened a consultation proposing the 600 MHz, 700 MHz and 3.5 GHz bands for 5G. In June it had adopted a 2025 national frequency allocation plan.

A national backbone of more than 2,275 km of fibre, financed by a 2014 China Eximbank loan, was handed over in the first quarter of 2017.

Commercial Starlink sales opened on 13 March 2025. Niger was the seventeenth African market the service entered. In January 2026 Airtel Niger began selling "Starlink by Airtel", under which the operator holds the Starlink relationship. An order signed on 30 June 2026 licenses community Wi-Fi zones against a file fee of 50,000 CFA francs and annual royalties of 50,000 to 200,000 a zone. It bans signal amplifiers and gives existing operators three months to comply. The providers' collective contests its equipment rules and fees.

Infrastructure is Niger's worst-scoring of the Ibrahim Index's sixteen sub-categories, at 28.1 out of 100 in 2023, 42nd of 54. It is also the country's second most improved, up 8.7 points over 2014-2023. Mobile communications rose 19.1 points to 47.0, ranking 47th. Internet and computers rose 16.8 points to 30.6, ranking 30th. Mobile connections equalled 59.4% of the population in January 2024. Fewer than a fifth of Nigeriens used the internet in 2024. The regulator checked mobile service in Niamey from 27 July to 28 August 2026. In September 2026 a press account reported quality unchanged after months of dropped calls and unstable connections. The regulator's findings have not been published.

Data Storage

On 29 October 2026 the national information-society agency's director-general inspected the national data centre, still under construction in Niamey. The agency presents it as strategic infrastructure for digital sovereignty, to host and secure the administration's data and applications. In March 2025 the government had announced it as a 9.5 billion CFA franc build with African Development Bank support, 13 per cent executed and due by 30 September that year. The project was put at about US$14.3 million in March 2025. It is the second component of the Trans-Saharan backbone project, whose fibre sections were received in November 2025.

Niger's 2022 data protection law imposes no data localisation requirement, so personal, government, financial and critical-infrastructure data may be held abroad. Transfers abroad must go to countries offering equivalent protection and be authorised. The authorising authority was abolished in June 2026 and its powers passed to the justice and interior ministries.

In 2023 a mutualised processing centre sited in Côte d'Ivoire and Niger brought twelve microfinance institutions into the UEMOA clearing system through partner banks.

Energy

In 2023, 67.4% of the urban population had electricity access against 10.4% of the rural population. Nationally, electricity reached 20.1% of the population in 2023. Access to energy is among Niger's ten worst Ibrahim Index indicators, at 10.4 out of 100 in 2023. That is 49th of 54 African states, up only 3.4 points over the decade.

In 2024 World Bank RISE scoring credited Niger with a current, officially approved electrification plan, at 82 out of 100 for governance. A dedicated mini-grids framework scores 65 and covers solar hybrid programmes and diesel-to-solar hybridisation. The Niger Solar Electricity Access Project closed in December 2024. It delivered new solar mini-grid connections to around 1,464 households. It hybridised existing NIGELEC mini-grids serving around 19,283 households.

In 2017, 78% of firms were affected by electrical outages, at an average of around 22 a month. Under a tenth of electricity generation came from renewable sources in 2021, the rest from thermal generation and imports. The regulated residential tariff was held unchanged in 2024 despite rising supply costs.

Technical Capacity

In August 2026 Niger assigned ANSI, the Agence Nationale pour la Société de l'Information, a systems-integration role in the identity stack. It works alongside the biometric identity card production service and the territorial surveillance directorate. In June 2025 the interior ministry signed two memoranda with a Libyan firm to finance, build and maintain the biometric e-ID and e-passport systems.

SIGRHEP, the personnel system presented in July 2026, is to be run by at least 150 human-resources and payroll managers across the public service. In 2023 donor-funded IT equipment and computer training for the police, biometric kit included, went to specialised judicial-police and territorial-surveillance units.

By 2022 the Université Islamique du Niger's Faculté des Sciences et Techniques had produced over 1,200 ICT and telecommunications graduates since 2009. In July 2025 ANSI named human competence alongside infrastructure among its pillars.

Cybersecurity

The Council of Ministers created the Centre National de Cybersécurité by decree on 11 October 2025 and approved its statutes. It named the centre the institution coordinating implementation of Niger's 2022 national cybersecurity strategy. It also adopted the decree organising the Agence Nigérienne de Certification Électronique under articles 17 and 18 of loi n°2019-03. The strategy itself dates from the Council of Ministers of 27 December 2022, which adopted it by decree for 2023-2027. That Council planned the centre and a digital forensics laboratory. Niger scored 41.86 out of 100 in the ITU Global Cybersecurity Index 2024, in the index's "establishing" tier. From 2 to 6 November 2026 a communications ministry delegation studied Burkina Faso's security operations centre to prepare Niger's own, which is not yet operating. The confederation's Year II digital roadmap, adopted on 5 November 2026, has each state's CERT funded from universal-service funds and regulatory fees.

The government installed a national committee on 23 July 2025 to deploy and operationalise the country's critical digital infrastructures. Its remit lists six components. Three are a national public key infrastructure, a National Cybersecurity Centre housing a security operations centre and a CERT, and a national internet exchange point. The others are government Tier III+ reference data centres, a national digital forensics laboratory, and the national fibre backbone with metropolitan loops.

One of those six, the national fibre backbone, had reached provisional acceptance by November 2025; a second, a Tier III government data centre, was still being built.

ANSI's director general set data sovereignty and encryption of every transaction and information item as the basis of Niamey's municipal tax platform (January 2025). The agency has said it wants to turn its AfricaCERT contacts into a cyber-defence alliance among Alliance of Sahel States members. It hosted the thirteenth AfricaCERT Handshake symposium in Niamey in July 2025. Duties on holders of data already exist: a controller must notify the HAPDP of a personal data breach without delay. Where the breach presents a high risk, the controller must also inform the people affected.

DPI

Data Exchange

In February 2026 a support mission settled the target architecture for linking civil registration to national identification. It created an inter-institutional task force under the digital agency ANSI. A Council of Ministers communiqué of 22 April 2026 records the interoperability gaps between the two still open and the enabling legal reforms in draft. WURI-Niger's component 3 finances a framework linking digital identity to public and private service providers, and it has not been built. Named beneficiaries are employment, justice, migration and passports, payments, national statistics and social protection.

Niger has not developed an e-government interoperability framework (2024). Its business, electoral, civil registration and land registers are siloed, with no cross-government exchange platform. The tax register is not integrated with any national identity system. Taxpayer registration turns on a civil status document, not an identity card number. The nearest instruments are the telecommunications code, the personal data protection law of December 2022 and loi n°2019-03 of 30 April 2019 on electronic transactions, electronic signature, data retention and penalties. On 18 May 2026 the Council of Ministers adopted a decree on managing the Registre National de l'Etat Civil. It provides interoperability mechanisms between that register, the Registre National de la Population and other functional systems. It gives a national unique identifier to every Nigerien and to long and short-term foreign residents. It is the first named mechanism, covering identity only.

Niger's National Single Window, operational since April 2021, added a licensing and permits module in 2024. The module links the customs, tax and Treasury directorates-general to exchange data. Elsewhere, registers connect on paper. A company gets its fiscal identification number only against a certified copy of its business register certificate. The e-invoicing system SECeF then records both identifiers.

A national health interoperability layer was reported in production in August 2026. By September automated workflows carried STELab laboratory results and CommCare community health data into DHIS2, on a health ministry server in Niamey. Agricultural information systems run separately, with no unified farmer database. In July 2025 the government listed a national internet exchange point among six critical digital infrastructures to deploy.

Digital Identity and CRVS

Mass enrolment for the CNIB-AES biometric national identity card began on 25 April 2026 at Niamey's Yantala commissariat. It runs across the capital's twenty commissariats before extending to the interior. The card is issued to citizens aged 15 and over, is valid for five years and costs 4,500 CFA francs. In March 2026 the interior ministry put the card and passport contract with the Libyan supplier at 85 per cent executed. Production is in Niamey only, with two million cards a year planned over five years. In August 2026 officials put the Nigeriens aged 14 and over legally required to hold a national identity card at about 13 million. In October 2026 the interior ministry said fewer than three million hold one.

Two foundational-identity tracks are running at once: a sovereignty-branded AES card and passport built with a Libyan vendor, and a World Bank programme piloting the open-source MOSIP platform with an Indian supplier.

The WURI project's MOSIP lab had registered around 500 people by 2026. In June 2026 the project consulted the market on a twelve-month contract for a biometric identification system integrated with MOSIP. It is to serve the National Population Register first. WURI-Niger, launched in October 2022, was restructured after July 2023 and folded into the Refondation programme. That made it the statutory backbone of the new National Population Register.

In a World Bank survey of 2021/22, 60 to 70 per cent of adults said they owned the foundational ID, with a 19-point gap between men and women. On public perception of how easy an identity document is to obtain, Niger ranked third of 54 on the Ibrahim Index, at 79.2 out of 100 in 2023.

Enrolment for the AES biometric passport opened on 16 July 2026. The police produce it with the Libyan supplier Al Itissal Al Jadeed and a local partner. Enrolment for the CNIB-AES opened in Agadez on 15 September 2026. Biometrics are collected at the regional police headquarters and processed centrally. No count of CNIB-AES cards enrolled or issued has been found.

Digital Payments and Fintech

One structure in Niger held a BCEAO licence to operate as a payment institution as at 28 February 2026, out of thirty-one licensed across the West African Monetary Union.

That single licence is the joint-smallest payment-institution sector in the union alongside Guinea-Bissau, against nine in Côte d'Ivoire and eleven in Senegal, and about three per cent of the union's licensed institutions.

In 2025 the BCEAO operated the SICA-UEMOA retail clearing and STAR-UEMOA settlement systems for all eight member states. Niger's National Public Treasury is a direct participant in STAR-UEMOA alongside the banks. Nigerien participants were among the 45 authorised to open PI-SPI instant payments to the public on 30 September 2025. Instruction n°001-01-2024 requires providers to segregate client funds and apply strong customer authentication. In June 2026 Niger reached the PAPSS network for inbound payments only, through a licensed partner and not as a direct participant.

In August 2026 Airtel Money Niger SA, the country's first BCEAO-licensed electronic money issuer, opened international transfer. Inbound remittances come from eight countries through MoneyGram, PayPal Xoom and other partners. Outbound wallet-to-wallet payments reach seven UEMOA states over its own network. Niger counted 9,746,822 mobile-money accounts in 2024. In 2024, 4.53% of these were active, against 30.90% across the union. Moov Money runs domestic transfers and utility bill payment over USSD.

The tax administration's e-SISIC portal has taken electronic filing and payment since September 2019. On 1 October 2026 the delegated operator of the vehicle one-stop shop opened payment of some services through a mobile-money operator. The transport ministry framed this as traceability of public revenue. In 2018, 98.7 per cent of civil-service salaries were paid electronically, against 11 per cent of pensions and none of the safety-net transfers. In April 2023 the government was moving pensions above FCFA 30,000, for more than 40,000 retirees, into bank accounts. Digital government-to-person payments remained at pilot stage in 2023. Access to banking services is Niger's third worst Ibrahim Index indicator, at 4.1 out of 100 in 2023, 48th of 54. The 2026 finance law taxes money deposits and transfers, mobile money included, at 0.5% of the gross amount and 5% above FCFA 2 million.

Registries

Niger's identity legislation of April–May 2026 establishes three registers, the national civil-status register (RNEC) among them. The National Population Register will give each person a unique number, the key to any public or private service that requires identification. It is designed to interoperate with civil-status management, the biometric identity card and travel documents. Enrolment starts in Niamey before extending to the rest of the country.

In 2025 the INCI computerisation platform was operational in 103 of Niger's 266 communes. Most rural registry and declaration centres record births by hand and forward them on paper. A 2023 continental assessment found electronic civil registration databases operating in urban towns only. The birth-declaration rate stood at 79 per cent in 2026, against 53.48 per cent declared within the legal deadline in 2022. On the Ibrahim Index, Civil Registration fell 12.5 points over 2014-2023 to 50.0 out of 100. In Diffa 173,109 birth certificates were issued between July 2023 and July 2026. Timely declaration there reached 88.04 per cent in 2025.

The business register has been held electronically since 2019, but only for enterprises registered in Niamey. Regional tribunals still register businesses on paper, outside the national database. Around 4.5% of the population holds legally authenticated documentation for its farmland, and some 32,000 titles have been issued since 1906. Cadastre digitisation has scanned maps, registries and titles, leaving geo-referencing and title management unimplemented. A September 2025 assessment found the Registre Social Unifié operational but not yet deployed nationwide. The first biometric electoral register, handed over in September 2020, held 7,446,556 voters against an estimated electorate of 9,751,462. OIF and ECOWAS auditors judged it reliable that month. Niamey's geoportal references more than 3,500 streets (2026). On 4 August 2026 an ad hoc committee began drafting the legal text for a beneficial-ownership register, with a three-month mandate.

Sectoral management information systems

In July 2026 the public-service minister told the Refondation consultative body that the absence of an integrated system limits control of the wage bill. SIGRHEP, a single biometric human-resources and payroll system, is being built to replace the ageing systems serving roughly 200,000 public agents. By that month 16,500 personnel files had been digitised and 23,000 updated. One record is to serve both establishment control and salary payment. At least 150 human-resources and payroll managers are to operate it.

Since 1996 the tax administration has run computerised registration, unique taxpayer identification and central management of declarations and payments through its AFP and SISIC systems. Customs has run Sydonia World since April 2016. The free e-SECeF certified e-invoicing platform launched in February 2023. By February 2024 it had 13,000 users and 145,000 invoices.

The health ministry's 2023 monitoring guide has each health establishment key a standard monthly activity report into DHIS2. Health posts send theirs to a health centre to be keyed. By September 2026 the STELab laboratory system and the CommCare community health system fed DHIS2 through automated workflows. CommCare data had until then been keyed by hand. A 2025 paper warns of fragmentation and duplicated indicators between digital health platforms and district reporting.

In 2025 education statistics came from an annual school census on printed questionnaires, keyed at regional and central level. On 14 October 2026 the education minister reviewed the ministry's DUNE-RH human-resources platform and its e-learning platforms, data security included. The Gendarmerie's central case-file and search registry still ran on physical index cards in 2023. A committee to draft a national e-justice strategy was installed in June 2024. No draft has been found since.

In May 2019 the national police database held over 8,000 cases, with about 700 added a month. The programme had equipped 26 sites in Niamey by August 2019. In April 2020 INTERPOL reported the system fully operational, extended to nine sites outside the centre, and the only one in its regional programme connected to its global police communication network.

Other GovTech and e-Gov

Public Administration is one of Niger's three best-scoring Ibrahim Index sub-categories, at 57.5 out of 100 in 2023, 21st of 54 African states. Its Effective Administration indicator rose 7.2 points over the decade to 56.8. Niger sat in the low band of the UN E-Government Development Index in 2024.

On 16 July 2025 ANSI relaunched the citizen portal as the Portail Public, adding online services in stages. No list of its services has been published. The portal began as service-public.ne under the Niger 2.0 strategy. That strategy treats interoperability as a theme of wider digital policy, not as a plan of its own.

In February 2026 a procurement official wrote that most steps on SIGMAP remain offline. The platform has been mandatory since 2017, with electronic signature and online approval provided for in 2023. ANSI's electronic document and mail platform, hosted on national servers, reached the justice ministry in July 2025. It reached the foreign ministry and embassies in December 2025. In March 2025 the government was pursuing a "zéro papier" agenda with ANSI, the regulator ARCEP and the then data protection authority.

ANSI built Niamey's municipal tax collection platform, launched in January 2025. In July 2026 a proposal to turn the employment agency ANPE into a single-window agency alongside SIGRHEP went to the Refondation consultative body. At his installation on 22 September 2026 the new communications minister named a secure government intranet as the route to digital public services. No design, operator, budget or date for it has been published.

Digitalisation

Digitalisation of sub-national government

Niamey launched Naneye Yarda, its digital municipal tax platform, in January 2025. Fifty field agents geolocate taxpayers as they collect, and more than two thousand had been located by the launch. ANSI, the national digital agency, built the system. Every transaction is audited automatically. Collection runs through the mobile-money operators Al Izza and Nita. By January 2026, 35,000 Niamey taxpayers were registered on it, with CFA 8.08 billion assessed and CFA 2 billion collected.

In 2022 the commercial and moveable-credit register was held electronically only for enterprises registered in Niamey. Regional tribunals processed registrations by hand, outside the national database. In 2025 civil-status computerisation reached a minority of the 266 communes, with roll-out continuing under a 2023-2027 plan. The WURI project's 2026 account of its digital civil-status platform matches that picture. Enrolment in the National Population Register is to begin in Niamey's five arrondissements communaux before reaching the other regions.

A national tour explaining the civil-registration and identification reforms reached Agadez on 28 August 2026 as its fourth stage. It briefed administrative and traditional authorities on the population register, the biometric card, WURI and the new identification agency.

Rural digital data capture

In 2020 the World Bank approved its Smart Villages for Rural Growth and Digital Inclusion loan, USD 100 million for rural broadband, digital financial services and digital skills. It was still running in 2026, its final year.

In 2024 the SIMA and SIMB market information systems and RECA's e-Extension service ran separately, with no unified farmer database. RECA's service had digitally profiled 24,000 farmers. None was linked to a government data exchange. A national e-Agriculture strategy with a costed 2023-2027 action plan of 22 projects names interoperability between such platforms as an aim.

In 2023 electronic civil registration databases operated in urban towns only. In 2025 the annual school census was completed on printed questionnaires and keyed at regional and central level. The 2023 health monitoring guide has monthly activity reports keyed into DHIS2, with health posts sending theirs to a health centre. By July 2026 mobilisers, mobile teams, mobile court hearings and a few computerised communes had lifted timely birth declaration in Diffa well above the national rate.

Electricity access was sharply urban in 2023. Rural Economy is Niger's second best-scoring Ibrahim Index sub-category, at 65.4 out of 100 in 2023, 21st of 54. Rural Economy Support rose 24.0 points to 72.0. Rural Market Access fell 12.6 points over the decade to 72.4, and Rural Land & Water Access stands at 46.7.

Technology

AI

On the Africa AI Governance Index 2026, Niger ranked 42nd of 54 states with 0.73 out of 4, in the Nascent tier. The index found no adopted national AI strategy, no AI-specific law and no AI budget. In August 2025 the meteorology, water and plant-protection services were trained on Rayuwa, a text and voice assistant with image-based crop diagnosis. Its operator's institutional status is not stated, and no user count has been published. Researchers published Feriji, the first French-Zarma parallel corpus and glossary, in 2024. SALAN, published in May 2026, holds more than 2,000 hours of audio in eight of the country's languages. It includes 110 hours of verified Zarma and Hausa transcripts.

ICT Industry

On 20 August 2026 ministers inspected an electronics assembly plant at Hamdallaye, in the Tillabéri region. It was 63 per cent built and due in service by the end of October 2026. The project is carried by DHY Technologie SA, with an announced investment of about CFA 2.94bn and 275 permanent jobs (August 2026). No report of the plant entering service had been found as at November 2026.

Niger's rules for business and competition scored 66.5 out of 100 on the Ibrahim Index in 2023, 14th of 54 African states and up 5.4 points over the decade. The wider business and labour environment those rules sit within scores 42.0 and ranks 33rd.

In January 2026 Airtel Niger, the leading mobile operator, began selling "Starlink by Airtel". It is a telco-integrated satellite service under the Bharti Airtel–SpaceX partnership, with Niger among the first African markets to trial it. Under that model the operator, not the customer, holds the Starlink relationship and bills for the capacity. Since 2025 Starlink in Niger had been sold as kits direct to customers.

Direct Go, an e-commerce and delivery platform built by the Nigerien firm Silsila Tech, took first prize in the 2021 e-Takara competition. It now runs at version 6.0.0, listing open pharmacies with routing directions alongside its ordering service.

Niger's 2025 business census counted 153,661 formally registered enterprises. No count of ICT firms among them has been published.

Innovation ecosystem

No Nigerien company appears in the Partech Africa venture funding tracker for 2023 to 2025, nor in the AVCA and Africa: The Big Deal datasets. Niger ranked 132nd in WIPO's Global Innovation Index 2024, near the bottom of the ranked economies.

CIPMEN, established in 2014, had incubated 222 companies and trained nearly 8,000 people by 2025. Its portfolio includes Niger Digital, Global IT and Sadit Technologies. In 2022 AfriLabs counted three Nigerien hubs among its members: CIPMEN, Cabinet Hub Zinder and STRAT-CORE Plus. The Cité de l'Innovation de Sadoré opened in 2022 with government, AFD and EU funding. It houses a Fab Lab and Codeloccol, Niger's first coding school.

Direct Go, the e-commerce and delivery platform that won the 2021 e-Takara competition, earns a commission on partner merchants' order value and a delivery fee. No equity investment in it has been found.

Capacity

Literacy

Education is Niger's second weakest Ibrahim Index sub-category, at 37.4 out of 100 in 2023. It ranks 44th of 54 African states, 2.4 points lower than a decade earlier. Enrolment scores 18.3 and quality 11.3, both 50th on the continent. Completion is the weakest of all 96 scores recorded for the country.

Human Resources in Education scored 90.5 out of 100 in 2023, 17th of 54 African states. Enrolment, completion and quality all sit in the continent's bottom ten.

In 2025 the school census was assembled on paper, completed by school heads and passed up through the regional education directorates before central entry. There is no digital capture at school level.

The first national campaign against disinformation opened at Tillabéry on 19 September 2026 under the slogan "Vérifie avant de partager". The NGO Jeunesse Plus runs it with the communication ministry's support. It pairs an online awareness campaign with media and information literacy workshops for local communities.

Training and skills

By 2022 the Université Islamique du Niger's Faculté des Sciences et Techniques had produced more than 1,200 ICT and telecommunications graduates since 2009.

Codeloccol, Niger's first coding school, opened with a Fab Lab at the Cité de l'Innovation de Sadoré in 2022. The incubator CIPMEN has combined incubation with training since 2014. In August 2026 an Airtel Africa Foundation course trained women in agri-food processing and pottery in Zinder in digital financial tools. In May 2026 the smart-villages project called for a firm to source, train and carry young digital talent under contract for ANSI. On 8 September 2026 Algérie Télécom agreed to set up a skills centre at the national telecoms school. Niamey hosted the 13th AfricaCERT Handshake symposium in July 2025, opened by the Prime Minister.

Research institutions

Niger ranked 132nd in WIPO's Global Innovation Index 2024, near the bottom of the ranked economies.

Inclusion

Access to services

Niger's social safety nets scored 22.6 out of 100 on the Ibrahim Index for 2023, 38th of 54 African states. That was 8.4 points down over the decade. The social protection and welfare sub-category fell 5.7 points to 39.9 over the same period.

A 2022 World Bank study of the WURI programme found 55% of the population had difficulty obtaining identity credentials. The biometric card whose mass enrolment opened in April 2026 costs 4,500 CFA francs. In April 2026 a money-transfer operator placed payment kiosks at enrolment sites so applicants could pay the fee and get a receipt. Banking access is among Niger's three worst Ibrahim Index indicators.

In October 2024 UNHCR described the WURI foundational system as built to reach every person on Niger's territory, whatever their nationality or legal status. Malian and Nigerian refugees received biometric registration and identity cards that year.

In 2025 the Registre Social Unifié was operational but not yet deployed nationwide. Household validation missions continued in Maradi and Zinder. Digital disbursement of government payments to people was at pilot stage in 2023. Digitised social transfers were piloted in the same two regions, and a treasury payment system was still in preparation.

The ombudsman has taken and tracked complaints against public bodies online since September 2021. A national survey of digital-financial-services risks, validated in December 2025, found users unaware of their routes to redress.

In August 2026, 500 women in agri-food processing and pottery in Zinder completed a ten-day Airtel Africa Foundation course. It covered business management, financial planning and digital financial tools. Opening the national civil-status tour at Dosso in August 2026, the region's governor singled out rural people, the vulnerable, children, women and displaced persons. He said identification must be a factor of inclusion and not of exclusion.

Digital divides

In 2023 electronic civil registration databases operated in urban towns only. Electrification had the same shape in 2023, rural access a fraction of the urban rate. The Ibrahim Index puts Niger near the bottom of the continent on access to energy.

Mass enrolment for the biometric national card opened in April 2026, and the card is not free. Fewer than a fifth of Nigeriens used the internet in 2024. In August 2026 officials put the population legally required to hold a national identity card at about 13 million. In October 2026 the interior ministry said fewer than three million hold one.

Women's mobile phone ownership in Niger was last measured against men's in 2015, and sat far below it.

The World Bank's Smart Villages programme for rural broadband, digital financial services and digital skills was still active in 2026. Starlink has been on sale since March 2025. A Niamey delivery startup accepts Visa and Mastercard from abroad, so diaspora money can pay for household purchases.

Data

National statistics

Niger's 2025 business census counted 153,661 formally registered enterprises. In February 2026 the fifth population census was piloted with digital collection tools in 96 enumeration areas of three Maradi communes. The pilot was evaluated in May 2026. No date for the general enumeration had been published as at October 2026. The statistics institute's yearbook for 2020-2024 was validated on 30 April 2025. A US$1.45 million Global Environment Facility grant approved in 2026 is to build a national framework for climate transparency. The environment council CNEDD executes it.

On the World Bank's Statistical Performance Indicators for 2023, Niger scores above 89 per cent on data use. Data sources, the weakest of the five pillars, scored 20 to 49 per cent in 2023. In 2023 data products sat at 70 to 89 per cent, and data services and infrastructure at 50 to 69 per cent. The Ibrahim Index puts statistical capacity at 43.8 out of 100 in 2023, 33rd of 54, up 3.2 points over the decade.

In 2025 the school census was completed by school heads, collected through the regional education directorates and keyed centrally into STATEDUC. There is no digital capture at school level. In 2023 civil registration was largely on paper, its digitisation a partial rollout in which Niamey's records reach a central server. Final foreign trade figures for 2020-2024 are built from SYDONIA customs declarations and processed in EUROTRACE. The 2023 health statistics yearbook is compiled from routine facility reporting on DHIS2.

Four different national birth-registration rates were quoted at official events within three weeks of August 2026 — 79 per cent, 80 per cent, 79.63 per cent and, for 2023, 60 per cent — none of them attached to a named survey instrument, which leaves the country's own civil-registration coverage fixed at roughly four-fifths and no finer.

Open data

On the Ibrahim Index, disclosure of public records rose 17.8 points over 2014-2023 to 38.7 out of 100, 18th of 54 African states. It is one of Niger's ten most improved indicators. Accessibility of public records reached only 26.7, 32nd of 54, up 4.8 over the same decade.

In December 2024 the statistics institute's NADA catalogue listed household, food-security and living-conditions survey microdata. The institute licenses its published data free and worldwide, commercial reuse included, with attribution. In the Open Data Watch ODIN 2024 assessment, health facilities score highest at 8.5 out of 10. Balance of payments, government finance and pollution statistics score 8.0 each. International trade scores 3.0, and price statistics and crime and justice 4.5 each. Built environment, education facilities, education outcomes and health outcomes score 5.0 apiece.

Across the twenty-three categories assessed in 2024 the scores average 5.8 out of 10, with only four at 8 or above and eight at 5 or below.

Article 27 of the 2022 data protection law gives individuals a right of access, including to data held in identity systems. The central bank publishes the operating rules of STAR-UEMOA and SICA-UEMOA, the payment systems Niger uses. They cover settlement finality, priority processing, settlement-account checks and participation requirements.

Geospatial data

Niamey's municipal tax platform Naneye Yarda is worked by 50 field agents who geolocate taxpayers as they collect (January 2025). This gives the capital a located record of its municipal tax base. The record is built from the ground, not from imagery.

Imagery has steered a public payment once: a satellite water-requirement index triggered drought cash transfers to 15,400 households in November 2021. A 2024 World Bank test of three satellite drought indexes as transfer triggers found none best everywhere. Under the first phase of the CREWS early-warning initiative, from 2017, the hydrology agency issued ten-day flood bulletins. The meteorological service issued daily weather bulletins. A second phase prepared in 2024 adds flood forecasting and agrometeorological services in selected areas. Niger hosts one of four African regional stations receiving meteorological satellite data directly, built with EUR 20m from the African Development Bank (2021).

A January 2025 peer review found one of the meteorology directorate's 129 stations, an ASECNA station, compliant with the global observing standard. Its June 2026 contribution plan sets 17 new and 14 upgraded surface stations and four new upper-air sites.

In February 2024 the meteorology directorate ran the WRF forecast model four times a day at 4 km over Niger on its own 288-core high-performance computer, with power cuts and unstable internet the main difficulties.

Geopolitics

US / hyperscaler activities

In January 2026 Airtel Niger commercialised "Starlink by Airtel" under the Bharti Airtel–SpaceX partnership. Niger was among the first African markets to trial the arrangement.

Niger's authorities signed a partnership agreement with SpaceX on 29 October 2024. They presented it as part of the national effort to close the digital divide. Kits went on public sale in March 2025 through the company's website and approved resellers.

In April 2025 the United States Embassy's economic adviser attended the launch of NINETEC's Starlink distribution campaign in Niamey. Starlink is thus sold three ways: direct to households, through a Nigerien distributor and bundled by a mobile operator.

China activities

China Eximbank committed a government concessional loan of RMB 623.7 million, signed on 2 January 2014. Niger's national fibre backbone was built on that credit and is still being paid for. The loan, about US$103.8 million in constant 2023 dollars, financed the National Backbone Fibre Optic Cable Network and Communication Service Project. The National Assembly ratified it on 29 October 2014. The backbone was handed over to the authorities in the first quarter of 2017.

The 2014 loan bought more than 2,275 km of fibre on the Niamey–Tillabéry, Zinder–Diffa and Agadez–Tahoua routes, 136 GSM and WCDMA sites, CDMA and ADSL networks and e-government services. The 2014 terms were soft: a 20-year maturity, a seven-year grace period, 1% interest and a 0.25% commitment fee. The state operator SONITEL is responsible for repayment. The length is the point: servicing runs on well past the working life of the equipment the loan financed.

That single 2014 commitment still outweighs anything since: the Chinese backbone loan, closed in 2017, remains the largest of any kind. The largest live commitment to Niger's digital sector is the World Bank's USD 100 million Smart Villages for Rural Growth and Digital Inclusion loan (P167543), running to 2026.

Fourteen financiers appear in Niger's digital sector since 2015 (November 2026). The World Bank leads with about USD 128 million across three operations, ahead of the African Development Bank at about USD 48 million across a loan and a grant; USAID has the most commitments, 18, worth about USD 8 million (November 2026). China Eximbank's 2014 backbone loan falls before the 2015 window and is not counted.

EU activities

The Cité de l'Innovation de Sadoré opened in 2022 on funding from the Nigerien government, the French development agency AFD and the European Union. It houses a Fab Lab and Codeloccol, the country's first coding school.

Gulf/UAE activities

No Gulf or Emirati investment in Niger's digital sector was under way as at September 2026.

India activities

In January 2026 Airtel Niger launched "Starlink by Airtel", the satellite service of the Bharti Airtel–SpaceX partnership. Niger was among the first African markets to trial it. An Indian-owned operator is thus the party selling American satellite capacity to Nigerien subscribers.

In February 2025 the Indian vendor BioEnable ran a MOSIP-based biometric enrolment pilot under WURI-Niger and the Interior Ministry. It used dual-iris scanners in the four communes of Tanout, Mayahi, Doutchi and Tagazar. This open-source identity track runs separately from the regional AES card.

Digital sovereignty

Public enrolment for the AES biometric passport opened in Niamey on 15 September 2026. The Libyan firm Al Itissal Al Jadeed runs the identity infrastructure. The passport completed the AES states' replacement of the ECOWAS travel regime. Memoranda of June 2025 give the firm the design, financing, construction and maintenance of the passport and electronic identity card systems. The agreements' terms on data jurisdiction, portability and exit had not been published as at September 2026. Nor had the location of the biometric identity database, or the law it is held under.

In June 2026 civil works on the national data centre were finished and its technical equipment was being installed. Its feasibility study sized it to host government applications and to sell hosting to businesses. No instrument fixing where state data is held, or under whose law it is processed, was in force as at September 2026. Personal data may leave the country only for states offering equivalent protection, with authorisation. That power passed to the justice and interior ministries when the data protection authority was abolished in June 2026.

The 2025 finance law gave 300 million CFA francs to interconnecting the customs information systems of the AES states. In July 2026 Niger sent its civil-registration, digital-agency, biometric-card, legal and finance officials to study Burkina Faso's identification system.
