Status values. Implemented — in operation or in force. Piloting — running with a limited user group or in a controlled environment. In development — build or drafting under way, not yet operating. Planned — announced or provided for, no build or draft on record. Enacted — an instrument passed into law; pair with a qualifying clause for its in-force date. Under review — a law or policy currently being reconsidered. Discontinued — closed or superseded. Not held — the base carries no reliable statement of status; these are the gaps to fill and are listed again at the end.

Summary of position

The Gambia's identity and payment spines both entered service inside twelve months. The National Identity Management System launched on 1 July 2026, issuing instant GAM ID cards from a state data centre opened the same day, with nationwide enrolment from 4 August 2026; no enrolment figure has been published since. Payments clear through BANTABA 2.0, launched by the Central Bank on 15 December 2025.

The country has a data-protection statute and no visible regulator. The Personal Data Protection and Privacy Act was enacted in 2025 with extraterritorial scope and an independent Commission provided for; nothing held states whether that Commission has been constituted.

The most specific claim the state makes for its own digitalisation is fiscal. The finance minister reported tax collected rising from D11 billion in 2022 to D25 billion in 2025 and attributed it to digitalisation of collection rather than to rate rises — an attribution by the collecting side rather than an evaluation of it.

Two questions about foreign operators are open: Starlink is still unlicensed after a refusal stated in May 2026, and 80% of the state mobile operator passed to YCELL on 9 July 2026.

The law is in force and its regulator is not on record. The Personal Data Protection and Privacy Act 2025 ends a legislative process stalled for several years: extraterritorial jurisdiction, seven lawful bases, a prohibition on processing genetic, biometric, health, racial and political data absent a lawful basis and a specific condition, and rights of access, rectification, erasure, objection and protection from solely automated decisions. It provides for an independent Commission. Nothing held states whether that Commission has been constituted, staffed or funded.

Two further bills would complete the frame, and both are unfinished. The Communications Bill 2025 passed second reading on 2 March 2026 and went to committee; it covers electronic communications, broadcasting, electronic commerce, electronic signatures, computer misuse and cybercrime in one instrument, and the Press Union set out the media laws it wants repealed alongside it on 18 July 2026. The State Intelligence Service Bill was validated at a workshop reported 31 July 2026 and is reported to require judicial warrants and oversight for intrusive measures. Its text is not held and does not appear in the National Assembly's bills library, checked 2026-08-08, so those provisions are held as reported rather than as drafted.

Policy sits ahead of instruments. The National Data Policy 2025 is still a draft with no adoption on record, and the Master Plan 2024-2034 carries no mid-term review or costed plan. The National Cybersecurity Strategy and Action Plan 2022-2026 runs out at end-2026; the bodies it designs, GM-CSIRT among them, are not evidenced as standing. Regional cooperation runs through Senegal: the two regulators signed a cooperation and free-roaming agreement in June 2026 with no commencement date given.

External financing for the digital programme is concentrated in one operation and thin elsewhere. The World Bank's regional digital integration programme carries a US$50m equivalent allocation for the Gambia, approved on 7 November 2023 inside a wider US$293m regional operation; no disbursement figure, results framework or mid-term review has been published for the Gambian share. The same programme funds the National Labour Digital Skill initiative at US$3.35m against a target of 280 certified, with no completion figure published since. The African Development Bank's digital government project remains at pipeline stage, at about US$1m, and the Gates Foundation's payment-system pilot has closed with no evaluation held. The domestic side of the ledger is not visible at all: no appropriation act, budget speech or estimate showing the digital votes for the communications ministry, the regulator or the digital programme is held.

Hosting arrived in 2026, in two facilities ten weeks apart. QGroup House, the first data centre, was inaugurated on 30 April 2026, built by QGroup and QCell with Huawei as vendor, and the state's National Data Centre at Abuko opened on 1 July 2026 carrying the national identity system. Neither facility's capacity, tier rating nor operating cost is published, and nothing states who operates them.

The backbone is under a modernisation contract and not yet modernised. GAMTEL signed a US$50m partnership with SYSROAD, announced 5 January 2026, to take national capacity from 40 Gbps to 800 Gbps; no completion date or milestone schedule accompanies it. The network it upgrades was built on a US$25.9m China Eximbank concessional loan whose servicing fell into arrears, repayable by GAMTEL and collateralised against an escrow balance the lender can reach; nothing published since 2020 states the network's condition, utilisation or debt position.

Satellite access is unresolved. The Minister of Digital Economy said on 24 May 2026 that he had refused to approve Starlink's permit, on the ground that the operator lacked the competencies and equipment for a VSAT permit and required a general satellite licence instead. An analyst argued in February 2026 for data-localisation and lawful-interception conditions before any approval; no such conditions appear in an adopted instrument.

Identity is the spine, and it changed hands in January 2026. Card production moved from a foreign concession to an eight-year build-operate-transfer contract with Margins ID carrying a 70/30 revenue share and a phase-2 data centre, with ten-year card validity. No contract value is published. Underneath sits the electronic civil registration system, which registered about 1.17m people with a unique identification number and remains the interoperability backbone; the June 2025 commitment to integrate it with cards and the passport has produced no published milestone. The social registry stands at 203,022 households across 40 of 43 districts, unchanged since April 2024.

Payments are interoperable at the switch and plural at the front. BANTABA 2.0 clears in real time across banks, mobile money, microfinance institutions and fintechs. Government collection runs through three separate arrangements: Yonna Wallet, authorised on 2 March 2026, the SUTURA platform wired in by memorandum in July 2026, and the revenue authority's tax invoicing, live since 9 July 2025. None publishes a transaction volume or a fee structure. Separately, UNDP is piloting blockchain-based payment distribution with FreedomPay Wallet, grouped with pilots in Haiti, Syria, Kenya and Guatemala said to cut distribution costs from about 10% to as little as 2%; no Gambia-specific volume or beneficiary count is published.

Tax administration is where the state's digitalisation claim is loudest and least verifiable. The digital excise tax stamp is studied by five African revenue administrations, and the authority attaches to it a claimed 95% reduction in smuggling of excisable goods, for which it gives no method and which no independent party has verified.

Interoperability has a blueprint and no adoption: the National Enterprise Architecture was validated under GovStack on 22 May 2026 and is not adopted, funded or implemented. Where exchange does run, it runs through customs — the three-month roadmap agreed with Senegal on 16-17 July 2026 is the most operationally specific data-sharing commitment the base holds.

Statistics are the most complete system in the stack. The fifth labour force survey was disseminated in July 2026 with the first comparison across three rounds. Both volumes are donor-financed and no domestic budget line for the series is established.

Nothing in the base establishes an artificial-intelligence position for The Gambia — no policy, no strategy, no readiness assessment and no ministerial statement.

The technology-sector position is the disposal of the state mobile operator. Government told lawmakers in December 2025 that YCELL had bid D6.7 billion for 80% of GAMCEL, and the agreement signed on 9 July 2026 carries a GMD 6.1 billion modernisation commitment covering 4G and 5G radio access, core, operations and billing systems, hybrid tower power and revenue assurance. The base does not reconcile the two figures, and no post-transaction financial position for either operator is published.

One announced platform has produced nothing. A memorandum with the Kalp Foundation for a blockchain-based Gambia One platform was signed in January 2025; nineteen months later nothing held records a build, a pilot, funding or a withdrawal. The other innovation activity on record is a youth showcase on 9 July 2026, with no follow-on funding.

Inclusion

Access to services

System or instrument Status ↗ source Milestone
Affordability of internet access Not held —

Skills work is small. The National Labour Digital Skill methodology was validated on 23 December 2025 against a target of 280 certified, with no completion figure since. A digital rights programme trained 400 women and girls across four regions between September 2025 and March 2026, and its implementer names unreliable internet, high data costs and unreliable electricity as the obstacles to continued practice — the closest thing the base holds to a demand-side account of access.

On access itself there is no domestic measure. DataReportal's commercial compilation put internet users at 45.9% of the population in March 2025; no domestic price basket, affordability survey or regulator tariff series is held against it.

Nine are areas the base holds nothing on: the power behind the digital estate, the capacity that runs it, rural data capture, open data, public participation, artificial intelligence, and European, Indian and Gulf cooperation.

The other four are objects the record points at and does not hold. The data-protection Commission is provided for in an enacted statute and appears nowhere else. The legal basis on which identity cards were issued in Mauritania, Congo and Togo was questioned by the opposition in August 2025 and has drawn no answer.
