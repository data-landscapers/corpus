Status values. Implemented — in operation or in force. Piloting — running with a limited user group or in a controlled environment. In development — build or drafting under way, not yet operating. Planned — announced or provided for, no build or draft on record. Enacted — an instrument passed into law; pair with a qualifying clause for its in-force date. Under review — a law or policy currently being reconsidered. Discontinued — closed or superseded. Not held — the base carries no reliable statement of status; these are the gaps to fill and are listed again at the end.

Summary of position

Eswatini's digital agenda is well financed and thinly founded. The Digital Eswatini Project secured more than E1.06bn of World Bank financing for a 2027 rollout, and the citizen application it funds is live. What sits beneath it is not: the national identity card does not meet the criteria for digital identification, the population register held is a 1984 paper register, and the Digital Landscape Assessment found nine of twenty-one ministries keeping no regular backups.

The data-protection institution moved from paper to practice in the period: the Act has been in force since 2022, and the authority opened its first major investigation in June 2026, into a ministry that published unblurred national identity documents. Payments are the most deliberately built layer — an interoperability-first switch, an e-KYC registry and a FinTech Strategy — against more than 90% of person-to-business payments still made in cash.

Coverage is not the constraint: the ICT ministry puts mobile coverage at 95% against mobile-internet subscription of 57%, blaming data prices and paper-based public services, and data costs 3.47% of gross national income per head, above the international affordability threshold. The regulator proposed on 20 July 2026 raising the minimum service standard from 2 Mbps to 10 Mbps; the 2 Mbps standard remains in force.

International capacity rose from 47 to 72 Gbps, above target, on a World Bank-supported fibre rollout; the Digital Eswatini Project is to connect 1,031 public institutions — 859 schools, 134 health facilities and 38 tinkhundla centres; and a government Starlink gateway was commissioned in April 2026 to reduce dependence on cross-border fibre.

The state's own estate is the weak point, on the government's own evidence. The Digital Landscape Assessment endorsed on 21 May 2026 found nine of twenty-one ministries keeping no regular backups, eleven with no ICT maintenance contract, and the finance ministry running mainframe software past end of life. Only 31 of 419 government services are online, and eighteen ICT professionals support the whole assessed estate. The same report names near-universal dependence on one hosting provider as a structural concentration risk.

That provider is the National Data Centre, on which the base holds an operator's prospectus disclosing no capacity, tenant, uptime tier or utilisation. Its power supply was still being sourced in February 2026, five years on.

The payments layer is the most deliberately designed part of the stack. The Eswatini Payment Switch launched Fast Payments at Ezulwini on 11 December 2024, phase one of three, connecting four commercial banks, the building society and three mobile-money operators — built for the people banks do not reach. Open banking and card, point-of-sale and ATM switching are still to come; three of eight participants were live on launch day and no source held confirms whether the rest followed. The National FinTech Strategy 2025-2030 targets 500,000 e-KYC users and 80% interoperability by 2028, and the central bank is standing up the e-KYC registry both depend on.

What has not moved is behaviour: more than 90% of person-to-business payments were still cash-based in August 2025. Two July 2026 developments bear on why. The central bank launched the FinScope 2026 survey, its governor stating that the 87% access rate recorded in 2018 measured access and not use; and a parliamentary committee put the price of a transaction on the record — a withdrawal of E4,000 attracting E79 to E89 in fees — ordering a benchmark against SACU and Common Monetary Area banks. Cross-border, Eswatini is not a PAPSS participant and no accession date is stated, while low-value Common Monetary Area flows move onto a regional scheme with no timetable or mandating instrument on file.

Identity is the foundation the rest is built without. The national identity card does not meet the criteria for digital identification, and production of even that card was suspended in late September 2025 and resumed on 22 February 2026, disrupting banking, employment and travel for months. Electronic IDs are planned for the 2027 rollout. The Births, Marriages and Deaths Registration Act 1983 has run a compulsory civil register since 6 January 1984, not shown to be digitised. A proposal to require DNA testing before issuing birth certificates would cost about 46% of the Home Affairs budget.

The citizen-facing layer shipped ahead of that foundation. Government In Your Hand launched in May 2025; six weeks later a reporter could locate public data on it, which the e-government services director conceded rather than denied, citing the absence of a proper digital ID and digital signatures.

The Data Protection Act 2022 took effect on 4 March 2022, with fines to E5,000,000 or 2% of annual turnover and a SADC-adequacy model for cross-border transfers. The World Bank recorded the office as not yet operationalised in July 2025, three years on.

The authority ran a campaign with a 31 March 2026 registration deadline for organisations collecting personal data, then opened an investigation into the Ministry of Home Affairs for publishing unblurred national identity documents, which missed the 72-hour notification duty. In July it moved to sectoral rule-making, beginning validation of advisory guidelines on processing personal health data, to be published as a regulatory instrument under the Act. Eswatini's bilateral health agreement with the United States, and the data-sharing agreement attached to it, are withheld and in litigation since 2 April 2026.

The National Data Governance Policy followed the same order: procured through a tender in July 2025, a first workshop in April 2026, and validated on 27 July 2026 with a stated cycle of 2027 to 2031 — text unpublished. In the Senate on 10 August 2026, a senator warned the country remained exposed for lack of the policy; the ICT minister said she was targeting completion before end-2026 and that government had yet to decide between a standalone data-governance law and amendments to existing legislation.

The same body regulates communications and polices data protection, and the sponsoring ministry confirmed on the record on 4 August 2026 that implementation, enforcement and oversight do not fall within its mandate but are vested in ESCCOM. In the same answer it said it had completed the Online Media Publishing Guidelines 2026, introducing publisher registration bound to accuracy, privacy protection, child safeguarding and the responsible use of AI. Neither their text nor a commencement date is public.

No national AI strategy or statute is held. What exists instead are two instruments that reach AI without being AI instruments: ESCCOM's November 2025 guidance urging broadcasters to manage AI ethically and transparently against deepfakes, and the Online Media Publishing Guidelines 2026, whose AI provisions ride into force on a publisher-registration regime. An AI-use rule enforced by a registration requirement is bounded by that requirement's reach.

The stated posture is skills and governance first. The prime minister told the Global Dialogue on AI Governance in Geneva in July 2026 that small and developing nations should not become mere consumers of technologies developed elsewhere, urging investment in local innovation, research, digital literacy and computing infrastructure, framed around the National 4IR Strategy.

Foreign partnerships carry the sector. The Google Cloud EMEA memorandum was established as a cloud-services and e-government framework rather than a monetisation deal after a cabinet controversy; no value or term has been published. A UAE-delivered women's digital academy took its first cohort of 50 in July 2026, designed, staffed and certified abroad, and digital transformation was named a priority of the Taiwan relationship in August 2026 with nothing signed or costed. Domestically, Letshego holds a provisional licence to become a full digital bank.

Access is improving on ownership and unresolved on use: Global Findex 2025, cited in an August 2025 account, puts account ownership at 63%, up from below 50% a decade earlier, led by mobile money.

Capacity is where the ambition is largest relative to the base. The ICT minister set a target of 300,000 digitally skilled citizens in a population of about 1.2m. Delivery so far is a second community innovation hub, opened at Macetjeni on 6 August 2026 after Pigg's Peak, with a third planned, and AI, robotics, coding and cybersecurity courses announced there with no start date, provider or cohort size given. No public research programme in digital or data science is held.

Financing is concentrated and overwhelmingly external. The Digital Eswatini Project was approved at more than E1.06bn, E648m for broadband and E450m for the citizen application, funded by the World Bank. Beyond that loan, tracked non-state digital finance stands at US$55m across four deals for 2020 to 2026, on the wiki's own compilation as of 6 August 2026 — not a published figure. No domestic appropriation line for the ICT ministry, the regulator or the science park is held, so the base cannot say what the state itself puts in against what it borrows.
