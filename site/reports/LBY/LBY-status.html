This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

On 1 June 2026 Prime Minister Abdulhamid Dbeibah launched a National AI Strategy 2026–2030 alongside a National AI Ethics Charter. It replaced the National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence 2025–2030, which the General Information Authority published with UN-ESCWA in October 2025. The authority had described the earlier strategy as the state's highest reference strategy.

The Ministry of Communications and Informatics published a National Artificial Intelligence Policy in 2024. It is the single instrument covering nine regulatory domains, from broadband and cybersecurity to digital identification, payments and e-commerce. In April 2025 the prime minister approved a National Strategy for Government Digital Transformation on four tracks. They are digital government, a digital economy, an inclusive digital society and a safe digital environment.

On 15 June 2025 the Central Bank governor launched the National Financial Inclusion Strategy 2025-2029 at the first Electronic Payment Forum. The projects launched with it included financial-inclusion accounts, full digitisation of government payments and a digital banking identity. The strategy's consumer protection pillar proposes a dedicated unit and a legal framework. Its payment pillar commits to more government-to-person digital payments. None of the accompanying projects carries a published budget, operator or delivery date.

The World Bank's 2025 GovTech assessment found no ministry or department leading public-sector digital transformation. It found no cross-government forum for senior digital officials, and nothing published on progress or spending. The body responsible for data governance sits inside another institution, and its data governance strategy is still planned. There is no open source software policy for the public sector. On 4 August 2026 the Ministry of Civil Service convened the General Information Authority, the Libyan Digital Company and its own IT office to draw up a national digital transformation plan. No timetable, budget, lead agency or published document was set for it.

Libya scored 4.5 out of 100 for effective administration in 2023, last of 54 African states. It scored 20.6 for public administration in 2023, 53rd of 54.

Legislation and regulation

In July 2026 a House of Representatives committee met the National Security Council in Benghazi to prepare a comprehensive cybersecurity law. The committee was created by Speaker's decision 115/2025 and is chaired by MP Asma al-Khoja. No such law had been enacted at that date.

Law No. 5 of 2022 on Combating Cybercrimes gives digital identity standing as a legal concept. It criminalises the unauthorised use of another person's digital identification tools. The eastern authorities began enforcing it in February 2023, arresting a singer and a content creator. In 2025 a near-final draft law on cybercrimes and digital evidence, meant to replace it, was put to experts. Law No. 6 of 2022 on Electronic Transactions regulates the certificates and signature tools of accredited providers. Libya has no data-protection statute.

In August 2025 the Tripoli-based General Authority for Telecommunications and Informatics held that Law No. 22 of 2010 gives it alone the power to issue telecoms licences. It invoked the law when it suspended Huawei's activities that month. The House-appointed government's communications ministry has licensed a national operator under the same law.

In February 2026 the General Information Authority's interoperability plan recorded the law enabling data exchange as still under development. A data-governance guide and an open-data policy were also in draft. An August 2026 arrangement between the interior and labour ministries on expatriate-labour data cites Law No. 12 of 2010 and Law No. 24 of 2023. It sets no legal basis for the exchange itself.

Police record-keeping still ran in 2023 under the 1953 Code of Criminal Procedure, which prescribes paper reports and monthly returns carried to the Public Prosecution. In March 2026 the Tripoli prime minister decreed electronic payment an approved method of collecting state revenues and public dues. Treasuries and public agencies are required to accept it.

Data protection

The Central Bank's Personal Data Protection Regulation, Circular No. 18 of 2025, is Libya's only enforceable data protection rule. It bars supervised banks, fintechs and payment providers from storing personal, financial or credit data outside Libya. It carries fines of LYD 100,000 a violation and became mandatory on 1 July 2026.

In 2024 Libya had no standalone data protection law and no data protection authority. In August 2025 officials said a government draft law and a structure for an authority were in their final stages. Privacy provisions are scattered across the 2022 electronic transactions and cybercrime laws, the 2011 Constitutional Declaration and Act No. 4 of 1990. On 26 October 2026 the National Council for Public Freedoms and Human Rights and a House committee began a joint review of a draft personal data protection law. Council of Europe experts had reviewed the draft, which adds a chapter on artificial intelligence. In 2025 nothing monitored implementation, and nothing was published on complaints or enforcement. Articles 73 to 79 of the electronic transactions law set rules on collecting, processing and transferring personal data. They bind public entities and accredited authentication providers, with consent requirements and a right of access. In 2024 no Libyan law required anyone to notify a data breach within a set time.

In the first quarter of 2025 the Interior Ministry handled 135 cybercrime cases, most concerning social media, and seized 188 devices. Cases reached 470 in the first half of 2025, against 604 in all of 2024. In January 2026 the Attorney General's findings on identity fraud suspended procedures at the implicated civil registry offices. The National AI Ethics Charter of 1 June 2026 requires human decision-making to stay central to AI in health, justice and security. Digital freedom scored 42.9 out of 100 in 2023, 33rd of 54 African states and 7.2 points down since 2014.

Regional collaboration

Libya scored 2.2 out of 100 for regional integration in 2023, last of 54 African states. It is a member of the Arab Maghreb Union, COMESA and the Community of Sahel-Saharan States.

In October 2026 a project official said Libya's fibre had reached the Egyptian border, and that work on a link to the Chadian border had begun. The link is to be finished by the end of 2026, working with Chad's authorities and operators. It is offered as a route to Europe over the Medusa cable for Chad, Niger, Mali, the Central African Republic and Burkina Faso.

In June 2025 a Libyan biometrics firm won a contract to modernise Niger's passport and national identity systems. The Central Bank of Libya joined the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System at the end of 2025, its twentieth country. The bank is listed in the system's published network coverage for June 2026. UN-ESCWA prepared the 2025 artificial-intelligence strategy with the General Information Authority.

In March 2025 the House-appointed government's communications ministry granted a unified telecoms licence to OZON Aljaded under Law No. 22 of 2010. The Tripoli authority reads the same statute as reserving all licensing to itself. In August 2025 it cited ITU principles on member states' sovereignty when it suspended Huawei.

Standards

The Central Bank of Libya publishes LYPay's technical documentation, API specifications and compliance guidance on a public developer portal (2025). In 2026 the national standards centre was Libya's ISO member body, taking part in one technical committee and observing five.

In 2025 the government's interoperability framework existed only in draft or planned form. There was no data quality framework, no monitoring of system uptime and no guidance on replacing legacy systems. In December 2025 the General Information Authority and UN-ESCWA published a roadmap for a four-phase Libyan Government Interoperability Framework. It is a proposed action plan without binding milestones. The April 2026 National Guide for Government Data Governance requires government bodies to integrate through the National Data Exchange. It adopts the once-only principle, under which a citizen gives the state a piece of information once. The authority applies the guide one institution at a time, with no compliance regime.

In 2025 the e-procurement portal was not operational and published no data to the Open Contracting Data Standard. It exchanged no data with other systems and had no governance of registers, security or audit trails. The digital transformation work announced in August 2026 named no interoperability framework, standard or registry. In February 2026 the General Information Authority set out compliance standards to follow the National Digital Accessibility Policy. They are to bind all public and private providers of digital services, and had not been issued at that date.

Public debate and participation in policymaking

In 2025 Libya had no national platform for citizens or businesses to take part in policy decisions, and no facility for petitions or anonymous feedback. It had no platform for feedback on public services and no published service standards. It had no right-to-information law and published no statistics on information requests. Public records were close to unobtainable in 2023.

Rights fell 7.2 points over 2014-2023 to 26.3 out of 100, 47th of 54 African states. It was the steepest fall of Libya's sixteen governance sub-categories. In 2023 media freedom scored 38.2, and freedom of expression and belief 28.1, down 9.0 points over the decade. In August 2026 the eastern government's internal security agency held an activist in Benghazi for five days. He had posted on Facebook and TikTok in support of protests, elections and a constitution.

In 2024 the High National Elections Commission introduced biometric verification of voters in municipal council elections, alongside the digital voter register it keeps. The last national registration drive closed in 2021; only phased municipal registrations have followed. Registration and checks run by SMS to 17017 with the National Number (February 2025). Decision 253 of 8 December 2025 set the rules for updating the register for the presidential and National Assembly elections.

Finance

Financial sustainability

On 11 April 2026 the House of Representatives and the High Council of State agreed Libya's first unified state budget in more than thirteen years. The Central Bank presented it as a step towards ending the country's financial division. No 2026 budget for the digital estate had been read as at September 2026.

The House's Law 9 of 2024, the last budget law before it, voted LYD 1.06 billion to the Civil Status Authority, LYD 1.05 billion of it salaries (FY2024). It voted LYD 41.0 million to the communications ministry's office and LYD 5.5 million to the information security authority in 2024. It financed every chapter from oil and sovereign revenue and printed no external financing (FY2024). Development spending was left to the Development and Reconstruction Fund, outside the law.

The digital lines of the 2024 budget law come to LYD 1.17 billion appropriated, nine-tenths of it civil-registry salaries, and the unenacted 2025 bill proposed LYD 1.13 billion (FY2024).

In 2025 the Central Bank recorded LYD 41.2 million spent on the General Authority for Communications and its affiliates, LYD 5.1 million of it on development. Telecommunications revenue was LYD 224.1 million in 2025. From January to September 2026 it recorded LYD 31.7 million spent on the authority, about 0.04% of state outlay of LYD 88.6 billion.

The Tripoli government's 2025 regulation of public-private partnerships bars a private partner from owning a project or its assets permanently. Assets return to the contracting authority on expiry, and disputes go before Libyan courts. The EU- and French-funded INVEST4Libya runs to 30 June 2029, working with the finance ministry, the Central Bank and the Audit Bureau. It covers public financial management and green and digital investment.

MoUs and other agreements

In March 2026 ZTE and the Tripoli-side state operator Libya Telecom & Technology signed a memorandum of understanding at MWC Barcelona. It covers 4G and 5G planning, e-wallet, e-commerce and content services, and consumer terminals. It binds neither side, and no value or timetable has been published.

In January 2026 LPTIC signed a memorandum with the US engineering firm KBR on infrastructure, data centres, cybersecurity and 5G. No value, timetable or work under it has been announced. In July 2026 the holding company met Nokia about accelerating 5G, with no agreement following.

In July 2025 UNDP and the General Information Authority signed a memorandum to strengthen national data systems.

New investments

The European Union and France fund INVEST4Libya, EUR 11.2 million from January 2026 to June 2029, run by Expertise France. It works with the Ministry of Finance, the Central Bank and the Audit Bureau on public financial management. It also brings digital and green finance into financial regulation and supports digital and green entrepreneurship.

France funded Raqameyon, a national digital-skills programme run by Expertise France from March 2023 to April 2024. It was worth less than a million euros and aimed at public- and private-sector staff, women, entrepreneurs and the Ministry of Education. It followed an earlier French project of the same kind, Raqam-e.

In June 2026 the Central Bank used EPAIX 2026 to launch international Visa acceptance at local terminals and a national stand-in processing service. In April 2026 the UAE's Network International was building a payments and card platform for a Benghazi bank founded in 2024.

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

In 2023 Libya ranked first of 54 African states for mobile communications, at 91.9 out of 100 after a 60.7-point rise over the decade. In 2026 it had no operating internet exchange point: LYB-IX is registered but has no connected peers. In May 2025 the Medusa submarine cable was inaugurated at Misrata, with landings at Tripoli and Benghazi then planned. In October 2026 the cable's shore end was installed at Benghazi, with no ready-for-service date given. A 2021 memorandum with Telecom Egypt set out to upgrade the border cable and carry Libyan traffic over Egyptian cables.

In December 2016 the state backbone had 14,000 km of fibre complete and 23% of a 9,000 km second phase built. In December 2020 the fixed operator completed two backbone routes from Mizda, of 56 km to Nesma and 122 km to Abu al-Ghurab. In July 2021 the fixed operator contracted Infinera for an optical transport network across 60 sites. No account of that network's delivery has been found. Root name servers came online in Tripoli and Benghazi in October 2025. In January 2026 Almadar Aljadid opened Libya's first 5G network, in central Tripoli. In April 2026 the regulator convened operators to move from IPv4 to IPv6.

4G reached 83.7% of the population in 2025, against about 40% in 2021. More than four-fifths of Libyans used the internet in 2024. Mobile connections ran at close to twice the population in 2025. Rural connectivity is limited (2025). On 27 May 2026 floods cut the fibre between Ghat and Tahala; 11 of the 12 sites were back within three days, most over VSAT backup. In September 2026 Starlink's talks with the regulator, open since 2024, had settled no licence terms.

In January 2026 the build was state-led through the Libyan Post, Telecommunications and Information Technology Company, whose subsidiaries include Almadar Aljadid. On 3 October 2026 a holding-company subsidiary reviewed progress on a project for 7,000 telecommunications towers. No count of towers built, cost or completion date was published.

Data Storage

In January 2026 the General Information Authority proposed a National Cloud Computing Policy, developed with UN-ESCWA. The national government data centre and disaster-recovery centre called for in April 2025 had not been built. In 2025 there was no shared cloud platform across government. In 2024 there was no hyperscale capacity, and the facilities listed in Tripoli, Benghazi and elsewhere were small.

In October 2019 Trans-Sahara and the state-owned Tatweer Research announced a containerised data centre for Tripoli. It was to serve government agencies and local companies, and sought PCI DSS certification for the financial sector. Its manager named political unrest, unstable electricity and infrastructure as the obstacles. In September 2026 the state telecoms company's KM-4 site in Tripoli held Tier III design certification, with no capacity disclosed. In October 2026 a private Libyan group set out plans for a Benghazi data centre of more than 160 racks and about 2 MW. It is expandable to about 5 MW, and no operating date was given.

The Central Bank's 2025 data protection regulation bars the banks and payment providers it supervises from holding personal or financial data outside Libya. It became mandatory on 1 July 2026.

Energy

In the early hours of 18 July 2026 several main power stations, Al-Khaleej and Misrata among them, dropped off the grid and about 1,350 MW was lost. The figure is the electricity ministry's, in the government appointed by the House of Representatives. In October 2024 the General Electricity Company's stations ran on record volumes of imported diesel as gas output fell. Electricity reached 73.2% of the population in 2023, up from 70.0% in 2022. Access to energy scored 68.9 out of 100 in 2023, 11th of 54 African states and 5.5 points down since 2014. Rural Libya is far behind urban Libya in access to electricity.

Renewables were about 3% of total final energy consumption in 2021, with no utility-scale grid-connected renewable capacity. In December 2023 the Renewable Energy Authority launched a National Strategy for Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency 2023-2035 with USAID support. A 'Go Green' initiative launched with it targets 500MW of rooftop solar. In 2023 power was close to free at the point of use, under one of the world's heaviest energy subsidy regimes.

In 2019 a data-centre operator named unstable electricity among the reasons one is hard to run in Libya. In November 2020 power cuts at banks' data centres were already stalling e-banking. In August 2026 the Central Bank raised cash withdrawal ceilings, citing repeated power cuts and fuel shortages.

Technical Capacity

In 2025 no ministry or department owned public-sector digital transformation, and nothing was published on its progress or spending. A government entity focused on GovTech existed, but no council led GovTech initiatives and no body monitored digital spending. There was no government programme to raise digital skills in the public sector. In 2023 public administration was one of the three governance areas in which Libya scored worst in Africa.

Libya's first e-passport programme went to an overseas vendor and was deployed from 2021 for diplomatic and special passports. In June 2026 the Central Bank brought in international specialist firms to investigate a cyber incident.

In May 2026 the electronic payment system collapsed under record load. In 2023 e-management in the Ministry of Interior was at an early stage, held back by weak infrastructure and a shortage of specialists. In 2024 the cadastre still rested on incomplete aerial photogrammetry, with survey suspended after 2011.

Cybersecurity

In October 2026 the Government of National Unity ordered the immediate shutdown and isolation of every government system, website and database running on PHP. It cited two critical vulnerabilities flagged by the US National Security Agency. In June 2026 the Central Bank of Libya disclosed a cyber incident affecting some of its technical systems. It isolated the suspect systems and ran the investigation with international specialist firms. The 2022 electronic transactions and cybercrime laws stop at confidentiality duties and penalties. In September 2026 work opened on an integrated national cybersecurity plan. It brings together the heads of the cybersecurity, information and information security authorities. No draft or timetable has been published.

The National Information Security and Safety Authority, created by Decree No. 28 of 2013, accredits electronic authentication providers (2024). Its personal-data policies bind only public bodies, and it cannot take complaints or enforce. Cabinet decision 358 of 15 July 2026 created a National Cybersecurity and Digital Sovereignty Authority under the unity government's cabinet. In July 2026 a House committee met the National Security Council in Benghazi to draw up a comprehensive cybersecurity law. The National Guide for Government Data Governance names cybersecurity-by-design a foundational pillar. It sets digital identity as the reference point for trust across government systems.

ITU's Global Cybersecurity Index put Libya's commitment at 68.09 out of 100 in 2024, in its advancing band. In June 2026 the Central Bank launched a National Stand-In Processing Service to keep payments running through disruption. It followed a record day of 2 million transactions that brought the payment system down in May 2026. In December 2025 the House Defence and National Security Committee warned of manipulation inside the Civil Registry Authority. It had let foreign nationals obtain National ID Numbers unlawfully. On 1 October 2026 the Attorney General's Office was reported to have ordered two National Oil Corporation officials detained. Oil field, production and reserves data had passed to a foreign company.

DPI

Data Exchange

In April 2026 the National Guide for Government Data Governance required government bodies to integrate through the National Data Exchange (LY-NDEX). It adopts the once-only principle. In 2025 the government interoperability framework existed only in draft or planned form, with no data quality framework.

LY-GOVREP has been in preliminary operation since July 2025 and connects 60 government entities under the Cabinet. It carries reports and inspection, not data exchange.

None of the government management information systems Libya has in use exchanges data with any other.

In December 2025 the General Information Authority published an interoperability roadmap with UN-ESCWA, proposing a four-phase framework. No law yet gives authority to share data across government.

On 23 April 2026 the authority pressed the Libyan Zakat Fund to connect its platforms to LY-NDEX. That month it agreed with the Union of Chambers of Commerce to build an electronic Chambers system designed to work with it. On 8 July 2026 a national workshop convened by the Interior Ministry closed. The Central Bank, the Financial Intelligence Unit, tax, customs and the civil and commercial registries worked through barriers to sharing data. The Unified Economic Number of July 2026 is to be implemented by linking databases across public bodies, with no architecture, standard or platform named.

Digital Identity and CRVS

On 13 October 2026 the Attorney General said a review had found more than 315,000 national numbers granted in breach of the law and more than 10,000 passports issued on forged data. He said the civil registry, the national-number system and the voter register do not match. In response 8,662 numbers were suspended, 1,855 passports recalled and LYD 84.5m in salaries and pensions frozen (October 2026). In September 2026 the Public Prosecutor halted 3,090 national numbers issued on forged data at one southern civil-registry office. In January 2026 the Attorney General found 61 family records moved between offices without ever existing in the central database. They had produced National ID Numbers and passports for 225 foreign nationals. The register is held centrally while local offices issue the numbers. At Al-Marj that month a foreign national was jailed and ten numbers cancelled.

Law No. 8 of 2014 makes the 13-digit National ID Number the statutory identifier before every state institution. It is granted at birth from civil registry data and is reserved for citizens. In 2024 it gated a bank account, a SIM card, the main social grants and the vote, checked on paper.

Civil registration scored 75.0 out of 100 in 2023, seventh of 54 African states. In 2020 one automated database linked 12 branches, 133 issuing offices and 400 service offices. By September 2024 the Intilaqa project had matched electronic and paper records for 97% of 1.8 million families. The latest nationwide count of number holders dates from 2013.

In November 2025 the General Information Authority began pilot trials of LY-DNID's second level, verification for citizens. The first level, for government-to-government exchange, was in use, and the third, biometric, was in technical trials. In April 2026 the biometric trials ran with the Central Bank, the anti-money-laundering authority and banks, not yet for customer checks. The 2014 law requires ten fingerprints, an iris scan, a photograph and a signature for every citizen. In May 2023 the passports authority set out a two-year project for an electronic identity card and passport covering eight million records.

Digital Payments and Fintech

The Central Bank put electronic transactions at about LD 865 billion by the end of September 2026. The figure was about LD 764.9 billion at the end of August 2026 and LD 643 billion at the end of July. From January to July 2026 instant payments carried LD 252 billion, mobile banking LD 209 billion and RTGS LD 143 billion. Cards at more than 270,000 terminals carried LD 33 billion in that period of 2026, and e-wallets LD 650 million.

In August 2026 banks still could not meet large cash withdrawals, and some clinics added a surcharge for paying by card. In May 2026 a record day of point-of-sale traffic brought the payment system down. In September 2026 the banking forum behind the Zero Cash initiative dropped it, because the central bank never issued its framework.

A 2020 National Payments Council regulation licenses electronic-payment and wallet providers. The bank's 2026 strategy for digital payments sets eight initiatives, from instant payments and a national switch to open banking. The bank has operated LYPay and OnePay since 2024. It publishes the scheme's API specifications openly, and LYPay carries person, merchant and business transfers on the same rails. Banks were directed to activate QR payment by 8 March 2026, and SoftPOS was launched in June 2026. In September 2026 the governor put a pilot for joining China's CIPS system at early 2027. A private bank is to send payments to China through Alipay, capped at US$5,000 a transaction (September 2026).

In September 2023 the social affairs ministry issued more than one million e-payment cards for the wives' and daughters' allowance, paid through the central bank's banking operations.

Access to banking scored 8.4 out of 100 in 2023, 44th of 54 African states. In 2026 citizens could send up to LD 100,000 a day person-to-person, and resident foreigners LD 50,000. The wallet opened to foreigners in March 2026 is issued on a passport and residency card. Decision No. 247 of 2024 added electronic collection of income tax from 15 May 2024.

Registries

On 17 July 2026 Decision No. 348 authorised the Ministry of Economy and Trade to implement a Unified Economic Number for every registered economic entity. A National Council chaired by the minister sets its standards. No timetable, budget, vendor or legal basis for the linkage is stated. In November 2025 the electronic commercial registry added an online letter certifying a company's registration.

In 2024 the cadastre covered about two-thirds of the country on incomplete aerial photogrammetry, with no spatial database. The Real Estate Registration Authority was shut for a decade and only partly reactivated in 2022. In January 2024 some branches had about 85% of records archived electronically, and the authority issued its first electronic certificate in April 2025. The company register went online in February 2021, and fee e-payment was being introduced in September 2025. Registration is still run separately by the Tripoli and Benghazi authorities, and the one-stop shop began reactivating in February 2026. Libya Post's national addressing system has issued over 762,580 addresses (2026). A June 2026 project would give every registered company an address tied to its location.

In October 2023 UNICEF announced a Unified Social Registry with the Tripoli government. In June 2025 a draft was approved to bring assistance programmes' data into one database, which is still under development. In 2021 an evaluation found no working data-sharing between the social affairs ministry and the Social Security Fund. The principal cash transfer verifies beneficiaries against the Civil Status Authority's database. In July 2025 the wife and children grant was linked to the civil registry electronically. In June 2026 arrears were paid only to families who had updated their data online. On 3 August 2026 the interior and labour ministries agreed to build a unified database for expatriate labour, designed around inspection and enforcement.

The electoral register has stood at about 2.87 million since the 2021 drive.

Sectoral management information systems

When September 2026 salaries were released, about 1.75 million of 2.2 million public-sector employees, 79%, were on the Ratibak Lahzi system. The Central Bank was still automating complaints about undisbursed salaries with the Ministry of Finance.

About 450,000 public-sector employees remained outside that system in September 2026.

In 2025 the Financial Management Information System was under implementation and not yet operational, with African Development Bank support since 2024. The Treasury Single Account had no interface to Central Bank systems. There was no unified budget classification, no debt management system and no HR or linked payroll system. A public investment management system published no project database. In 2023 Libya scored 0.0 for tax and revenue mobilisation and 8.3 for budgetary management, last of 54 on both.

In 2025 a customs system ran on off-the-shelf software, unmerged with tax administration. The customs authority announced full use of ASYCUDA for September 2025, after a 2022 IMF review found its pilot at Tripoli port barely used and insecure. No account of it going live has been found. In 2025 the public pension system did not use the national ID number. In December 2025 the Tax Authority launched a Unified Tax System, with no taxpayer count published.

In 2024 primary-care medical records were on paper, and health information systems were not in routine use. In November 2025 the health ministry and WHO were weighing extending DHIS2 to pharmacy and supply management. On 27 October 2026 the health minister, the digital economy minister of state, WHO and UNDP discussed a health digitisation plan for 2026–2030. No text of the plan has been published. A health number system launched in January 2024 is presented as the route to an electronic health file. A survey of 182 health-system staff in February 2025 found the electronic health record decreed in 2015 still not in use, with finance the main obstacle. In July 2024 the Education Management Information System was launched with UNICEF, after a 2022 assessment found no fully functioning one. In 2023 the Attorney General's Office had digitised 400,000 case archives. In September 2025 Expertise France advertised a post to pilot a digital case management system with it.

Other GovTech and e-Gov

In 2025 Libya's online public service portal was informational only. A business cannot be registered through it, and there is no government app for public services. There was no job portal, no online pension service and no published service standard. A Dawlati e-government platform was due to launch on 1 May 2024 in Libya Post branches. Cabinet Decision 705 of 26 December 2024 established it under the Government Communication Centre and gave its documents the legal force of paper records. No account of the launch has been found.

By June 2026 visa issuance had moved fully online through the Wafed and E-Visa systems, which had issued about 6,000 e-visas. In the week to 29 October 2026 the passports authority received 773 applications and approved 610. The Unified Export Window processed LD 79.5 million of exports from April to July 2026. It had 354 companies registered, shipping to more than 20 destinations. Since 5 August 2026 vessel-arrival notices and cargo manifests have been filed online at Tripoli and Al-Khoms.

Cabinet Decree 600 of 31 October 2024 put tenders through a single electronic platform. In July 2025 the Attaat platform carried tenders from 189 government entities and 626 bids. The Tax Authority's ly.tax portal offers online registration and payment. In 2025 there was no electronic invoicing.

In July 2026 the Ministry of Civil Service was scoping e-services with Huawei Libya, with no agreement announced. In October 2026 the Audit Bureau presented its Athar platform to more than 140 bodies it oversees.

Digitalisation

Digitalisation of sub-national government

Abu Salim municipality in Tripoli began moving its staff records onto an electronic archive in September 2026. By May 2026 the local government ministry's unified citizen service window was in use in Tripoli Centre and Abu Salim. A citizen service centre had opened in Ghadames, and other municipalities were to follow. In April 2026 Tripoli Centre began developing BaladiyaBOT, a digital assistant for municipal services. In 2025 the EU and UNDP set up the first municipality-led business incubator in Tripoli with the ministry.

In 2026 the postal authority's digital addressing system was live nationwide. In 2023 the Interior Ministry's e-management was at an early stage, held back by weak infrastructure and few specialists. In August 2026 the national digital transformation plan under preparation listed digital systems for local administration among its aims.

Rural digital data capture

MerWat-Libya, live since March 2024, lets the agriculture and water ministries and the remote sensing centre share land and water data. It is not part of the national data exchange. In 2020 the civil registration authority's service offices reached rural areas, working to one central database. Rural primary schools kept student, attendance and staff records on paper in 2022. In 2024 primary-care medical records remained paper-based.

In 2023 the Ibrahim Index left all four of Libya's rural-economy indicators unmeasured. The rural economy is the only one of its sixteen sub-categories with no score for Libya.

Technology

AI

In October 2025 LIBIGPT, a domestic generative-AI system, went public. In April 2026 Tadamon Bank was building an AI chatbot and voice service with an Indian vendor. In early 2026 the audit bureau began building an in-house AI model, Diwan Bot, for audit analysis. The labour ministry's AI-supported platform was about 30% complete in April 2026.

Libya's June 2026 AI strategy commits the Tripoli government to a National Data Exchange Platform by 2028. It aims to automate half of government transactions and digitise 70% of paper records by 2030. It also plans AI models to detect financial fraud and money laundering. The communications ministry's 2024 National Artificial Intelligence Policy remains the single published instrument covering AI alongside eight other domains.

ICT Industry

In June 2025 the Libyan biometrics firm Al Itissan Al-Jadeed won a contract to modernise Niger's passport and national ID systems. At home, HID Global was selected for Libya's first e-passport programme, deployed from 2021 for diplomatic and special passports. In 2025 Libya had no policy to support GovTech startups or private investment in them. It gave startups no innovation financing and no procurement priority, and published nothing on the support it provides.

Innovation ecosystem

Tatweer Research in Benghazi, an AfriLabs member hub, runs the Enjazi competition, the Benghazi Hackathon and the TEC incubator (2025). In 2025 the local government ministry, the EU and UNDP set up the first municipality-led business incubator in Tripoli. In 2025 there was no visible venture capital activity.

In 2025 Libya had no national strategy on disruptive technologies. Its public-sector innovation function existed on paper, with nothing published on its results. Libya is absent from WIPO's 2024 Global Innovation Index, which profiles 133 economies.

In January 2026 Jumhouria Bank launched electronic payment at petrol stations, and in June 2026 the Central Bank launched SoftPOS.

Capacity

Literacy

The ITU's Digital Development Dashboard, on data to December 2022, records Libya's ICT skills indicators as not available. In 2025 there were no government digital skills programmes for citizens or schools.

Libya's education score was 42.6 out of 100 in 2023, 41st of 54 African states and 4.1 points down over the decade. The fall came from equality of access, while quality held flat at a low level. The score rests on a partial set of indicators, several with no value for Libya. In 2024 the Open Data Inventory placed Libya's data on education outcomes and facilities near the bottom.

In July 2024 the Ministry of Education launched an education management information system with UNICEF. In 2023 the French-funded Raqameyon programme set out to put a digital training platform into a handful of schools. It also set out to train a small number of teachers and ministry staff.

Training and skills

In November 2025 the Ministry of Planning launched an institute offering a professional master's in AI and cybersecurity, run with UNDP. In 2025 the administration had no strategy of its own to raise public-sector digital skills.

The October 2025 AI strategy covered data-science and AI training. Its June 2026 successor commits to training staff in advanced technology and to seeding AI startups. Neither names a delivery institution or a budget. In February 2026 Libya and India announced cooperation on training national cadres in quantum computing and AI, with no start date.

France funded Raqameyon, a national digital-skills programme that closed in 2024. It followed an earlier French project, Raqam-e, and aimed at public- and private-sector staff, entrepreneurs and women. In February 2024 it closed a ten-week data-science course for state technology staff, run with the information authority. In January 2024 the information and information-security authorities launched a professional diploma in law and informatics. In 2025 hubs in Benghazi ran recurring competitions, hackathons and incubation.

Research institutions

In July 2025 UNDP and the General Information Authority signed a memorandum to strengthen national data systems. Its first focus is the 7th National Human Development Report.

The October 2025 AI strategy named university research and development among its priorities. The postgraduate institute launched with UNDP in November 2025 offers a professional master's. In October 2026 the scientific research authority signed a memorandum with the digital economy and AI minister's office for a national database of experts. It also plans an annual forum to assess digital transformation plans, with no budget or date.

Inclusion

Access to services

In March 2026 Central Bank Circular 9 of 2026 let legally resident foreigners open an electronic wallet on a passport or residency document. The Bank of Commerce and Development opened the first without a bank account, capped at LYD 50,000 a day for individuals. Citizens' ceilings are higher. Nothing comparable reaches undocumented migrants, asylum seekers or stateless residents.

Law No. 8 of 2014 reserves the National ID Number to citizens. In 2024 the number was a precondition for a bank account, a SIM card, social grants and a vote, checked on paper. In 2021 lacking it turned otherwise eligible people away from the main social grants, migrants and refugees among them. In June 2025 Libyans holding only a partial administrative number protested against recommendations that would leave them outside. By the end of 2025 UNHCR had registered 108,455 refugees and asylum-seekers, but Libya does not recognise its documents.

In October 2021 the prime minister's Citizen Service Centre took over 4,000 requests on its first day. The communications authority opened its Tawasul complaints platform in December 2024, and the agriculture ministry opened one for farmers in April 2026.

In 2025 the online public service portal was informational only. From 9 August 2026 daily cash withdrawal limits were set nationwide, highest in the south. Clinics were charging more for card payment than for cash in August 2026.

In February 2026 the General Information Authority and UN-ESCWA began implementing the April 2025 National Digital Accessibility Policy. Its mandatory standards, still to come, will cover persons with disabilities and the elderly. Equal access to public services scored 37.8 out of 100 in 2023, 29th of 54 African states.

Digital divides

In 2021 a mobile broadband data-only plan cost 3.6% of gross national income per capita, above the UN Broadband Commission's 2% target. The low-consumption data and voice basket cost 2.3% and the high-consumption basket 6.2% in 2021.

Most Libyans used the internet in 2024, and 4G reached the large majority of the population in 2025. Internet and computers is one of Libya's most improved indicators in the decade to 2023. Rural connectivity is limited (2025). In May 2026 floods cut whole clusters of south-western sites that hang off single fibre routes. Most were restored over satellite backup.

Data

National statistics

The capacity of Libya's statistical system scored 15.2 out of 100 in 2023, last of 54 African states and 20.2 points down since 2014. It is the third most deteriorated of Libya's 96 Ibrahim Index indicators. As at 2023 only a labour survey had been run in the previous ten years, of the five survey types the World Bank tracks. No agricultural or business census falls within the last twenty years. In July 2026 the planning ministry's statistics director said no census had been held since 2006, owing to political and security conditions. The statistics bureau's 28th Statistical Book estimates the 2023 population at about 7.25 million. In 2023 data sources scored under 20 per cent, the weakest of the five pillars. Data services and data products both sat at 50 to 69 per cent in 2023.

In 2023 Libya was unscored on 22 of the Ibrahim Index's 96 indicators, including education enrolment and completion. None of the index's public perception measures was scored for Libya in 2023.

In September 2024 the civil registry's electronic records were matched against paper files across its offices. Health reporting runs on DHIS2, with WHO offering technical support in November 2025. In July 2025 UNDP and the General Information Authority signed a memorandum to strengthen national data systems. The bureau's 2024 trade bulletin draws on National Oil Corporation data and partner countries' records collected by the UN.

Open data

On 27 November 2025 the General Information Authority launched an Open Government Data Platform. By February 2026 it carried 91 datasets from nine government entities across eight sectors. The open-data policy that accompanies it was in draft in February 2026. On 14 October 2026 the authority met sectoral information centres and ministries to fold a national survey into the final draft of a National Policy for Open Data and AI. A UN-ESCWA expert presented practice elsewhere.

The World Bank's 2025 GovTech assessment recorded no open data portal and no right-to-information law. In 2023 public-records accessibility scored 3.0 out of 100, 53rd of 54, with disclosure of public records at 12.5. Disclosure fell 12.5 points over 2014-2023. In 2024 Open Data Watch found nothing published openly on agriculture, pollution, crime or poverty. No category scored above 4.5 out of 10 in 2024, and education facilities scored zero.

The Libyan state publishes no results, spending or performance information on any of the sixteen areas the GovTech Maturity Index tests for transparency in 2025, from cloud services and interoperability to data protection, the right to information, digital skills, startup support, the public service portal and citizen participation.

The Central Bank publishes LYPay's technical documentation and API specifications on a public developer portal (2025). In 2025 the e-procurement portal published no data to the Open Contracting Data Standard.

Geospatial data

In March 2024 FAO and AICS launched the MerWat-Libya geospatial platform. It lets the agriculture and water ministries and the Libyan Centre for Remote Sensing share land and water data. In September 2025 the grain authority's partnership with the Sahara and Sahel Observatory moved to training on satellite monitoring of cereal campaigns. In 2024 UN-Habitat found the cadastre resting on incomplete aerial photogrammetry, with no spatial database.

Geopolitics

US / hyperscaler activities

In January 2026 LPTIC, the state telecoms holding company, signed a memorandum with the US engineering firm KBR on network and data-centre infrastructure. On 8 October 2026 the communications authority and the US State Department signed a cooperation framework on digital infrastructure, AI, data centres, cybersecurity and satellites.

In December 2023 the Renewable Energy Authority launched its 2023-2035 renewable energy strategy with USAID support. A 'Go Green' initiative launched with it targets 500MW of rooftop solar.

China activities

On 17 August 2025 the Government of National Unity's telecoms authority suspended all Huawei activities in Libya. It cited contracts with illegitimate entities, breach of Law No. 22 of 2010 and ITU principles on sovereignty. Three days later it agreed terms letting Huawei continue, after the company undertook to comply with that law. Huawei has been in Libya for more than twenty years and helped roll out 3G and 4G (2025).

In July 2026 the Ministry of Civil Service was scoping its digital transformation plan with Huawei Libya. In March 2026 ZTE and the state operator Libya Telecom & Technology signed a 4G and 5G memorandum at MWC Barcelona. On 24 October 2026 the Qanwan investment foundation and Huawei's Libya branch signed a cooperation agreement on investment, technology and digital transformation. No value or project was stated. In 2019 a planned private-cloud facility for Tripoli specified a Huawei containerised data centre.

EU activities

In May 2025 the Medusa submarine cable, an 8,700 km system linking eight Mediterranean countries, was inaugurated at Misrata. Its capacity was contracted in 2023 through the Libya International Telecom Company. In July 2026 LPTIC met Nokia over accelerating 5G.

France funded two digital-skills programmes, Raqam-e and then Raqameyon, which ran from March 2023 to April 2024. In 2025 the EU and UNDP set up Libya's first municipality-led business incubator with the local government ministry. In April 2026 Tripoli Centre began developing BaladiyaBOT under a GIZ programme funded by the EU and Germany. EU money funded a prototype online registration tool, the only digital part of a Commercial Registry otherwise run in person.

Gulf/UAE activities

In April 2026 Network International of the UAE was building a payments and card platform for Seraj Islamic Bank. The bank is based in Benghazi and was founded in 2024.

India activities

In February 2026 Libya and India announced cooperation to train Libyan cadres in quantum computing and artificial intelligence. In April 2026 Tadamon Bank engaged the Indian vendor JMR Infotech to build an AI chatbot and voice service.

Digital sovereignty

Libya's June 2026 AI strategy commits the Tripoli government to a Libya Sovereign Cloud in 2026-2027, led by the communications authority. It also plans high-performance computing centres with national operators, to cut reliance on services abroad.

In February 2026 the communications authority said companies were offering data storage without permits, and that it would draft a first data-centre and cloud law. A national cloud computing policy was proposed in January 2026. The Central Bank's bar on banking data leaving Libya binds only the institutions it supervises. No instrument fixing where state data is held was in force as at September 2026.

Since 2021 the international carrier LITC has sold private links to Microsoft's data centres abroad as an Azure ExpressRoute partner. The health ministry's information centre hosts the health statistics system on Al-Madar's cloud, and the Central Bank runs the LYPay and OnePay rails itself. Where the national identity database is hosted had not been published as at September 2026.

LPTIC's January 2026 memorandum with KBR covers data centres alongside 5G and cybersecurity. Its terms on data jurisdiction, portability and exit had not been published as at September 2026.
