Summary of position

Mauritius has an unusually complete digital policy stack and a thin published record of what it delivers. A national AI strategy, a national data strategy, a fintech strategy, a critical-infrastructure cyber regime in force and a 119-page omnibus amending 58 Acts all sit on the record; no user figure has been published for the Mobile ID wallet since it launched in 2024, and none for the national payment switch at all.

The data-protection regime is genuinely strong — about 3,000 enforcement actions a year and an EU adequacy bid — and sits alongside a state interception apparatus described in Parliament as running since 2017 without judicial oversight, for which no reform instrument has been published. The year's legislative volume arrived as a budget instrument, and the Data Protection Act 2017 is not among the Acts it amends.

Hosting is the country's strongest asset. Ten data centres operate across five locations, and the Rose-Belle Tier IV facility is the sovereign-hosting anchor: it carries e-health, the government portal, the Mobile ID wallet, the penalty-points system and government artificial-intelligence workloads, and gives the ageing Government Online Centre the disaster recovery it lacks.

International capacity is ageing and its replacement is not contracted. LION and LION2 still carry most international traffic, and SAFE retires around 2027. Against that sits a prime-ministerial commitment to join the America-India Connect system, behind which is a signed non-disclosure agreement and unfinished negotiation, and two operator-planned landings with no contract.

Satellite is regulated but not authorised. The 2025 decision on non-geostationary broadband requires service through a locally licensed provider, a local point of presence with Mauritian-geolocated addresses, real-time terminal dashboards and provider-retained terminal ownership, and no service has been authorised under it. Domestic opinion is divided on whether it matters given existing coverage.

Cybersecurity is where 2026's law took effect. The critical-infrastructure designation regime came into force on 1 June 2026 with a twelve-month compliance window and penalties up to 25 years' imprisonment and Rs 2m; before it, no framework existed to designate and regulate operators of critical systems. Beside it, the July omnibus would let the central bank compel financial institutions, and others it specifies, into a threat-sharing platform — with the perimeter set by the central bank rather than the cyber authority.

Identity is delivered and unmeasured. The Mobile ID wallet launched on 4 September 2024, billed as the first African wallet built to the international standard, on the legal basis of regulations made the same year; no user figure has been published in the two years since. It sits on a complete civil-registration base, which is what makes it unusual: most states here are building register and credential at once.

Payments are the same pattern. The MauCAS switch is a mature interoperable rail publishing no national transaction volume; the adoption evidence comes from one bank, which reported QR payments up 67% in 2025 and cash falling from 30% to 27% of consumer spend. Around that rail the state is building governance: a fintech strategy of June 2026 and a governance committee given statutory form from 1 September 2026, minister-chaired and expressly not a body corporate.

The cross-border ambition runs ahead of the corroboration. The India payment link of February 2024 is the one established corridor; a Seychelles corridor was announced as launched with no corroboration from the partner or either central bank, and Mauritian participation in the Southern African settlement system reaches the record through a South African spokesperson rather than through the Bank of Mauritius.

The welfare state is being digitised in bulk: 10.5m benefit documents scanned into a future platform serving about 800,000 payments a month.

The plumbing under all of it is old: the standing interoperability instrument is a framework published in 2002 and the standing health strategy one from 2015, neither with a published successor.

The data-protection regime is among the most active on the continent: about 3,000 enforcement actions a year, 99% resolved without proceedings, mandated chief data officers and administrative fines being added; regulations promulgated on 12 June 2026 formalise data protection officers from 1 January 2027; and the commissioner has confirmed the country is pursuing European adequacy.

Against that sits the conduct of the state itself. Parliamentary revelations describe large-scale interception since 2017 — calls, internet traffic and messaging platforms — wired into major operators, accessed by counter-terrorism and national security units without judicial oversight, with the Prime Minister confirming illegal wiretapping. No reform instrument or judicial-authorisation requirement has been published since, and leaked recordings continue to test the framework.

The year's legislation arrived as a budget instrument. The omnibus introduced on 24 July 2026 amends 58 Acts and repeals one, on a staged commencement from 1 September 2026, carrying the fintech committee, the AI City Scheme, the threat-sharing platform, a digital travel authorisation and a virtual-asset solicitation rule that presumes any publicly accessible communication targets Mauritius. Its companion Finance Bill widens what the statistics office may share with the finance ministry. The Data Protection Act is not among the Acts amended.

Two commitments stand unfulfilled, neither with a text or a date: a Freedom of Information Bill announced in January 2026, and a National Data Governance Strategy with Data Protection Act amendments committed in July.

The sharpest domestic objection is to the priority rather than the substance: a named analyst argues that contract enforcement, not technology, is what limits growth, prescribing digitalised case management alongside a small-claims process.

Mauritius put governance before deployment. The national strategy and its guidelines launched on 9 April 2026 govern by impact rather than geography: vendor-neutral, border-agnostic, with mandatory bias audits in financial services and gaming and duties on foreign providers to keep a local representative. They are non-binding and read as a third model beside the scale-first and penalty-first approaches elsewhere.

What the state operator has built is a platform layer rather than a compute one. The AI Registry went live on 9 July 2026 as a directory of Mauritius-relevant models, agents and skills that does not host or sell what it lists, its stack open-sourced. The enterprise platform's school pilot was deliberately narrowed from a student tutor to a teacher assistant after teacher and parent resistance — a rare documented retreat on pedagogical rather than technical grounds. The certification work the operator co-chairs is defining a framework that does not yet exist.

The compute underneath is an assertion: installed accelerators are the operator's own claim, and a domestic critique argues the infrastructure-first sequencing risks obsolescence on delivery.

The legal frame is an incentive scheme, not a governance one: the AI City Scheme and an AI Founder permit at US$1m invested certify a hub and expedite immigration. And the country has no measured baseline of enterprise adoption — commissioned by a chamber of commerce in July 2026, after the strategy and after the budget. Information technology contributes about 5.4% of gross domestic product against an ambition of 10-12%.

What is contracted with the hyperscalers is skills and confidentiality: the Amazon memorandum carries no disclosed value and no binding deliverable, its training running initially in rented premises, and Google already operates a ground station at Baie-du-Jacotet.

The 2026-2027 budget sets the year's clearest training commitment: a 50,000-trainee target for artificial intelligence skills — 25,000 professionals, 12,000 pupils, 8,000 teachers and 5,000 civil servants in one year — with no published enrolment figure and no measure of the base it starts from.

Inclusion has one named sub-national commitment: a ministerial mission to Rodrigues in May 2026 set a cybersecurity working framework, a youth awareness project, and integration of the national sign-on through post office agents.

The 2026-2027 budget is the year's clearest financial commitment to the digital programme: it names artificial intelligence 35 times, funding a Rs 25m national AI learning platform, an investment tax credit of 15% a year over three years, a special economic zone of 83 arpents with preferential data-centre power, e-gates, e-visa and e-judiciary, and a Rs 13m cyber forensic laboratory.

External financing is old: the largest digital-sector commitment on record is a US$73.7m Chinese preferential buyer's credit from 2018, with no repayment or completion statement published since. The commercial money movement runs the other way: one payments gateway reports its Mauritian volume up over 300% year on year, crediting the country's exit from the financial action task force grey list.

Six are subject areas with nothing on record: technical capacity to run the estate, satellite data, rural data capture, local-government digitalisation, Chinese cooperation, and any measure of digital literacy.
