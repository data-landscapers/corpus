This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

The World Bank's 2025 GovTech Maturity Index records the digital transformation strategy as old and due for updating, and a whole-of-government approach as planned or in draft. The cybersecurity strategy in force in July 2026 was the expired 2021-2025 text. On 11 August 2026 the universal-service agency presented a 2026-2030 strategic plan of four pillars and ten projects. Four projects are followed by the supervising ministry at FCFA 60.144bn, and six run on the agency's own funds (2026). It targets 300 newly connected localities, smartphone penetration of 80% by 2028 against 40% now, and more than 15,000 km of backbone fibre lit by 2028. Its starting point is 70% of rural households without internet access (August 2026). No adoption instrument for the plan has been found.

The National Digital Development Strategy 2021-2025 has seven pillars, including digital infrastructure and e-government. It carries a paperless-administration target for 2030. The minister presented the strategy as Côte d'Ivoire Numérique 2030 at VivaTech in June 2025. Strategies on artificial intelligence and data governance to 2030 went to the Prime Minister on 13 March 2025; the OECD's AI policy repository carries the AI text. A seminar in April 2026 aligned the AI, cybersecurity, data-governance and zero-paper strands under the Ministry of Digital Transition. The ministry also has a 2026-2028 roadmap of seven axes, beneath the national strategy and the PND 2026-2030.

The GovTech index records that ministry in 2025 as an established institution with a whole-of-government mandate. Its budget was approved at CFA60.78 billion for 2025, and it presented CFA83.2 billion for 2026 in March 2026. In May 2026 it opened a call for six senior posts to strengthen delivery of the state's digital projects. In December 2025 the economy ministry adopted a 2026-2030 information system master plan of 44 projects at CFA10.156 billion. Its diagnostic found weak IT governance and platforms that are not interconnected. The Digital Acceleration Project shortlisted six firms on 30 September 2026 to support the digital and artificial intelligence compact. The compact's text has not been found.

Legislation and regulation

Loi n°2013-546 of 30 July 2013 on electronic transactions is the legal base of the national public key infrastructure. Decrees of 2014 and 2016 add cryptology, electronic signature and archiving. The 2013 package also has a cybercrime law and Law No. 2013-450 of 19 June 2013 on personal data. That law grants rights of access, correction and deletion, and requires ARTCI's prior authorisation to process sensitive data.

Loi n°2024-352 of 6 June 2024 on electronic communications covers licensing, spectrum, universal service, the regulator's powers and sanctions, and repeals the 2012 ordinance. Ordonnance n°2017-500 of 2 August 2017 governs electronic services. The Council of Ministers of 18 June 2025 adopted a decree formalising the digital nationality certificate, which gave the electronically issued certificate a legal basis. No single standalone law covers the digital identity lifecycle. The biometric card rests on Loi n°2019-566, five articles that make enrolment compulsory from 16 and leave the card's specifications, application and renewal to the Council of Ministers.

The 2026 environmental summary for the Grand-Bassam data centre applies the impact regime of Loi n°96-766 and Décret n°96-894. The project's study went to the Agence Nationale de l'Environnement for validation. Data exchange between public bodies rests on the e-services ordinance and a 2021 decree adopting a general interoperability framework. The binding payments rules are regional, from the WAEMU instruments on electronic money to the central bank's interoperability deadline of 30 June 2026.

On World Post Day, 9 October 2026, the telecoms regulator's head called for the postal network to be adapted to the digital economy. The report records a revision of the 2013 Postal Code under way. Draft net-neutrality guidelines went to public consultation from 6 July to 6 August 2026, allowing zero rating only where it is non-discriminatory and unpaid. The regulator published the operators' responses in September.

Data protection

By July 2026 the telecoms regulator ARTCI had imposed no financial penalty under the 2013 personal data law, despite 210 complaints and 18 inspections. ARTCI is the data protection authority, empowered to investigate and impose administrative penalties. It must authorise the processing of sensitive data and keeps a public register of processing operations. It is the same body that regulates the operators it supervises for data protection. It warned in June 2024 that biometric access and attendance control at work must comply with the 2013 law. Between 26 January and 25 May 2026 a contracted firm collected data for its census of public and private bodies' compliance with the law. No results have been published. On the World Bank's 2025 GovTech Maturity Index the legislation is in force and assessed as effective, with a supervisory authority in place.

Law No. 2013-450 sets conditions on interconnecting personal data files: interconnection must not infringe data subjects' rights, must use appropriate security measures and must respect the principle of relevance. Personal data may go abroad only to a country offering an equal or higher level of protection. The state has meanwhile financed surveillance with Bank of China lending. It paid for video surveillance and licence plate readers across Abidjan, Yamoussoukro, Bouaké, Odienné, Korhogo and San Pedro. It also paid for a national emergency command centre with surveillance towers in cities across the country.

Digital freedom fell 11.3 points over 2014-2023 to 62.6 out of 100, 22nd of 54 African states. Personal Liberties is among the country's ten worst-scoring measures at 32.8 out of 100 and 35th of 54, down 10.1 points over the same decade.

Regional collaboration

The ECOWAS Digital Sector Development Strategy 2024-2029 was adopted at Cotonou on 4 October 2024. The same meeting required all mobile operators in every member state to apply the ECOWAS Roaming Regulation fully by the end of 2025. The ECOWAS Community budget for 2026-2028 has a single ICT-named programme line, with nil actual spend from 2024 and nil in the estimates to 2028.

The BCEAO's interoperable instant payment system went live on 30 September 2025, with coverage still partial at launch. Interoperability became mandatory across the Union from 30 June 2026. Since 2 November 2026 every interoperable electronic-money transfer has had to route through the central bank's platform. In the first days, users in Côte d'Ivoire reported transfers debited but not credited. Côte d'Ivoire leads the Union's cross-border electronic-money flows (2024).

Customs has run a regional data exchange, SIGMAT, since 2019. In September 2026 it was operating on the Abidjan-Bamako corridor, and the two customs administrations adopted a roadmap to correct persisting difficulties. Its deployment between Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire was being prepared in July 2025. On 11 October 2024 the Ivorian and Guinean ministers signed a protocol to interconnect the two countries' fibre-optic networks.

In March 2026 the ECOWAS-World Bank identification programme validated a cross-border strategy for mutual recognition of foundational credentials. Côte d'Ivoire is one of the countries implementing that programme. Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger severed ties with ECOWAS in 2024 and have built a separate Alliance of Sahel States credential stack. It includes a common biometric passport from January 2025, and biometric national ID cards in Burkina Faso from November 2025 and in Niger from March 2026.

A 2026 environmental summary for the Grand-Bassam data centre presents Abidjan as UEMOA's regional centre, where banks and financial institutions may store and move data freely across the Union. It pitches the site as a regional disaster-recovery location. Côte d'Ivoire scored 46.3 out of 100 on Regional Integration in 2023, 28th of 54 African states.

Standards

BCEAO Instruction No. 001-03-2021 requires payment service providers to report to the regional central bank within 24 hours any incident interrupting service for more than two hours. A detailed report is due within three calendar days, and non-compliance carries penalties. Instruction No. 001-01-2024 counts a breach compromising the confidentiality of information systems and data as a major operational incident.

The state IT company SNDI deployed UXP, an interoperability platform, and launched it in September 2025. UXP is an improved version of X-Road developed by Estonia's Cybernetica. SNDI gives the platform's open-source character among its stated reasons for the choice. It traces the decision to a national digital-governance roadmap drawn up in 2022 with the Estonian consultancy Digital Nation. The criteria were speed of deployment, proven security and alignment with the paperless-administration target. In July 2026 the minister called for clearer allocation of responsibility between ministries' information-system heads where one public service needs several agencies.

Version 2.0 of the general information systems security reference framework, issued on 6 October 2025, re-bases it on ISO/IEC 27001 and 27002:2022. The framework and a critical infrastructure protection plan are in force by decree. There is a national CERT, and essential service operators must regularly evidence their security implementation. ANSSI-CI has commissioned a national public key infrastructure as the sovereign root of trust for every certification authority operating on Ivorian territory. ONECI has joined the Secure Identity Alliance's OSIA initiative for open interoperability standards. In 2018 the identity register, the health insurance database and the electoral register were decoupled from one another. Harmonised technical specifications for national ID credentials across the region are a stated WURI objective for 2026. On the network side the regulator is pushing IPv6 adoption. It has made peering a recurring national exercise, from a first forum in August 2025 to Peeringday 2026 in June 2026. In November 2026 it sent draft fibre-deployment standards and a fixed-network quality-of-service protocol to its regulatory council for adoption.

Public debate and participation in policymaking

In 2025 the World Bank's GovTech Maturity Index found no public e-participation platform and none of its six participation measures met. The same index records a citizen feedback mechanism for public services.

In 2023 Deliberative & Participatory Governance scored 73.6 out of 100 and 8th of 54 African states, essentially unchanged over the decade. Public Perception of Political Participation fell 14.8 points to 44.7, 29th of 54. Freedom of Expression & Belief rose 9.3 points to 77.2 and 14th of 54, and Media Freedom fell 5.7 points to 69.3, still 8th of 54. Ivorians' own confidence in free speech is among the ten most deteriorated measures, down 12.6 points to 37.2 and 33rd of 54. Civil Society Space fell 16.7 points to 62.4 and 20th of 54, one of the ten most deteriorated indicators. Freedom of Association & Assembly was unchanged at 50.0.

In October 2022 the public consultation for the Grand-Bassam data centre reached 12 of 15 identified stakeholders and drew opinions from seven. The state IT company published a clarification note in 2025 defending how the interoperability platform was procured. What is public about that platform comes through ministerial and press announcements, with no dashboard or transparency report on its governance and data-sharing agreements. Access to public records improved from a low base over 2014-2023, and accountability and transparency remained the country's worst-scoring governance sub-category, flat over the decade.

Finance

Financial sustainability

The 2026 finance law carries FCFA 4.4 billion for Microsoft licences and support under the e-GOUV project. This recurrent line on the budget ministry's vote stood at FCFA 2.04 billion in the 2024 law.

In 2024 the state's own money carried 88.8% of the FCFA 90.8 billion appropriated to digital lines. Its 68 digital investment lines, voted at FCFA 18.9 billion, closed 2024 at FCFA 27.4 billion spent after in-year revision.

The 2026 law splits capital spending between the Treasury and external financing only in aggregate. Its estimates do not print the source of financing against digital lines. The 2024 settlement accounts show the digital acceleration project PADCI opened in-year at FCFA 500 million, wholly on loan.

Article 18 of the 2026 finance law funds two special accounts of the digital ministry from earmarked levies and fees. They receive FCFA 33 billion for universal service and FCFA 2.58 billion for telecoms regulation (2026). The 2025 figures were FCFA 35.3 billion and FCFA 4.59 billion. The 2026 tax annex assigns the cyber-security agency ANSSI 2% of the tax on telecoms and money-transfer companies. In 2024 the rural-technology levy's transfer to the universal-service agency ANSUT was written down in-year from FCFA 32.3 billion to FCFA 24.4 billion, all of it paid.

ONECI's director-general said in May 2026 that maintenance of the national identity databases is paid from the office's own budget. The Interior ministry's identification transfer to it rose from FCFA 5 billion in 2024 to FCFA 8.79 billion in 2025 and again in 2026. The audit court found identity-card and passport fees collected by ONECI and the private SNEDAI outside the state's accounts (2022 accounts, reported January 2024).

In July 2025 the minister put the recurring cost of equipment upgrades at FCFA 15 to 19 billion a year. That follows FCFA 40 to 50 billion of capital spending, and he called for public-private co-financing (July 2025).

MoUs and other agreements

In July 2025 the Emirati firm G42 Presight signed a non-binding memorandum to collaborate with the digital ministry on the next national digital development strategy. A second non-binding memorandum of the same month covers modernising the public administration. Beyon Connect, Post Connect and La Poste de Côte d'Ivoire signed a memorandum in November 2025 for a six-month OneID and OneBox proof of concept. George Mason University and Cybastion hold a memorandum with the ministry on artificial-intelligence and cybersecurity skills.

In June 2025 Raxio Côte d'Ivoire and ARTCI signed a memorandum for Raxio's Grand-Bassam data centre to host the third point of presence of CIVIX, the national internet exchange. Raxio has a second memorandum on data sovereignty, to host government workloads in its CIV1 facility. Orange Côte d'Ivoire and UNDP signed on 21 May 2026 to take digital skills and youth entrepreneurship beyond Abidjan. An education pact with French Tech Abidjan brings some 75 firms into technical and vocational curricula. Orange Bank Africa and the SGPME signed two partial portfolio-guarantee conventions on 23 July 2026 to widen small-enterprise credit.

New investments

The US Export-Import Bank-backed package Cybastion is authorised to deliver stood at USD 170 million in July 2026. It covers a national data centre, a government digitisation platform and smart border surveillance. EXIM had approved a USD 66 million data-centre guarantee in August 2025 and a USD 47 million financing for the Ministry of Commerce and Industry. Its chairman presented the latter as countering Chinese influence. Both are under EXIM's China and Transformational Exports Program.

Two Bank of China agreements of 31 August 2018, CFA 53,267,282,421 and CFA 8,724,228,100, financed a Huawei contract for the education ministry's data centre and network. They were fully disbursed by September 2022. Loans of CFA 48,316,189,461 and CFA 7,948,624,543, signed 22 May 2019, put fibre and licence-plate cameras into six cities and were disbursed by mid-2021. A CFA 73,931,502,506 loan of April 2020 for a national emergency command centre and towers at 46 sites was 75 per cent disbursed at end-2022. A CFA 72,678,277,635 loan for a third surveillance phase was 66.4 per cent disbursed at end-2022.

An IDA credit of FCFA 84,356,070,200 for phase 1 of the Projet d'accélération digitale was ratified by decree on 4 February 2026. It is the first phase of a USD 330 million World Bank envelope to 2035. The African Development Bank is putting UA 63,968,067 into e-government over 2023–2028 and KfW EUR 20 million into e-governance to 2027. European Union grants of EUR 3.5 million to 2031 for private-sector support and EUR 1,715,000 to 2026 for agriculture and the low-carbon transition do not split out their digital share.

Côte d'Ivoire's digital estate has drawn 112 external commitments, worth about USD 1.5 billion, starting between 2015 and 2026, with windows running to 2031 (November 2026). Most of the money is debt. Bank of China accounts for just under half of the total, ahead of the World Bank and the US Export-Import Bank; about a quarter of the money is approved, with no record yet that it is moving (November 2026). More of it funds state security and government administration than connectivity or inclusion.

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

The World Bank's digital acceleration programme, appraised in September 2025, scopes its connectivity component to public facilities: schools, health establishments, courthouses, social centres, city halls, sub-prefectures and regional directorates. It targets 648,572 people newly using broadband by 2030, among them 20,000 students, 1,250 health employees, 850 in city halls and sub-prefectures and 250 in tribunals. Phase 1 is fenced to Abidjan, Yamoussoukro and twelve regions selected on poverty and low connectivity.

At 30 September 2026 the regulator counted 1,090,434 fixed internet subscriptions, up 59.9% in a year, 59.8% of them fibre. It counted 60.9 million mobile subscriptions, up 0.8%, and 41.4 million mobile internet subscriptions, up 11.2% (September 2026). The regulator measured mobile coverage at 97% of the population and 83% of the territory in 2019. 4G covered 91% of the population in 2022. 41% of individuals used the internet in 2024. International bandwidth was 47 kbit/s per user in 2022.

The regulator's provisional 5G allocation of 19 December 2023 re-used existing frequencies, and the network covered 7% of the population in 2025. On 14 October 2026 the Council of Ministers adopted the decree setting the financial terms for freeing the 3300-3600 MHz band, including compensation for its holders. No definitive award has been found. Orange began selling satellite broadband on Eutelsat KONNECT capacity in January 2026, and MTN struck its own Eutelsat deal for rural white areas in April 2026. The digital minister announced a 12-month provisional licence for Starlink in June 2026.

ARTCI counted 38,915 km of optical fibre at the end of September 2026. One internet exchange point was active in 2025, with 13 to 14 members. ARTCI signed a memorandum with Raxio in June 2025 to host a third point of presence of the exchange, CIVIX, and ran a peering forum in August 2025 and Peeringday 2026 in June. A regional workshop in Abidjan agreed in June 2026 on a consortium of licensed operators to commercialise spare fibre on the TRANSCO CLSG transmission network. Liberia wants that fibre as a terrestrial route through Côte d'Ivoire and Guinea, against its dependence on one submarine cable.

Data Storage

MTN opened a 336 m2 modular data centre at Yopougon on 5 June 2014, and offered colocation at four sites in its 2024 interconnection catalogue. Orange's Tier III GOS data centre at Grand-Bassam, built in 2016 at 1.3MW, serves the operator's 18 Middle East and Africa subsidiaries. MainOne opened a Tier III colocation facility beside its Abidjan cable landing station in December 2019. Raxio's CIV1 at Grand-Bassam opened in 2024, Uptime Institute Tier III certified with 400 racks and 3MW of IT power. ST Digital opened a Tier III data centre at Grand-Bassam in October 2025 with OVHcloud. No global hyperscale provider runs a region in the country.

The National Data Centre at the VITIB zone in Grand-Bassam was just over 20% complete in December 2025, delivered by Cybastion with US EXIM backing. Its opening was targeted for 2027 as at September 2026, at 1.73MW of IT capacity, 228 racks and 10 petabytes of storage. Construction of its sovereign core began in May 2025. EXIM approved a $66m guarantee in August 2025, its first for a data centre in sub-Saharan Africa, equal to 39.6bn FCFA. The budget ministry opened a shared disaster-recovery data centre at Yamoussoukro in July 2021, replicating customs, tax and budget data outside Abidjan. A Tier 3 administration data centre at Yamoussoukro's polytechnic, under the African Development Bank-led e-government project, had its site visited in September 2026. The project counts five administration data centres, four of them functional. Its steering committee noted delays in the works on 18 September 2026.

Under the 2013 data protection law, personal data may go only to a country offering an equal or higher level of protection. The draft cybersecurity strategy for 2026-2030 extends the state's scope to cloud.

Energy

In 2023, 72.4% of Ivorians had access to electricity. Connected firms reported around four electrical outages a month in the World Bank's 2023 enterprise survey. Access to energy rose 14.4 points over 2014-2023 to 56.2 out of 100 and 15th of 54 African states. Infrastructure as a whole is the second largest driver of the country's governance improvement, up 13.8 points to 59.3 and 11th of 54. Rural access lagged urban access by a wide margin in 2020.

Hydroelectricity supplied about 24% of generation in 2023. The World Bank's RISE assessment records a comprehensive off-grid regime in 2024, with the rural electrification programme PDER providing subsidies and provision for private participation. The ministry counted 8,690 electrified localities at end-2024 against 3,887 in 2017, on a coverage rate of 94.3% and an access rate of 98.6%. Mean annual interruption time rose to 26h13 in 2024 from 17h54 in 2021, against a 2030 target of under fifteen hours.

The 2026 environmental summary for the Grand-Bassam data centre lists its operating risks as generator fuel and lubricant spills, wastewater, NOx and VOC emissions, and water consumption.

Technical Capacity

The Ministry of Digital Transition opened a call on 26 May 2026 for six senior posts. They cover artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, digital-economy regulation, information systems, digital transformation of the administration and digital infrastructure. In 2023 the statistical system's capacity scored 44.2 out of 100, against 62.4 for public administration as a whole. The government's public-sector digital skills provision is recorded as only partly in place in 2025.

By April 2026 Huawei's global network operations centre had partnered on Orange Côte d'Ivoire's network monitoring and operations for ten years. The partnership oversees operations across more than twelve subsidiaries and over 100 million customers (April 2026).

The public Ecole Supérieure Africaine des TIC, founded in 2012, is the main domestic ICT school, beside a small set of private and international providers. The organised developer community is concentrated in Abidjan around groups such as Code d'Ivoire.

Cybersecurity

The text in force as at 23 July 2026 was still the 2021-2025 strategy adopted in December 2021, its successor a draft still to be finalised for the Council of Ministers. ANSSI-CI put that draft to stakeholder review that day, on five axes: governance and law, human capital, critical-infrastructure protection, the state's technical capacity, and cooperation. It extends the scope to artificial intelligence, emerging technologies and cloud, and adds monitoring against performance indicators. The draft applies the fifth pillar of the national digital development strategy.

Decree No. 2021-916 brought the General Information Systems Security Reference Framework and a Critical Infrastructure Protection Plan into force. The national response team CI-CERT operates alongside a requirement that essential service operators regularly evidence their security. In July 2026 ANSSI-CI commissioned a national public key infrastructure, the first sovereign root of trust for certification authorities on Ivorian territory. It is named as underpinning electronic signature and digital identity. The country sits in the third, "establishing" tier of the ITU's Global Cybersecurity Index 2024.

In July 2026 Moov Africa placed inbound international A2P messaging, the channel for one-time passcodes, under an exclusive messaging and firewall agreement with Omobio. A US technology firm and Cisco inaugurated a security operations centre in Abidjan on 5 November 2026, billed as a regional hub. ECOWAS ministers adopted a directive on cyber confidence-building measures in October 2024. Internet access and cybersecurity are among the five priorities of the digital ministry's 2026 budget.

In October 2026 the digital minister cited more than 14,700 cybercrime complaints and losses of nearly FCFA 23 billion in 2025. ANSSI-CI's report for 2024 had counted 12,100 complaints and FCFA 6.96 billion of losses. The agency ran the first national cyber-crisis simulation in Abidjan on 28 and 29 October 2026, on a scenario hitting energy, telecommunications and payments at once. It said there that it had recorded 147 major vulnerabilities in public and private bodies since January 2026.

DPI

Data Exchange

The Unified eXchange Platform went live in Abidjan on 25 September 2025. Estonia's Cybernetica built it, and the state IT company SNDI implemented it over a twenty-month project. Its pilot phase still covered twelve public bodies in July 2026. Five administrations had joined the platform as at 22 July 2026. The pilot institutions include the tax directorate, the national civil-status office, the social security fund and the Abidjan trade court. They do not include the education ministry or any passport or immigration authority, so neither function exchanges data through the platform.

The contract is worth EUR 387,350, about FCFA 254 million (October 2025). It covers the platform core, the development of five priority services, and a transfer of skills to SNDI's teams and to the participating administrations. The state traces the choice of platform to a national digital-governance roadmap drawn up in 2022 with the Estonian consultancy Digital Nation. Its stated criteria were speed of deployment, proven security and alignment with the paperless-administration target.

A December 2025 protocol sets up automated exchange between the tax directorate's management system and the single window for external trade. That system already interfaces with other administrations. The urban land platform SIGFU joins the tax directorate, the land registry, the cadastre and the urban planning administration. A labour market information system run by the national employment and training observatory launched on 23 June 2026. It is built to the SDMX exchange standard and presented as the first interoperable system of its kind in francophone Africa. A convention signed in April 2026 is to interconnect the social registry with the identification office's databases and its national population register. The health ministry's digital director said in November 2025 that a national technical reference framework and a health data centre connect 269 health establishments.

In July 2026 the responsible minister said the first services put onto the platform were chosen largely because agencies volunteered, and not against user priorities. He called for clearer allocation of responsibility between ministries' information-system heads where one public service needs several agencies.

Digital Identity and CRVS

The IIAG measure of Ivorians' perception of access to identity documents fell 25.1 points over 2014-2023 to 33.2 out of 100, 27th of 54 African states, the country's most deteriorated indicator. The biometric card is compulsory from age 16, and identity certificates may be issued to children between 5 and 15. In 2018 the card cost 5,000 FCFA, about US$8.40, against a poverty rate of 46%. Supporting documents and transport pushed the real cost above the fee, and civil society groups campaigned against it. Applicants must first obtain a certificate of nationality from a Court of First Instance. In 2025 a pilot of the MOSIP identity platform migrated 10,000 records from the national insurance system.

A 2018 decree created the register of natural persons, managed by ONECI, as the reference database for other government systems. A national identification number is assigned at birth through it. ONECI reported 9,323,415 numbers assigned in 2025, 1,291,891 of them through civil registration. Interconnection of personal data files falls under the 2013 data protection law.

ONECI opened its online authentication platform to banks, and Société Générale Côte d'Ivoire integrated it first, in November 2025. A valid identity document has been required to register a SIM card since 2011. ONECI put the card on a phone with the myONECI+ application in April 2025. Legally resident foreigners are eligible for a biometric residence card, and asylum-seekers were approved for enrolment from 2025.

The 2018 decree makes civil status records a source for the population register. Most civil status centres are now connected to the Cityweb software. The share of births registered within the legal deadline fell from 66.8% in 2021 to 56.6% in 2023 and 55.4% in 2024. On 31 July 2026 the interior ministry said it rose 2.2 points in 2025. On 5 October 2026 the government launched a feasibility study, due by January 2027, for a national authentication platform and identity wallet built on the register.

Digital Payments and Fintech

Registered merchants taking electronic payments rose from 1,131,590 in 2023 to 2,719,463 in 2024, which the central bank attributes to QR codes. ARTCI counted 32.5 million active mobile money accounts at 30 September 2026, up 13.6% in a year.

Côte d'Ivoire is the union's largest electronic-money market, with 34.7% of WAEMU transaction value in 2024, up from 33.4% in 2023 and ahead of Senegal on 30.2%. Its share of transaction volume was 21.4% in 2024. It leads cross-border flows, with about 33 million intra-union operations worth FCFA 1,924 billion in 2024, up 37.95%.

58% of adults held an account in 2024, up from 51% in 2021, with the whole gain through mobile money and bank accounts flat at 21%. An ARTCI survey in 2017 found 56.00% of men and 26.96% of women held a mobile money account, 41.94% overall. Formal banking access is the country's lowest score on any of the Ibrahim Index's 96 indicators, at 21.4 out of 100 in 2023.

A presidential decree of 8 May 2024 makes the Treasury's TrésorPay-TrésorMoney platform exclusive for non-tax revenue and mass public expenditure across public and parapublic administrations. Deployment had reached 55% of public services in 2023, against a target of at least 90% for 2024. A WAEMU treasury may issue electronic money only in a closed loop confined to beneficiaries of state allocations. Côte d'Ivoire's is one of only two treasuries in the union that issue it.

The BCEAO's instant payment system went live on 30 September 2025 with 45 connected institutions and several major banks staying out. From 2 November 2026 every interoperable electronic-money transfer must route through the platform. Wave opened the platform to all its Ivorian customers in the week of 6 November 2026, free up to FCFA 8,000 a day. Payment institutions must run a complaints mechanism, and those offering only payment-initiation or account-information services must insure against unauthorised access to account data. Agents levy a charge on deposits and withdrawals that no operator tariff provides for. The central bank said in 2026 that the Ivorian Treasury already makes regular bulk payments through the platform.

Registries

The Independent Electoral Commission's 2025 statement describes a biometric voter list, consultable online and by SMS. Registration covers roughly half the voting-age population. Online company creation averaged 2.4 days at mid-July 2026, against a 24-hour target for the end of the year. The council of ministers validated a national numeric address code on 26 February 2025, identifying 14,279 Abidjan streets. The FCFA 12bn project covers 500,000 addresses and is to reach 15 cities by 2030. Laying of street signs was suspended in November 2025 to correct defects, and by March 2026, 25,750 of 87,914 had been laid in Abidjan. There is no single nationwide digital address register.

Budgetary and financial management scores 83.3 out of 100 and fourth of 54 African states in 2023. Inclusion

Access to services

On 3 August 2026 the identity enrolment centre at Grand-Bassam was running on a single agent handling intake, registration and card collection at once. It turned dozens of applicants away by mid-morning, as it had for weeks. Ivorians' sense of how easily an identity document can be obtained has fallen more than any other measure in the country's governance profile.

Côte d'Ivoire's inclusion and equality score was flat over the decade at 54.4 out of 100 (2023). Equal socioeconomic opportunity scored 73.2, and equal access to public services 51.9, 17th of 54 and down 1.5 points. The national public service portal is informational, not transactional, on the World Bank's 2025 assessment. Since March 2026 the government assistant EMY 101 has answered civil-status and tax questions over WhatsApp, Messenger and a toll-free line. The Miliê citizen portal has taken users' concerns about the administration since 7 February 2019. The civil-service ministry's user relations centre had handled more than 10,000 requests by toll-free line and its Logirec platform by January 2024.

Only 13.46% of households had internet at home in 2017. In 2017 those without it named the price of the service (22.78%) and the cost of equipment (21.69%) far ahead of the service being unavailable in the area, at 6.67%. Côte d'Ivoire levies the highest combined handset tax in Africa, at 50% (2025). A mobile broadband basket cost 2.4% of gross national income per capita in 2022.

More than 400,000 households received state cash transfers and accompanying measures between 2017 and 2024. The productive safety-net programme paid its 230,000 active beneficiary households by mobile money in 2024. FCFA 35.19bn had been transferred by the end of 2024. In 2024, 33,881 asylum-seekers received unconditional monthly transfers, 142% more than the year before. The population register is open by decree to residents, visitors and refugees. Asylum-seekers still lacked the unique identifier that access to some financial services depends on when their enrolment was approved for roll-out in 2025. An interministerial order of 2 July 2025 granted refugee status to more than 69,000 Burkinabè and about 1,500 Malians registered since May 2021.

Digital divides

Women's access to public services in Côte d'Ivoire fell 4.6 points over the decade to 2023, to 46.0 out of 100 and 24th of 54 African states. It is one of the few declines inside a women's equality measure that otherwise rose 6.7 points over the same period.

Mobile phone ownership stood at 81.38% of men against 47.19% of women in 2017, a 34-point spread on 64.83% ownership nationally. Actual use of a phone split 85.27% of men against 58.67% of women (2017). Mobile-money use reached 37.07% of men against 16.08% of women, on 26.91% overall (2017). The regulator's 2023 household survey found 61.0% of men aged 15 and over using the internet against 44.8% of women. Ivorian fieldwork puts the cause in social norms, weaker awareness of what the internet is for, the price of a smartphone and lower digital literacy rather than any explicit bar on women's access (2018).

Household computer ownership was 10.34% in urban areas against 0.28% in rural ones in 2017, a ratio of about thirty-seven to one. Phone ownership was 74.67% against 54.36% (2017). In 2023, 85.70% of urban households had internet access against 57.66% of rural ones. Mobile networks already covered nearly all Ivorians in 2018, if much less of the national territory. Electricity access stood at 94% in urban areas against 42% in rural ones (2020). The rural connectivity programme's coordinator said in November 2026 that about 578 radio sites built over 2024-2025 covered some 684 localities and about 900,000 people.

Education is among Côte d'Ivoire's weakest governance sub-categories, held down by enrolment and by equality in education (2023). Birth registration was markedly worse in the countryside than in the towns (2016).

Data

National statistics

In 2023 the statistical system scored 44.2 out of 100 and ranked 32nd of 54 African states. It rose only 4.6 points across the decade. The public administration sub-category around it scored 62.4 and ranked 13th.

The enterprise and establishment census counted well over a million formal and informal economic units (2025). That is far more than the formally registered business base. Population, agricultural and business censuses have all been run within the preceding ten years. The country scores above 89% on data use and 70-89% on data products (2023). It scores 50-69% on data services and infrastructure and only 20-49% on data sources (2023). Data sources, the pillar covering censuses, surveys and administrative collection, is the worst of the five. The survey programme is concentrated on households and labour, against two health surveys and one each for agriculture and business, leaving the productive side of the economy thinly measured (2023).

The main disease-surveillance tools depend on paper at facility level (2024). By October 2026 the paper civil-status registers had been digitised in 14 centres, about 3.8 million records. On-time birth registration fell to 55.4% in 2024, and the interior ministry said on 31 July 2026 that it rose 2.2 points in 2025, with 24.17% of deaths registered.

Vital statistics are published: the annual civil-status yearbook comes from the Interior Ministry on UNICEF funding (2025). Three decrees on the National Statistical System were adopted in the Council of Ministers on 18 June 2025. They cover the statistical visa, the tariffing of statistical services, and the communication of administrative files. A fourth decree, adopted on 5 August 2026, makes prior validation by the statistics agency and the national statistics council compulsory for every public statistic. A labour market information system built to the SDMX exchange standard launched on 23 June 2026. The telecoms regulator publishes market statistics under article 82 of ordonnance 2012-293. Its Panorama, deposited with the ITU, carries 2018 market data and a household survey with 2017 fieldwork.

Open data

Côte d'Ivoire's score for disclosure of public records rose 22.0 points over 2014-2023, to 50.2 out of 100 and 8th of 54 African states. Accessibility of public records rose 13.8 points, to 40.1 and 17th. The accountability and transparency sub-category they sit in stayed flat at 40.0 and 24th, the country's worst (2023).

An open government data portal is running and publishing basic information and datasets (2025). A national data governance strategy to 2030 was handed to the Prime Minister on 13 March 2025, alongside the national AI strategy. The state has an established institution with a whole-of-government digital mandate. The mining cadastre is online, built on Landfolio to run the full cycle of mining titles (September 2025). It is presented as aligned with EITI standards. A project to develop the Geoportail du Grand Abidjan, an urban-planning data platform to be built over 2025-2027, was launched in June 2026. The national portal listed 211 datasets in October 2026, economy 73 and society 39 leading.

On the Open Data Inventory the strongest categories are balance of payments, government finance and health facilities at 7.5 out of 10 (2024). Prices and gender statistics follow at 7.0. The weakest scored categories are energy and education outcomes at 4.5, and pollution and resource use at 5.0 (2024). Two categories — digital and communications, and food security — carry no score at all.

Across the 21 categories that carry a score the average is 6.0 out of 10 (2024), with economic data at 6.5 and social data at 6.1 against 5.2 for environmental data, the thinnest of the three domains.

The Islamic Development Bank is financing digital transformation and health security through 2029, developing health information systems and digital health platforms under the National Health Development Plan.

India activities

In April 2025 the Grand-Bassam technology zone VITIB was seeking finance from Indian investors and the Export-Import Bank of India for its development plan to 2028. An early loan from that bank built the zone. No Indian-built system, bilateral digital agreement or training programme had been found as at August 2026.

Digital sovereignty

The state-owned data centre was due to open in 2027 as at September 2026, offering government bodies cloud hosting, colocation and connectivity. The digital minister said in July 2025 that 97% of the country's data was stored abroad. When the centre was launched with Cybastion in December 2023 it was to host the systems, applications and data of every public administration body. The data jurisdiction, portability and exit terms of the Cybastion contract and of Raxio's memorandum to host government workloads had not been published as at September 2026.

The national data governance strategy 2024-2030 requires data classed as critical information infrastructure to be processed and stored in the country. Staff handling it must be at least 20% Ivorian (March 2025). It keeps non-critical government data in the national data centre or cloud, and allows non-sensitive government data in a public cloud. It set 2025 for a cloud policy, 2026 for the national data centre and 2027 for a national cloud. In 2025 government systems were hosted partly inside and partly outside government infrastructure.

In 2025 the state IT company SNDI presented the Cybernetica contract's complete transfer of skills to its teams as a guarantee of digital sovereignty. Where the national identity database is hosted, and under whose jurisdiction, had not been published as at September 2026.

The digital minister set a target in June 2026 of national compute capacity for artificial intelligence within twelve months. The BCEAO had put out to tender a centralised public key infrastructure covering all eight WAEMU states by July 2026.
