Status report

This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

The whole-of-government 2026-2030 Development Plan carries the digital-transformation vision, with its objectives, priorities and timetable. Parliament approved the plan by loi n° 2026-16 of 20 July 2026. Its Volume II mentions digital or digitalisation 414 times, artificial intelligence 119 times, data 72 times and cybersecurity 9 times (June 2026). It frames the digital chapter as integration into the global digital economy while preserving digital sovereignty.

In February 2026 the Head of Government instructed the administration to expand electronic payments, interlink citizen services and embed cybersecurity, artificial intelligence and open data in the digitalisation programme. A ministerial council placed digital-transformation projects under national follow-up. The World Bank recorded a GovTech and digital transformation strategy as current in 2025. The whole-of-government approach behind it was still planned or in draft, though a lead ministry and a forum of senior digital officials were in place. The body responsible for data governance published no report on its results or spending (2025), and the same assessment found no data governance strategy. A first national strategy for the governance, opening and use of public data reached final draft at a workshop in March 2026. No adoption has been found.

The health and communication technologies ministers signed a National Health Pact on 1 October 2026, with sovereign governance of health data and modernised digital platforms among its six axes. Its text and budget have not been found. The communications minister said on 9 October 2026 that the postal operator's 2026-2030 strategy rests on digital transformation. Artificial intelligence and digital identity are to be built into the postal system, and a resource-planning system is being introduced in phases.

Overall governance scored 61.2 of 100 in 2023, ninth of 54 African states, after falling 4.7 points over 2014-2023. Public administration fell 12.5 points to 60.2 over the same period, sixteenth of 54 in 2023. Across the index Tunisia improved on 41 of 96 indicators and declined on 52 (2023).

Legislation and regulation

In February 2026 twelve MPs put bill 2026/012 before a joint session of the finance and digitisation committees. The single-article amendment would reopen the electronic invoicing obligation that Article 53 of the 2026 Finance Law extended to all service providers from 1 January 2026. The amendment is in committee and the obligation is in force. The physicians' order said on 3 October 2026 that it keeps its reservation on compulsory e-invoicing for medicine, citing medical secrecy and patients' data.

A finance-ministry decree of 12 February 2026 allowed remote declarations to the National Business Register. Documents filed electronically carry the same legal force as paper before the courts and the administration. On 25 September 2026 the central bank issued circular 2026-10, repealing its 2018 circular on payment establishments. It sets three tiers of dinar payment accounts, allows remote account opening with equivalent identity assurance and caps a cash transfer at 3,000 dinars.

The Electronic Exchanges and Electronic Commerce Act of 2000 still gives legal effect to electronic signatures, electronic identification and online commerce. A decree-law of 12 May 2020 created the unique citizen identifier register, and an organic law of 11 March 2024 governs the biometric identity card. A decree-law of 11 March 2023 covers cybersecurity. Decree-Law 54 of 2022 pairs computer-crime provisions with offences over content sent across communications networks. A 2005 law governs electronic fund transfers, and the 2018 Startup Act governs startups. A 2016 banking law brings electronic money within means of payment and licenses payment institutions. A 2020 decree-law names the national computing centre sole operator of the interoperability platform. It bars public bodies from asking users again for data the platform can reach. The Code of Criminal Procedure requires a physical register for custody entries.

In April 2026 the communication-technologies minister pledged further law and regulation on artificial intelligence and data protection. The social affairs minister said on 8 October 2026 that the remote-work bill was in its final administrative stages. He said a legal text on the platform economy had been prepared.

Data protection

A replacement for the 2004 data protection statute was deposited with the Assembly of People's Representatives on 14 July 2025. It is an organic law of 132 articles in six titles, from processing principles and rights to the authority and penalties. In February 2026 it was under review by the Rights and Freedoms Committee. The committee heard the sponsoring deputies on 17 February 2026, and the government's legal counsel and the data-protection authority on 25 February. The bill would introduce a data protection officer and oblige public institutions to obtain declarations or authorisations for any processing. It would move enforcement towards financial penalties set by a sanctions department inside the authority.

Organic Law 2004-63 of 27 July 2004 governs until then. It gives rights of access, rectification and objection, requires declaration or authorisation before processing, and sets criminal sanctions. It bars transfer abroad where it threatens public security or vital interests, and requires adequate protection in the destination country. The authority must authorise every transfer, within one month. No authorisation decision, refusal or adequacy finding has been found. The Instance Nationale de Protection des Données à Caractère Personnel registers processing declarations, handles complaints and issues appealable decisions. In 2025 the World Bank found the authority's results and complaints record held internally and unpublished. A digital-rights forum said on 3 October 2026 that the authority's activity has stopped, and called for it to be reactivated.

On 27 July 2026 the health ministry said data in the linked national health dataset is stored securely, naming no legal basis and no role for the authority. The national register of community companies, launched in July 2026, asserted secure storage with no legal basis or regulator named. Parliament's memo on the e-invoicing amendment says mass electronic invoicing raises serious data-protection issues.

A regulator's decision of 10 June 2026, announced on 5 October, obliges internet service providers to offer minimum parental controls. In November 2026 the family ministry began drafting a bill to protect children online.

Regional collaboration

On 24 July 2026 Tunisia was elected to a 2027-2030 seat on the African Telecommunications Union's administrative council, at the union's plenipotentiary conference in Abuja. The council sets continental direction on digital transformation, artificial intelligence, cybersecurity and capacity building. In September 2026 the health ministry presented a Tunis Declaration on digital health at an international telehealth conference. It commits to health-data protection under national law, cybersecurity and interoperable systems on open standards.

The central bank joined the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System in February 2024, and Tunisian commercial banks were still being urged to onboard a year later. In June 2026 the scheme listed Tunisia as coming live with no commercial bank participating. The route is therefore not open to Tunisian customers.

In April 2013 the two incumbents linked their networks at the Algerian border, with direct internet peering between them. In June 2026 a joint technical commission planned a new Algeria-Tunisia-Italy submarine cable, an upgrade of the terrestrial link and lower roaming costs. No instrument or timetable for the cable has been published.

In April 2023 Tunisia had signed the African Union's Malabo Convention but not ratified it. No later ratification has been found.

Standards

TUNPAY, a national mobile payments label that banks, payment institutions and the postal service must integrate, launched in May 2026. The central bank published the Elyssa settlement system's adhesion conditions and operating rules. The 2023 cybersecurity decree-law requires periodic technical audits reported to the National Cybersecurity Agency.

A 2009 law founds the national standardisation system and the institute that manages standard-setting, certification and conformity marking. In 2017 the institute registered ISO/IEC 27001:2013 on information-security management as an identical national standard. It is Tunisia's ISO member body, taking part in 21 technical committees and observing 153 (October 2026). In May 2026 it opened a public enquiry on draft standards for measurement management and cables, none of them specific to digital systems.

A joint order of May 2021 makes development norms for online public services binding. The government's November 2021 reference guide sets their service typology, maturity levels, user-centred design and security criteria. In 2025 the World Bank recorded the interoperability framework as in place and widely used across government. Its use, compliance and benefits were neither monitored nor published. A unified budget classification covers central and sub-national government, and e-procurement data follows the Open Contracting Data Standard (2025). A data quality framework, uptime monitoring of government systems and guidance on replacing legacy systems were also in place.

In 2025 a bank hosting tender applied the rule that public bodies place confidential or sensitive data with providers certified under the National Cybersecurity Agency's N-Cloud and G-Cloud schemes, on Tunisian soil. In 2025 the World Bank found no policy favouring bids from startups and smaller firms, and no open-source software policy for the public sector. The interoperability layer is built on the X-Road lineage, extended under a Phase-2 contract in May 2026.

Public debate and participation in policymaking

In 2025 the World Bank recorded a national citizen participation platform that also accepts petitions. Citizens and businesses cannot use it to take part in policy decisions, and government responses are not made public. Government platforms take complaints and information requests under published service standards, but there is no record of a service changed in response.

Deliberative and participatory governance fell 16.8 points over 2014-2023 to 69.8 of 100. The participation sub-category fell 34.7 points to 46.1, 27th of 54 and the country's steepest fall in the index. Civil society space fell 30.7 points to 64.2, and media freedom 17.0 points to 54.3 (2023). The country ranked 137th of 180 on the 2026 world press freedom index, down from 129th the year before.

Decree-Law 54 of 2022 attaches offences to content transmitted over communications networks. A bill to revise it was deposited in February 2024 and referred to committee in April 2025, with 38 sponsors. The bill is still unvoted and its operative text unpublished. In July 2026 the Sfax court of appeal gave a journalist a first firm one-year prison term, requalifying the case under article 86 of the telecommunications code. On 30 September 2026 decree-law 2026-1 replaced the decree-law's article 24. A first false-news offence now carries a fine of 10,000 to 20,000 dinars in place of a prison term of up to five years. A repeat offence still carries six months' prison and a 50,000 dinar fine.

Network measurements showed unannounced mobile disruptions to messaging apps nationwide during both 2026 baccalaureate sessions, 3-10 June and 29 June-2 July.

The 2016 organic law creates the access-to-information authority and sets a duty to publish, request and appeal procedures and sanctions. In December 2025 the government confirmed the authority has had no president or vice-president since its council's mandate lapsed. It cannot meet lawfully, and has called for urgent legislative revision. In 2025 the count of requests received and granted was held internally and not published. Disclosure of public records scored 32.3 of 100 in 2023, against 59.6 for accessibility.

Finance

Financial sustainability

In 2024 the telecommunications and ICT development fund collected TND 232.4 million and spent TND 98.9 million. The fund is a special treasury account whose receipts are earmarked levies and fees. The 2026 finance law votes its spending at TND 100 million, as in 2025.

The state's own money carried 71.4 per cent of the digital lines in the 2024 mission budget notes as appropriated, TND 106.8 million of TND 149.5 million (FY2024).

The 2024 Communication Technologies note carried TND 25.7 million of on-lent external loans for e-governance and TND 17.0 million for the Digital Tunisia 2020 project. Running costs of information systems were general-budget lines in the 2024 notes of Communication Technologies, the Interior, Justice and Finance. The African Development Bank loan behind Digital Tunisia 2020 pays for the national computing centre's private cloud, on a 24-month build from February 2026.

The 2026 finance law votes the digital development programme TND 161.3 million, against TND 150.1 million in 2025. Outside the fund, the 2026 finance law prints no financing source against its digital lines. The ministry's 2025 performance plan ties the programme's investment to on-lent World Bank and African Development Bank loans. It plans investment credits rising to TND 80.6 million in 2027.

The 2027 budget orientations, set in August 2026, put reliance on the country's own resources first and name the digital transition among the budget's axes. On 6 November 2026 the communication technologies minister presented a 2027 draft budget of TND 1.07 billion for his ministry, 9.3 per cent more than in 2026. The national AI strategy for 2026-2030 was presented in August 2026 with no budget, timetable or result indicators made public.

MoUs and other agreements

In February 2026 SoleCrypt and Schneider Electric signed a non-binding memorandum of understanding on AI data centres. It covers low-water, liquid-cooled data centres built to reference architectures co-developed with Nvidia. On 11 May 2023 Orange Tunisia agreed with Medusa to host the Medusa submarine cable at Bizerte and develop its landing. On 27 June 2023 China's industry ministry and Tunisia's communication technologies ministry signed a memorandum on ICT cooperation. On 16 July 2023 the European Union and Tunisia signed a non-binding memorandum on a strategic and global partnership. Its digital section names the Medusa cable and undertakes to assess Tunisian participation in the Digital Europe Programme.

New investments

On 24 July 2024 the EBRD approved a senior loan of up to €190m to Tunisie Telecom under Project Hannibal, in four tranches with a first committed tranche of €50m. It is the Bank's first public-sector operation in Tunisia without a sovereign guarantee, and was announced on 16 January 2026. It carries a first-loss EU EFSD+ guarantee and an €11m EU grant (July 2024). Its first listed use is the operator's connection to the Medusa submarine cable, beside fibre roll-out and 4G+ and 5G upgrades.

Eighty-six external commitments to the digital estate are on record, with start years from 2015 to 2026 and together about USD 702 million (November 2026). Seventy-eight are grants worth about USD 145 million, eight of them from Germany's development ministry at about USD 115 million (November 2026). The EBRD accounts for about 29 per cent of the total, the European Investment Bank's three 4G loans for about 23 per cent and the World Bank loan for about 14 per cent, with Korea's land-information loan at about 9 per cent and France's e-health loan at about 4 per cent (November 2026). Connectivity takes just over half and government digital services about a quarter. The EU grant covers exactly 30 per cent of the ViaTunisia budget, €9.6m of €32m (2024).

The EU is co-funding the ViaTunisia landing infrastructure with €9.6m from the Connecting Europe Facility against a €32m budget, from March 2023 to August 2025. In 2019 the World Bank committed USD 100m to digitise social protection and education services, active to 2027. In May 2025 a consortium led by Samsung C&T signed a USD 50m contract for the land information system. It is financed under a USD 60m loan agreement of July 2021 with the Export-Import Bank of Korea.

In May 2026 the EBRD was funding the interoperability platform's second phase with Switzerland's SECO, for completion in 2027. World Bank financing agreed in 2026 is directed at a beneficiary registry and social-protection payments. No value has been found for either.

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

The Medusa cable came ashore at Bizerte on 31 October 2025, its first landing point in Africa. Its France–Tunisia route entered service on 3 June 2026. Orange Tunisia owns the Bizerte landing station and the Tunisian section of the cable, and brought it into commercial service. The 1,050 km Bizerte-Marseille route was built as the ViaTunisia project on an open-access model. In February 2025 Tunisie Telecom agreed to take a dedicated Bizerte–Marseille fibre pair carrying 20 Tbps. The incumbent is 65 per cent state-owned and 35 per cent held by Emirates International Telecommunications (July 2024). It is running an EBRD-financed programme of household fibre, backbone upgrades and migration to 4G and 5G.

All three operators launched commercial 5G in February 2025, under 15-year licences of November 2024 giving each 5 MHz at 700 MHz and 100 MHz at 3.5 GHz. Orange opened with 400 sites live, and Ooredoo offered fixed-wireless access at up to 100 Mbps. 4G covered 96 per cent of the population (2024). A data-only mobile broadband basket cost about 1.5 per cent of gross national income per head (2024), inside the ITU's affordable band. Mobile broadband is the main means of access, and voice is flat (2025).

In 2024 more than half of households held a connection in Tunis, Ariana, Ben Arous, Monastir and Sousse, against under a fifth in Sidi Bouzid, Kasserine and Jendouba. The mobile penetration figure counts SIM cards, not people. The Internet & Computers score rose 38.1 points over 2014-2023 to 76.9 of 100, 4th of 54 African states. Public perception of infrastructure fell 13.2 points to 34.3 over the same decade, 23rd of 54. One exchange point, TunIXP, had four peering participants in 2026. It is run by the Tunisian internet agency, which has also managed the country-code domain and national address allocation since 1996.

Data Storage

Data centres are a named project line in Volume II of the 2026-2030 Development Plan, approved by law on 20 July 2026. In October 2025 the Tunisia Investment Authority named data centres one of two core investment priorities. It recommended a cloud and data-centre special economic zone, built through public-private partnerships and powered by renewables. In February 2026 SoleCrypt announced a solar-powered data centre at Bizerte, landing point of three subsea cables. In October 2026 it enlarged the plan to a 40 MW AI campus with a 7 MW first phase targeted for early 2028. None of it is built.

The National Centre for Informatics runs the government hosting service and national data-centre capacity (2026). In 2025 the government cloud was a state-run private cloud offering infrastructure as a service, with use, security and savings neither monitored nor published. The centre's national backup facility, begun in 2006 with World Bank support, is the recovery site for critical state applications. An African Development Bank-financed private cloud contract was awarded at EUR 8.17 million and TND 1.31 million, on a 24-month build from February 2026. In June 2026 the lender judged the cloud would not be finished even with the loan extended to the end of 2027. On 29 September 2026 the health minister named a Tunisian sovereign cloud as host for every citizen's health identifier and medical record by the end of 2026. No operator was named.

Orange Tunisia has a facility outside Sousse, and ATLAX and Dataxion have sites in Tunis (2026). No hyperscale cloud provider ran a region in the country in 2025. In October 2026 an operator and an IT services firm announced cloud resources hosted and operated in Tunisia, with no value, capacity or start date.

Circular 16 of 22 July 2024 requires public bodies to host confidential or sensitive data on Tunisian soil. Providers must be certified under the National Cybersecurity Agency's N-Cloud and G-Cloud schemes. The Societe Tunisienne de Banque's 2025 hosting tender requires data held in Tunisia with an N-Cloud certified provider.

Energy

Renewables supplied about 4 per cent of electricity generation in 2025, down from 4.4 per cent in 2024. Natural gas supplied 95 to 96 per cent, though installed renewable capacity had reached about 1.21 GW (2025).

In 2023 electricity reached 100 per cent of the population, with no material urban-rural gap. The Ibrahim index scored electricity access at 99.9 of 100, 3rd of 54 African states (2023). Firms reported 0.4 outages a month in 2024, about five a year. National renewables policy provides for photovoltaic systems at isolated sites.

In October 2025 the Tunisia Investment Authority recommended expanding renewable generation to power data centres. The Bizerte data centre is to draw on a 60 MW solar site at Tozeur, whose base camp opened in November 2025. The incumbent operator's modernisation programme includes energy-efficiency and solar investment.

Technical Capacity

In 2025 the government's digital-skills effort for the public service reached only basic skills. Tunisia had an identifiable community of public open-source contributors in 2022. It has one of the world's highest shares of tertiary graduates in STEM subjects, fed by established engineering and ICT institutions. Government effectiveness sat mid-table among African states in 2023.

The national computing centre was founded by statute in 1975, with its missions set by decree in 2007. It runs national systems and e-administration and trains the administration in information technology. The 2020 interoperability decree-law makes it sole operator of the national exchange platform. No staffing plan, skills audit or operations report for it has been found. In August 2024 a donor project equipped the finance ministry's data centre and trained more than 400 of its executives.

Every one of the seven core government systems assessed in 2025 — financial management, debt management, customs, tax, HR, payroll and social insurance — runs on custom-built software rather than commercial or open-source packages, a pattern that implies in-house build capacity and commits the state to maintaining all of it itself.

Cybersecurity

Circular n. 5 of 2 September 2026 gave the administration a written security baseline. It requires encrypted connections across official sites and platforms and mandatory renewal of security certificates. It requires two-factor verification for administrator access, protection against traffic-saturation attacks and hosting on servers approved by the state. It bans the use of messaging apps and social media for exchanging official documents. It requires regular audits, immediate reporting of incidents to the national cybersecurity agency and an end to shared credentials. It carries no compliance deadline, audit schedule or list of approved hosting providers. No operational governance for compliance, security and audit trails is recorded for Tunisia's tax management information system, its financial management information system or its treasury single account (2025). The ITU scores Tunisia at 81.93 on its Global Cybersecurity Index, in the top Role-Modelling tier (2024). The index measures institutional commitment.

Decree-Law 2023-17 of 11 March 2023 requires organisations within its scope to undergo periodic technical audits reported to the National Cybersecurity Agency. They must also notify it of cyberattacks, with financial penalties for failure. The National Security Council's National Cybersecurity Strategy 2026-2030, published in 2026, revises the 2020-2025 strategy. The cybercrime framework, Decree-Law 54 of 2022, mixes computer-crime provisions with offences covering content carried over communications networks. In payments, operators of settlement systems must run an annual information-systems security audit reported to the Central Bank. Licensed payment institutions have carried the same duty since 2018.

Governance for customs, HR, payroll and e-procurement systems is established but kept internal and unpublished (2025). The cybersecurity audit of the Sahetna.tn health portal was completed and reviewed at a steering committee on 10 June 2026. It has not been released.

The agency's CERT declared 657,540 incidents and detected 35,000 traffic-saturation attacks in the first half of 2026. The national agency warned on 30 July 2026 of an SMS phishing campaign sent from foreign numbers using fake unpaid-traffic-fine notices. Its answer was advice to distrust unsolicited links and to check that a site sits on the .tn domain that government websites use.

DPI

Data Exchange

In 2025 two data-exchange services between agencies entered production: death notifications from the civil registry to the social affairs ministry, and a tax-status lookup from the finance ministry. Both run on the interoperability platform operated by the National Centre for Informatics.

The government reported the first phase of the Unified eXchange Platform delivered by the end of 2025. The platform derives from X-Road and is supplied by Estonia's Cybernetica with EBRD and Swiss SECO funding. Its first phase onboarded five institutions in the interior, education, transport and social affairs ministries. The government has published a phased roadmap with large-scale deployment as the next stage. A second phase contracted in May 2026 and due in 2027 adds six business-environment bodies, among them the enterprise register, the investment authority, the finance ministry and the central bank. It names a higher UN e-government ranking as an objective.

In September 2026 the continental public-health agency sought consultants to draft a national framework for health information exchange. Health data exchange runs through the CNAM health insurance fund's own systems, and municipalities and other sub-national bodies are not connected. Neither the electoral register nor the justice sector appears in either phase. The interior ministry said in October 2026 that its criminal-record strategy rests on interconnection with the justice and finance ministries, with no date stated. The business register exchanges data through bespoke web-service contracts made under the 2020 interoperability decree.

In 2025 core government systems exchanged data through a Government Service Bus and point-to-point interfaces. The national health identifier is bound to the identity card, the social security number and school and university identifiers.

Digital Identity and CRVS

In December 2025 the interior ministry's account of the biometric card programme carried no implementation date, issuance start, supplier or enrolment figure. Organic Law 2024-22 of 11 March 2024 creates a card whose chip holds the fingerprint and photograph in encrypted form. It makes the card compulsory from age fifteen, and lets only designated police, national guard and customs agents read the chip. The existing card carries only an inked thumbprint image and no electronic biometric template.

The E-Houwiya mobile identity has run nationwide since August 2022 and is legally equivalent to face-to-face identification. It is free and optional, and carries a personal X.509 certificate and a qualified digital signature. About 200,000 people held one in March 2025, and the interior ministry put users above 450,000 in May 2026. It gives single sign-on to the e-Bawaba portal and the health insurance, social security and school platforms. The investment authority has required it for investment declarations since 1 July 2026, and the business register requires it for its digital services. An agriculture ministry circular requires every body under it to integrate the credential by 15 November 2026.

Decree-Law 2020-17 of 12 May 2020 created the unique citizen identifier register for every Tunisian in the civil-status birth register. It held about 15 million records under an eleven-digit lifetime identifier (2020). It is restricted by law to Tunisian nationals, with no provision for foreign residents or refugees. It deduplicates on demographic data alone and holds no biometric template. The identifier is assigned at birth registration, and the card requires a birth certificate. Rural civil status offices still write events by hand into duplicate paper registers, which alone carry legal force. Public perception of the ease of obtaining an identity document fell 41.9 points over 2014-2023 to 55.1 of 100. The Sahloul university hospital reported 180,000 health identifiers unified under the national health identifier.

Digital Payments and Fintech

In May 2026 the Central Bank of Tunisia and the national electronic payments operator Société Monétique Tunisie launched TUNPAY, a national mobile payments label. Banks, payment institutions and the postal service must integrate it into their wallets, interfaces and acceptance points.

A domestic instant mobile money system has run under Central Bank governance since 2018 and is deployed nationwide. Only 12,843 of the 113,739 accounts at the payment institutions were active at end-2025, against 23,830 a year earlier. The central bank counted 719,000 wallets in September 2026. Access to banking services scored 22.5 of 100 in 2023, 19th of 54 African states. In 2021, 75 per cent of adults used formal financial services, up from 61 per cent in 2018. Ooredoo Fintech obtained a Central Bank licence in March 2026 for the walletii wallet. Online payments rose 27% to 18 million worth TND 1.229bn in January to September 2026, and in-store card payments 18.6% to 41.3 million, on central bank figures. There were 45,900 POS terminals and 1,552 active merchant websites at 30 September 2026.

In 2022 the IFC and the Central Bank launched Paysmart for electronic payment of utility and other public bills. Remote payment of the vehicle tax and an electronic fiscal stamp were both in service by the end of 2025. In May 2026, 209,600 households received their Amen Social transfers and child allowances through digital payment tools, against a target of 80 per cent by 2030.

Under Law 2016-35 the Central Bank owns, operates and supervises the Elyssa real-time gross settlement system and the SGMT settlement system. Law 2005-51 of 2005 sets payment-specific consumer protection duties. Circular 2024-005 requires system operators to run a board-level audit and risk committee and an annual security audit. There are no payment-specific data protection provisions; transaction data is governed only by the general 2004 personal data law. The National Financial Inclusion Strategy ran to 2022.

About 1,568,000 pupils, some 75%, registered online for the 2026-2027 school year, as did about 287,300 students by end-September 2026. They paid through the postal operator's free wallets and payment application.

Registries

Six ministerial decisions gazetted in August 2026 open compulsory cadastral survey of unregistered unbuilt land from 1 November 2026, in zones of six governorates. The land property office reports its processing system networked across all 22 regional directorates, with average processing cut from 40 days to 8. The postal service runs only a four-digit locality postcode, in place since 1980, and the cadastre records parcels, not street addresses. A national address register was announced as under construction in November 2022, and none has been found in service since. In May 2025 a consortium led by Samsung C&T signed a $50 million, three-year contract to digitise the cadastre's archives and build a land information system.

The national business register went fully digital on 1 July 2026, ending paper filing. It is deployed nationwide, with online registration and a public search portal, and held about 800,000 companies in 2024. In December 2024 it agreed with the electoral commission to share data directly between the two registers. On 21 September 2026 it became the first African member of the European Business Registry Association. An arrêté of 13 January 2026 opened its beneficial-owner register to anti-money-laundering authorities and to the public on a legitimate interest.

Birth registration has stood at or above 99 per cent since 2001, in rural and urban areas and for both sexes. Registration is open to foreigners whose civil events occur in the country. Madania has centralised civil registration from all 350 municipalities since April 2005, issuing documents online and exchanging records with the social security funds. Only births and deaths are systematically digitised, and marriages and divorces are never entered. In April 2026 a platform for declaring births from the maternity ward went into pilot use at a La Marsa hospital.

The electoral commission ISIE managed a digital roll of about 9.75 million voters in 2024, with an online portal for checks and updates. About 2.3 million electors were enrolled automatically for the July 2022 referendum. The Amen Social registry is digital and nationwide, covering about 333,000 poor and 620,000 low-income households (2023).

Sectoral management information systems

By 27 July 2026 medical files for about 100,000 people had been linked to the national health identifier. In September 2026 the health ministry put active registrations at 800,000, with teleradiology in 30 facilities. The identifier is the EVAX code created for Covid-19 vaccination, which held over 3 million childhood immunisation records (July 2026). It is assigned from the identity card at registration, with nationwide rollout targeted by the end of 2026. Phase 1 of the digital hospital project was delivered by the end of 2025. In September 2026 the health ministry announced a national platform to track medicine stocks, with no supplier or launch date.

The health system remained largely paper-based in April 2025. In February 2025 the health ministry called connecting primary care facilities to the internet a forthcoming phase. An EU and health-ministry programme is digitising the primary care information system, with pilot sites at Nabeul. Health institutions in Kebili began adopting the national health identifier in July 2026. The national Tarbia.tn platform brings school services into one portal for parents and teachers.

The tax administration processed more than 245 million operations electronically in 2024. It runs a nationwide digital register with online taxpayer identification and electronic declaration. Customs said in November 2025 that its SINDA 2 system would be ready in 2026. In 2025 customs and tax ran separate custom-built systems. An HR system with a self-service portal was linked to payroll, and a social insurance system covered non-health benefits. In November 2026 the Administrative Court's central chambers held their first electronic hearings on its Selim system. Budgetary and financial management fell 27.7 points over 2014-2023 to 41.7 of 100, 43rd of 54 African states.

The religious affairs ministry launched a digital platform to manage mosque staff, presented on 9 October 2026. It tracks personnel files, identifies vacant posts and links central and regional services.

Other GovTech and e-Gov

The Khadamet services application registered 50,000 users in the ten days after its launch at the end of August 2026. It reached a second mobile platform on 11 October, through the mobile identity. In November 2026 the minister put its services at more than 40, with 150 targeted for 2027. On 6 November the communication technologies minister promised remote requests for the identity card, passport and driving licence before the end of 2026.

Tunisia scored 0.6935 on the UN's 2024 e-government index, 87th in the world and third in Africa. In 2025 its main public service portal was informational, so most services could not be completed on it. The tax portal supports registration, filing and payment, and the social insurance portal registration and benefits. In August 2026 the tax administration launched remote completion of the patente procedure. About a quarter of internet users report using online public administration.

Article 53 of the 2026 Finance Law made electronic invoicing compulsory for all service providers from 1 January 2026. On 3 November 2026 the finance minister put invoices received through the platform at about 1.8 million, nearly 37,000 of them settled.

In 2025 the World Bank found a financial management system covering central and local budgets, and debt management covering foreign and domestic debt. TUNEPS, the e-procurement portal, was mandatory for public buyers and counted 1,300 public bodies and 12,000 suppliers in June 2021. A treasury single account settles through the real-time gross settlement system. There is no public investment management system and so no project database. In September 2025 a portal of e-justice services went to international tender on African Development Bank financing.

In February 2026, 192 digital-transformation projects were under national follow-up by a ministerial council chaired by Head of Government Sarra Zaafrani Zenzri. In 2025 no entity monitored digital spending across government, and the GovTech institution's progress report was unpublished. The industrial property institute launched a digital system for trade marks, patents and designs in June 2026.

Digitalisation

Digitalisation of sub-national government

The employment ministry launched a national register of community companies in July 2026, a registration and tracking platform for local development. On 24 June 2026 the municipality of Tunis issued the first fully online building permit, and the national Taamir platform is generalised to 16 municipalities. A four-year German-backed local governance project closed on 30 April 2026. It left digital service houses in 19 communes and a municipal revenue-management system among four solutions. Thirty-four digital service houses were completed across 21 governorates between December 2025 and June 2026, and 68 more are scheduled for 2027 to 2030. In October 2026 a programme simplifying about 2,250 administrative procedures, 80% of them to be digitised, was put at about 70% advanced.

In 2025 a unified budget classification covered central and sub-national government, and the financial management system covered local budgets. Every institution on the national interoperability platform was a central-government body in 2024.

A 2022 cooperation-agency study found property-tax rolls kept on the GRB software and no e-payment for revenue collected by communes. A 2024 webinar synthesis reports e-payment of municipal fees and version 2.0 of the software at Bizerte.

Rural digital data capture

In March 2026 the health ministry told parliament that a cold-chain management platform had run since 2025 across 500 health centres and local hospitals. In 2021 rural civil status offices entered births, deaths and marriages on paper first, transcribing them into the national system afterwards. In November and December 2025 three workshops trained 72 municipalities on civil-registration practice, including the Madania system.

Tunisia ranked first of 54 African states on the Ibrahim Index's rural economy measure in 2023, at 85.7 of 100. The score rose ten points over the decade, the country's fastest-improving sub-category. Rural market access scored 94.4, also first in Africa (2023).

In September 2025 an interior ministry official announced 21 model police and national guard centres, with digital fingerprint readers replacing ink, without saying where. In 2020 justice-sector electronic case exchange did not reach the police, National Guard, customs or prisons.

A national school platform launched in 2024 brings school services together, and primary computer labs were equipped in lagging regions. In November 2025 the education minister said most schools were connected to high-speed internet under the Edunet 10 programme. A telecommunications foundation has equipped 185 public primary schools with offline-capable digital kits since 2014.

In 2024 internet use was 81.4 per cent in urban areas against 61 per cent in rural areas.

Technology

AI

In August 2026 the communication technologies minister presented a national AI strategy for 2026-2030 to a ministerial council. It is framed around privacy, constitutional rights and national sovereignty. In May 2026 the minister described AI governance frameworks on data protection, privacy and sovereignty as in drafting. In November 2026 the ministry planned an AI governance framework and an AI ethics charter for 2027, with six AI-based government projects against two in 2026. In 2025 the World Bank found the national strategy on disruptive technologies not adopted. No law on artificial intelligence has been found.

In 2025 the World Bank found no chatbot or other AI used to improve citizen engagement. In November 2026 the national business register launched an AI chatbot for company founders and a bot that checks and reserves trade names. In May 2026 customs added a machine-learning module to its selectivity system for risk targeting. In October 2026 customs said first trials improved targeting by 20 to 25 per cent. The share of declarations sent to the red channel fell from 4.7% in 2025 to 2.6% in 2026. In June 2026 the electricity utility and Japan's cooperation agency presented a machine-learning demand forecasting system. In September 2026 the finance ministry was receiving bids for machine learning in tax verification, to run on local infrastructure. No accuracy figure against the methods replaced has been found.

A Chinese-funded supercomputer at the El Khawarizmi computing centre entered its trial period in July 2026. It is a TND 32m grant within a project begun in 2013. A commercial GPU cloud is offered from a Tier IV data centre, with limited stated capacity (2026). The largest AI facility announced is a 40 MW campus at Bizerte, with a first phase due in early 2028.

In the INT observatory's 2026 national survey, roughly half of internet users reported using AI applications.

Reported use of AI applications runs well ahead of reported use of online public administration in that same survey (2026).

ICT Industry

In May 2026 an Indian mobility-technology firm committed 1.52 million dollars to a software-engineering centre at Sfax serving global carmakers. An American automotive supplier's Tunisian hub has passed 100 million dollars of investment, with nearly 200 software engineers against a target of 500 (December 2025). Both figures come from the investment promotion agency. No employment or export series for the sector has been found.

In 2026 the technology sector accounted for around a tenth of GDP across more than two thousand firms. Gross value added of the information and communication branch reached 4,894.4 million dinars at current prices in 2025, against 3,469.1 million in 2020. The World Bank records no business or establishment survey in the decade to 2023.

Electronic transaction values rose in 2024, while the shipping and postal services that e-commerce depends on weakened over the decade to 2023. In 2025 a company could be started online through the public service portal. Tunisia has a visible open-source developer community contributing on GitHub.

Innovation ecosystem

In 2025 the government had an innovation strategy, a running programme and an established innovation lab. It also had policies to support GovTech startups and to finance innovation by startups and smaller firms. Neither the programmes' results nor the innovation institution's performance was published. The state-mandated ANAVA fund of funds had committed 45 million euros across ten venture funds by September 2026.

The Startup Act of 2018 gives startups a legal framework and a labelling procedure. In April 2026 the minister said a Startup Act 2.0 bill was being drafted, and none had been adopted by 23 September 2026. About 1,400 firms held the startup label in 2026, and the ministry aims for 2,000 in 2027. Tunisia ranked 81st on WIPO's Global Innovation Index for 2024.

Tunisian startups raised venture funding through 2024 and 2025, among them the payments company Konnect and the energy firm Wattnow. The El Ghazala technology park hosted about 90 firms and the Manouba park more than 200 in September 2025. Sousse's Novation City ran an Industry 4.0 incubator with about 50 startups in March 2025. In June 2026 the Sfax technology park announced a 15,000 square metre extension, to open in 2028, and two ELIFE centres opened at Sidi Bouzid and Tozeur, bringing that network to six. The Tunisia Digital Summit held its tenth edition in April 2026, with about 2,000 participants. The EU-funded hub The Dot reported more than 500 startups supported since 2021 (October 2026).

Capacity

Literacy

In 2026 the telecommunications regulator's observatory reported 85.9 per cent of respondents using the internet. It found 84.3 per cent owning a smartphone and 41 per cent a computer, and 47.9 per cent of internet users using AI tools (2026). No test of the population's digital skills has been found.

In July 2026 the national cybersecurity agency answered a phishing campaign with advice to distrust unsolicited SMS and to check that a site sits on the .tn domain. The agency states that Tunisian government websites use that domain exclusively.

Education completion scored 97.2 of 100 in 2023, first of 54 African states and 8.1 points higher than a decade before. Teacher supply scored 99.4, second in Africa. Education quality fell 8.7 points over the same decade to 56.2, tenth of 54. Public satisfaction with education fell 14.6 points to 33.5, 32nd of 54 (2023).

Training and skills

Government digital skills programmes are offered to citizens and schools free of charge (2025). Their results and progress are recorded internally and not published (2025). Nobody outside the ministries running them can say whether they work, or how many people they have reached.

The Plan de developpement 2026-2030 names artificial intelligence, data analysis, programming and cybersecurity as the skills base of a digital economy it makes a pillar of human-capital development. It attaches targets to none of them. Inside government there is a strategy for improving digital skills in the public service, pitched at basic skills (2025).

In 2022, 37.9 per cent of tertiary graduates were in science, technology, engineering and mathematics on UNESCO data. The pipeline is unusually strong for the region, resting on Tunisia's tertiary STEM and engineering base. The technology employment that absorbs it skews young. The national university network run by the El-Khawarizmi computing centre linked about 445 sites for nearly 400,000 users in 2023. The matching between graduates and jobs runs through the state. Tunisia operates a job portal for registration, search and applications online, with a separate portal for public sector positions, reachable through several channels (2025). Engineers are also leaving: more than 7,000 engineers left in 2025 and the Order of Engineers expects more than 8,000 in 2026. In ten years 46,000 have left, against 105,000 on the Order's register.

Research institutions

In December 2025 a state-linked strategic studies institute published the first Tunisian study on artificial intelligence and the social role of the state. It carries 143 recommendations across five sectors, built with about 125 participants. No record of government action on them has been found. In December 2025 the Virtual University of Tunis issued a charter for good use of AI at universities, requiring disclosure of AI use. Tunisian academic work in 2024 found paper still dominant in clinical record-keeping.

Tunisia has one of the world's highest shares of tertiary graduates in STEM subjects, with tertiary completion rising from 22.8 to 29.6 per cent (2025). Tunis El Manar, INSAT, ENIT and Sup'Com are long-established engineering and computing institutions.

Inclusion

Access to services

In 2025 the World Bank recorded Tunisia's national service portal as informational, so most services cannot be completed on it. Taxpayers were not involved in designing the tax portal's online services, which have no alternative channel. The administrative ombudsman, which takes petitions online, by mobile application and in person, received 95 electronically in 2020 out of 5,722 contacts.

Most Tunisians were using the internet in 2026. A mobile broadband basket was within the affordability threshold the ITU uses in 2024.

In 2023 public perception of how easy it is to obtain an identity document was among the most deteriorated Ibrahim Index indicators for Tunisia. The national identity card is required to enrol in the Amen Social programme and to collect any social protection payment. Birth registration is effectively universal across rural and urban areas and both sexes. Civil registration is open to foreigners whose civil status events occur in the country.

In 2024 part of Amen Social's cash transfers were paid through cards linked to electronic wallets, and the rest in cash. Access to banking was one of Tunisia's ten worst-scoring Ibrahim Index indicators in 2023.

In 2024, 26.6 per cent of Tunisian women aged 15 and over held an account, against 28.7 per cent in 2021. The family and women's ministry launched a national digital marketplace for women's and girls' products in August 2026, with postal payment and delivery.

The social affairs ministry launched a digital platform for the disability card in December 2025. It offers document upload, tracking through the citizen space, remote renewal and a QR-coded smart card. A 2019 circular instructs every public body to provide physical access, priority service, Braille, sign-language and audio aids, accessible websites and staff training.

Under a 2020 decree-law the unique citizen identifier is restricted to Tunisian nationals, with no provision for foreign residents or refugees. Tunisia has no national asylum system, and in June 2024 the authorities ordered the UN refugee agency to suspend asylum registration. The number registered with the agency fell from 18,362 in June 2024 to 7,515 in April 2026.

Digital divides

The regulator's 2026 survey put internet use at 99.2 per cent among 18 to 24 year-olds, against 54.1 per cent at 60 to 70. It was 69.1 per cent in households earning 600 dinars a month or less, against 95.9 per cent at 2,500 dinars and more (2026). Most users connect through the phone (2026). Mobile penetration counts SIM connections, not people.

The 2024 census found 40.4 per cent of households with a home connection, passing half only in Tunis, Ariana, Ben Arous, Monastir and Sousse and below a fifth in Sidi Bouzid, Kasserine and Jendouba. In February 2023 a digital inclusion index for UNDP and the ministry found 39.1 per cent of people not using the internet. It found no gap between men and women, a 54-point gap between the youngest and oldest, and Jendouba and Siliana lowest. The white-zones programme's first phase connected 94 uncovered sectors, 164 schools and 59 health centres, and a second tender for 103 zones in 15 governorates went out in June 2025.

A 2019 circular requires every public body to provide accessible websites, with Braille, sign-language and audio aids and priority service. In 2025 the state's citizen feedback platforms offered no support for users with disabilities. The disability card moved onto a digital platform in December 2025. No audit of public websites against the circular has been found. In 2025 digital skills programmes were open to citizens and schools free of charge.

Access to Public Services for Women stood at 57.8 of 100 in 2023, 13th of 54 African states and unchanged over the decade. Equal Access to Public Services was 59.3 in 2023, 8th of 54 and 3.7 points below a decade earlier.

Data

National statistics

The Mo Ibrahim Foundation scored Tunisia's capacity of the statistical system at 79.7 of 100 for 2023, 2nd of 54 African states but 4.2 points down over the preceding decade. The World Bank's pillar scores for 2023 put Tunisia at 89% on the use of data, and in the 70 to 89% band for statistical services and products. In 2023 it fell to the 50 to 69% band for both data sources and data infrastructure.

In 2023 the World Bank recorded that a population census and a business census had been run within the past ten years, and an agricultural census within twenty. The statistics institute published the 2024 census's official results on 17 May 2025. In September 2025 it said that the next census will be built on administrative registers, not face-to-face collection. Over the decade to 2023 Tunisia ran three or more labour force surveys, two household surveys, two health surveys and one agricultural survey.

In 2020 the civil register behind vital statistics digitised only births and deaths. Marriages and divorces were legally registered but never entered into the digital system. Death registration was estimated above 95 per cent complete over 2002-2017, with no measurable gap by sex or governorate. Medical death certificates covered 71.0 per cent of the 100,454 deaths of 2021, up from 37.9 per cent in 2001.

The 1999 law sets the principles of statistical activity, scientific independence, statistical secrecy and the duty to respond. A replacement covering big data, administrative-data access and the governance roles of both bodies is being drafted alongside a 2026-2030 strategy. The economy minister had promised its finalisation for the end of 2025, and that date has passed. A September 2025 bulletin combined 2024 census results with the social-security, pension, health-insurance and social-assistance schemes' own records to produce coverage indicators. The bulletin's indicators include 66.3 per cent effective social-security coverage of the occupied population and 82.1 per cent health coverage (September 2025). No data-sharing instrument between the institute and the schemes has been found.

Open data

A new version of the national open-data portal launched in early March 2023, and it listed nine reuse cases in October 2026. In 2025 its catalogue was comprehensive and refreshed weekly or daily, mostly by hand. Open Data Watch's 2024 scores for social statistics ran from 8 of 10 for population and vital statistics to 2.5 for crime and justice. Economic data ran from 7.5 for banking, monetary data and prices to 5 for national accounts and the balance of payments. Environmental data ran from 7 for natural resources to 3.5 for pollution. The cadastre office runs a public geoportal for land and parcel data (2025).

Accessibility of public records rose 19.5 points over 2014 to 2023 to 59.6 of 100, 4th of 54 African states. Disclosure of public records gained 6.3 points over the decade to 32.3, 30th of 54. In 2025 the World Bank assessed the right-to-information law as effective, with online publication duties and a monitoring body.

The 2021 open public data decree sets opening by default, the national portal, an open-data officer in every body and rules on metadata and licensing. A first national strategy for public data reached final draft in March 2026. In February 2026 the Head of Government directed the administration to embed open data in the digitalisation programme. In 2025 a data governance body existed inside another institution, without a data governance strategy. Tunisia publishes its e-procurement data to the Open Contracting Data Standard. Portal performance data and the audit trails of the customs, payroll, HR and e-procurement systems were held and not released (2025).

Geospatial data

In April 2026 the meteorology institute's head of forecasting said it had begun taking third-generation satellite data, alongside 150 new automatic surface stations. He put real-time forecast accuracy at 99 to 100 per cent (April 2026), a figure no other source tests. No national space or geospatial data policy had been found as at August 2026. The institute told a EUMETSAT forum in September 2024 that it has used Meteosat data since the 1980s for flood and drought early warning.

An EU project begun in August 2025, with a budget of EUR 2,745,200, deploys satellite and drone irrigation monitoring at one Tunisian site among four Mediterranean ones. The FAO's water-productivity programme runs with the agriculture ministry and the meteorology institute, with crop mapping in Jendouba and Kairouan. A Jendouba pilot refined evapotranspiration data from 20 metres every ten days to 10 metres daily for irrigation advice. In 2022 the agriculture observatory read FAO's satellite vegetation and agricultural-stress indices to judge the cereal season. In April 2022 a MENAdrought satellite drought index was extended to Tunisia, with seven water-resources staff trained. No national earth-observation programme run by the state has been found.

Geopolitics

US / hyperscaler activities

A 3.3 million dollar American business-support project running since 2019 closed in June 2026, handing its matchmaking platform to the American chamber of commerce. In December 2025 Ooredoo Tunisia announced a Google Cloud deployment of Vertex AI and Gemini models for marketing and customer engagement. In 2021 an embassy-funded programme paired Tunisian fintech startups with Amazon Web Services, IBM and Microsoft, each receiving 11,500 dollars of free cloud services. Its continuation has not been confirmed. No bilateral digital agreement or hyperscaler facility has been found.

China activities

On 15 December 2025 the Chinese ambassador and the higher-education minister opened a supercomputing and education cloud summit in Tunis. The country's first high-performance computing system, a Chinese donation installed by SOHO, ZTE and DSHELL, was inaugurated on 23 July 2026. No value or access rules for the machine have been found. On 27 June 2023 China's industry ministry and Tunisia's communication technologies ministry signed an ICT cooperation memorandum at Tianjin. It covers digital infrastructure, digital-technology applications, and network and data security. No record of its renewal or expiry has been found.

EU activities

The EU frames the Medusa cable's Bizerte landing as a Global Gateway project, and its ambassador Giuseppe Perrone attended the landing on 31 October 2025. The ViaTunisia landing was part-funded by a Connecting Europe Facility grant and coordinated by Orange Wholesale on an open-access model. Orange Tunisia, not the state incumbent, owns the landing station and the Tunisian section of the cable.

In July 2024 the EBRD approved Project Hannibal, a loan to Tunisie Telecom without a sovereign guarantee. An EU first-loss guarantee under the EFSD+ Digital Transformation window backs it, with an EU grant under the Neighbourhood Investment Platform.

Estonia's Cybernetica supplies the X-Road-based national data-exchange platform, funded by the EBRD and Switzerland's SECO, whose first phase ran to 2024. The second phase, with PwC as local partner, is due to complete in 2027.

Gulf/UAE activities

In March 2026 Ooredoo Fintech obtained a Central Bank of Tunisia licence for the walletii digital wallet, with QNB Group and Société Monétique Tunisie. Ooredoo and QNB, both Qatari, put a Gulf-owned group inside the licensed payments perimeter. Emirates International Telecommunications holds the minority stake in Tunisie Telecom alongside the state (July 2024).

India activities

In January 2026 the communication technologies minister and India's ambassador discussed capacity building, Tunisian participation in India's AI summit and joint digital projects. In May 2026 the two health ministries agreed to form a joint working team and draft memoranda on medical tourism and drug manufacturing. No financing commitment or signed bilateral instrument has been found.

Digital sovereignty

A head-of-government circular of 2 September 2026 requires official websites and public-service platforms to be hosted only with the national computing centre, public sectoral centres or licensed operators. Defence and national security are excepted. The cybersecurity agency's list of 17 September 2026 carried six providers holding the N-Cloud label.

A 2023 ministerial order requires every G-Cloud and N-Cloud provider to run primary and backup data centres on Tunisian territory. Circular 16 of July 2024 places sensitive systems in a body's own or a government cloud, and services to citizens and firms with N-Cloud providers. Credit-information companies may neither move their databases abroad nor host credit data in the cloud. In 2025 the Société Tunisienne de Banque tendered for an N-Cloud provider to host its Microsoft Azure platform in Tunisia, buying 1,500 Azure units over three years.

In January 2026 the national computing centre's new private cloud was awarded to Tunisia's Standard Sharing Software with China National Technical Import and Export Corporation. The centre's data centre serves about 4,500 government workstations and replicates to a backup centre. In February 2026 the health ministry said it would adopt fully Tunisian cloud solutions for national health data. The national AI strategy for 2026-2030 is framed around strengthening national sovereignty. No record of who holds the exchange platform's source code, or of its exit terms, had been found as at September 2026.
