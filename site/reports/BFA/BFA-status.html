This report is an attempt to provide a comprehensive summary of the status of digital transformation in your selected country. It is grouped by chapter and topic.

The report only uses evidence from public sources catalogued in the Corpus repository. Corpus aims to discover and index all evidence that is available from public online sources. Pay-walled, password-protected and unpublished documents are not included. This means that while there should be no 'hallucinations' there may well be gaps.

The report is written by Claude Code and updated nightly whenever new relevant evidence arrives.

Two shorter country reports are also maintained. The Progress Report tracks developments that have taken place over the past 12 months. The Maturity Assessment dashboard compares the progress of all 54 countries across 115 indicators.

Governance

Strategies, plans and policies

A national artificial-intelligence roadmap for 2026–2030 was validated in June 2026. On 23 October 2026 the digital ministry presented it with a projected budget of FCFA 30bn; its official publication was still awaited. In August 2026 the government said it intends to turn the roadmap into dedicated regulation. In July 2026 the Prime Minister's office tied AI capability in the central administration to a national digital-transformation roadmap to 2030. The 2025 agenda set four priorities: finishing the data-centre build, covering at least 500 underserved localities, enrolling the population for the unique identifier and launching PACTDIGITAL.

SN@DEN, the national digital-economy strategy, was adopted in June 2018 and runs to 2027. Under its main World Bank operation, Burkina Faso is to adopt an updated digital transformation strategy by August 2028. The update is to carry a gender and disability-inclusion lens.

In 2025 the budget carried the digital transition ministry as section 31, with four programmes. They cover electronic-communications infrastructure, the postal sub-sector, steering and support, and digital transformation. Most of the money runs through PACTDIGITAL, the World Bank digital acceleration operation effective from 19 September 2024. In March 2026 a prime-ministerial circular directed state bodies to favour national hosting. Health data need a special derogation to be hosted abroad. The same "zero data abroad" aim lies behind the two mini data centres opened in January 2026. In July 2026 the ministry intended to repatriate offshore public platforms before the end of 2026.

Legislation and regulation

On 3 August 2026 Burkina Faso's Assemblée législative du peuple adopted two texts. The first is a substantive law on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data. The second is an organic law creating ARCOD, a merged communications and data-protection regulator. Both were promulgated on 22 September 2026, as Law 037-2026/ALP and organic law 038-2026/ALP, repealing loi n°001-2021/AN of 30 March 2021. That law had replaced the 2004 law that had made the country one of Africa's earliest adopters of data protection legislation.

Identification acquired a statutory base with loi n° 027-2024/ALT of 11 October 2024 on the unique electronic identification of the natural person. The July 2026 enrolment decree is taken in application of it. There is no dedicated artificial-intelligence law. The government treats its separate roadmap as the route to one in due course. No localisation requirement is imposed in law (2024). Cross-border transfers of personal data are permitted subject to conditions and authorisation.

Loi n° 014-2024/ALT of 9 July 2024 on information-systems security repeals all contrary prior provisions without naming a single instrument. It also leaves the national cyberspace-control body that is to issue its approvals to be created by a later decree. A decree of 5 March 2025 set the conditions for granting, renewing and withdrawing the technical approvals required to import or sell security products. A decree of 30 July 2026 fixed the conditions for periodic security audits and for accrediting the auditors who conduct them.

Four separate ministerial permissions sit across two Burkinabè statutes — the agrément technique on the import and sale of security products, the accreditation of security auditors and the homologation of critical-infrastructure security products under the 2024 information-systems security law, and the visa statistique under the 2021 statistics law — and three have implementing decrees on file: the agrément technique since March 2025, the visa statistique since August 2025 and the auditor accreditation since July 2026. None has been found for the homologation.

Data protection

An organic law adopted on 3 August 2026 creates ARCOD, the Autorité de régulation de la communication et de la protection des données à caractère personnel. ARCOD absorbs both the Commission de l'Informatique et des Libertés and the Conseil supérieur de la communication. A decree of 16 October 2026 named the head of the Conseil supérieur de la communication as the merged authority's president. On 19 November 2026 the Council of Ministers named its first college of ten councillors. The substantive personal-data law voted the same day indexes fines to turnover excluding tax, scaled to the gravity of the breach. Both were promulgated on 22 September 2026, as organic law 038-2026/ALP and Law 037-2026/ALP. The second adds breach notification, impact assessment and mandatory data protection officers in some cases. It gives controllers six months to comply.

The regime it repeals was built on loi n°001-2021/AN of 30 March 2021. It was supervised by the Commission de l'Informatique et des Libertés, which may conduct on-site inspections and impose fines from 2 million to 100 million FCFA depending on the breach (2024). An implementing decree was adopted in council on 8 June 2022. A controller owes data subjects no notice after a security breach, only a limited rectification notice under article 21. Against the state's own identity database, individuals have no portal or other mechanism to view or manage the personal data ONI holds about them.

Transfers abroad run on authorisation: the commission's application form asks for the recipient, whether the destination country has a data-protection authority, the legal basis of the transfer and the data subjects' consent (September 2026).

The commission made no report public during 2024; its 2024 report was validated at its plenary session of 26 June 2025.

The national e-verbalisation system went live at midnight on 1 August 2026. It detects Highway Code offences by camera and notifies drivers for payment. A decree of 23 July 2026 on the registre national des organismes à but non lucratif fixes for the first time a retention period for those organisations' data after dissolution.

Regional collaboration

Burkina Faso cut its ties with ECOWAS in 2024, together with Mali and Niger. Its cross-border digital ties now run through the Alliance des États du Sahel (AES). The biometric identity card it introduced by decree of 6 November 2025 is aligned with the identity standards of the three AES states and with ICAO. The three states launched a common AES biometric passport in January 2025.

A World Bank delegation reviewed PACTDIGITAL and WURI with the digital ministry on 4 February 2026, reporting mid-term progress on both. Burkina Faso was still counted among the five WURI states when the programme's regional strategy on identity interoperability and mutual recognition was validated in August 2025. In October 2026 the confederation's customs directors and Togo's set 31 January 2027 for a technical unit supervising interconnected customs data flows. They set 1 January 2027 for the company lists behind a regional identification code.

Ouagadougou hosted the West Africa Telecommunications Regulators Assembly's fourth working-groups meeting in April 2026. At a UEMOA digital-economy salon in the city on 14–15 July 2026, professionals carried unanimously a proposal for an Alliance ouest-africaine des noms de domaine. In an interview published on 3 August 2026, its originator said it had still to move into an operational phase.

ARCEP Burkina Faso, Mali's AMRTP and ARCEP Niger signed a convention creating the Conseil des régulateurs du Sahel (September 2026). The council is committed to harmonised legal frameworks, coherent tariffs, common positions in bodies such as the ITU and border frequency coordination. It is also committed to mutual recognition of type-approved equipment and free roaming across the three states. Its statutes and rules of procedure had still to be drafted and adopted.

On 23 October 2026 ARCEP signed bilateral roaming agreements with its Beninese, Ivorian and Senegalese counterparts. The WAEMU Commission called for implementation timetables. On 5 November 2026 the three AES states adopted the digital part of the confederation's Year II roadmap in Ouagadougou. It sets 30 June 2027 for harmonised interconnection and tariff rules, and 31 December 2028 for a confederal network operations centre.

Standards

Décret n°2024-0922 of 2 August 2024 bars any public digital platform from going live until it has been checked against three referentials. They are the administration's visual identity and graphic charter, its données communes and its référentiel d'interopérabilité. Each is to be adopted by prime-ministerial arrêté, alongside a cahier de charges and a business functional-validation report.

The 2024 decree reaches every platform of the public administration, whether built in-house, bought or received as a gift. No development or procurement may start without a favourable technical opinion from the digital transition minister on the terms of reference. Where the administration bore the cost, the supplier must assign full rights in the source code and documentation to the state before service begins. Going live also requires an avis de non objection from the information-systems security body, obtained on a security-test report. A platform operated in breach is suspended on the minister's request.

In 2025 the government interoperability framework and data-exchange arrangement were in place but only partly used across government. There was no electronic government procurement system, nor any of the four e-procurement functions the index tracks. A Manual of Procedures for the Management of Information Systems was validated in September 2025 as a reference framework for government information systems. The national standards agency ABNORM is the country's ISO member body, participating in nine technical committees and observing five (October 2026). Its priority sectors are food, electrotechnical, environmental and building standards. Outside government, the certified electronic invoice was launched in January 2026.

Public debate and participation in policymaking

The law on freedom of association of July 2025 had been invoked to suspend or dissolve 1,174 organisations from mid-April to 11 June 2026. The largest student union was suspended on 26 May after criticising the government's security record. The communication regulator suspended TV5 Monde indefinitely on 5 May; it was the channel's third suspension after two in 2024. The intelligence agency took editors from their newsrooms in October 2025, releasing them within days.

Civil Society Space is the country's third most deteriorated IIAG indicator of the decade, down 39.8 points over 2014–2023 to 59.7 out of 100, 22nd of 54 (2023). Over the same decade Media Freedom fell 25.3 points to 53.4 and Freedom of Expression & Belief 31.1 points to 64.9. Digital Freedom fell 16.5 points to 45.3, 32nd of 54 (2023).

The index's Deliberative & Participatory Governance measure fell 8.6 points over 2014–2023 to 62.6, 20th of 54 (2023). Burkina Faso has no government e-participation platform and none of the five further participation mechanisms the index tracks (2025). It has no central citizen feedback or grievance-redress platform for public services (2025). The Commission Électorale Nationale Indépendante was dissolved in July 2025. Its responsibilities, including custody of the register, passed to the Ministry of Territorial Administration. No elections are scheduled before 2029.

Under the 2015 access-to-information law, the enforcement body ANAIP issues only an avis. A public body then has ten days to say what it has done, and its silence confirms the refusal. Binding relief lies solely with the administrative tribunal: production of the document, annulment of the refusal, an injunction under astreinte. Referral to ANAIP is a compulsory precondition to going to the tribunal. An organic law of 2023 extended the broadcast regulator's reach to social-media accounts above a follower threshold. Organic law 038-2026/ALP, promulgated on 22 September 2026, places broadcast-content regulation and personal-data supervision under one authority, ARCOD.

Finance

Financial sustainability

Burkina Faso's 2026 finance law gives the digital ministry 23.3 billion FCFA of grant and loan capital. All of it sits in the WURI identity programme and PACTDIGITAL. In 2026 the state alone finances 1.24 billion FCFA of the ministry's investment. The 2025 law had carried 15.4 billion FCFA of external capital.

The domestic-state share of the digital lines in Burkina Faso's finance laws, at the appropriated stage, fell from 56.2% for 2024 to 52.0% for 2025 and 39.8% for 2026, as the domestically financed lines eased from 18.2 billion to 15.4 billion FCFA while the externally financed side grew.

The 2025 law set 4.09 billion FCFA of domestic revenue to pay arrears under the state's agreement with Microsoft. That was more than all the ministry's other state-only investment together. The 2026 law carries no such line. Licence renewals for the police cybercrime brigade rose to 40 million FCFA in 2026 from 20 million. In January 2026 operating the two government data centres was costed at about 500 million FCFA over their first three years.

Of 6.64 billion FCFA of domestic investment voted to the digital ministry for 2025, 2.09 billion remained after in-year adjustment. That remainder was spent in full.

In September 2026 the universal service fund FASU was co-financing the push to connect 750 unserved localities alongside PACTDIGITAL. Onatel, Orange Burkina Faso and Telecel were each called on to co-finance on equal terms with the state. The external windows close in 2027 for WURI and in 2028 for PACTDIGITAL. No plan to carry the systems they finance on the recurrent budget had been published as at September 2026.

MoUs and other agreements

On 19 September 2025 the digital transition minister signed a memorandum of understanding with Presight International Services, a subsidiary of the UAE's G42 Group. No value or deliverable is attached to it. In September 2026 the state airports company signed a memorandum with ACI Africa and Barthauer Software. It is to pilot an airport certification and operations platform.

New investments

The World Bank's second phase of West Africa Unique Identification for Regional Integration and Inclusion runs 2020–2027. The 2020–2027 operation is worth USD 273 million and shared with Benin, Niger and Togo.

The World Bank's Digital Acceleration Project was approved on 23 January 2024 and became effective that September. It is USD 150 million running 2024–2028, covering broadband, digital government and digital financial services. It anchors PACTDIGITAL-BF, twelve flagship projects running to 2030 (March 2026).

The Bank's USD 30 million economic governance and GovTech project ran from 2016 to 2022. USD 20 million of additional financing for regional communications infrastructure ran to 2021. A USD 100 million financial-inclusion operation put USD 28.3 million into digitising government-to-person payments and microfinance; its coordinator set out its results in June 2026, after seven years.

Eighty-one digital commitments naming Burkina Faso are on record across 2015–2028, worth roughly USD 490 million equivalent, five still active (November 2026). Thirteen are completed and 63 have no status recorded. The World Bank accounts for five of the eighty-one and about USD 245 million (November 2026). Some USD 403 million of the whole is loans and credits, against about USD 66 million across 73 grants and one public-private partnership at about USD 21 million, while connectivity takes roughly USD 268 million of it (November 2026).

Chinese state lenders are the largest bilateral source: a China Eximbank concessional loan of RMB 560 million for Smart Burkina Faso and a Bank of China buyer's credit of USD 77.35 million for the national fibre backbone, together roughly a third of all recorded digital commitments, both with Huawei as contractor (2026).

In 2022 the first phase of ARCEP's white-zones programme, a public-private partnership with Orange Burkina Faso, installed 183 mobile sites. It cost a little over XOF 13 billion, most of it borne by the operator (2022). The European Union's indicative envelope of EUR 384 million for 2021–2024 sets nothing aside for digital.

ICT Infrastructure

Connectivity

About a fifth of the population used the internet in early 2024. Mobile internet costs 8.8 per cent of gross national income per capita, against an African average of 5.32 per cent (2025). Mobile connections outnumber people (January 2025). The ITU's ICT Development Index placed Burkina Faso at 30.1 out of 100 in 2024, 43rd of 47 African countries. Connection is conditional on identification: an operator must refuse service to anyone who does not present an identity document such as a national identity card or passport (2018).

Mobile Communications is Burkina Faso's most improved of the 96 indicators in the Ibrahim Index of African Governance, up 29.6 points over 2014-2023 to 58.6 out of 100. The index's Internet & Computers indicator rose 21.4 points to 24.3 out of 100 (2023). Infrastructure as a whole is the country's worst-scoring of the index's sixteen sub-categories at 31.6 out of 100 (2023).

Outside the towns, coverage is extended through a universal-service programme run by the regulator ARCEP as a public-private partnership, awarded by tender to Orange Burkina Faso, whose first phase was complete by 2022. That phase left thousands of localities with no mobile network at all.

An internet exchange point, BFIX, operates with sites in Ouagadougou and Bobo-Dioulasso (2024). The state's own network is RESINA, built out since 2007 around a fibre backbone with WiMAX stations, IP telephony, DNS and DHCP. A replacement's architecture was settled by July 2026. On 5 November 2026 the Council of Ministers approved state-funded projects including its optical and IP infrastructure in Ouagadougou and Bobo-Dioulasso. The national fibre network passed 11,292 km in 2025, with 270 km more in the ministry's 2026 work plan.

The minister put 3,140 public buildings on the national administration network on 8 October 2026, against 2,332 in the state ICT agency's 2020 report.

The minister said on 8 October 2026 that 750 villages with no network will be connected by end-2026 and 750 more by 2028, on FCFA 25bn mobilised by the State, under a target of no white zones by 2030.

Data Storage

In July 2026, 120 public digital platforms hosted outside the country had been inventoried for repatriation. Their migration into the two national data centres had started, with completion intended before the end of 2026. The digital transition minister said the search for platforms not yet listed was continuing.

Two mini data centres, commissioned in January 2026, cost 16 billion FCFA, about US$28.6 million. They provide around 3,000 TB of storage and over 7,000 virtual machines, about ten times the prior capacity. They are an interim step toward a planned national data centre. A Digital Infrastructure Supervision Centre, budgeted at about US$5.4 million, was due around October 2026. It is to centralise oversight of the backbone, the data centres and cyberspace. On 5 August 2026 managers put physical progress on the backbone operations centre and the first citizen service centre at about 20%.

In March 2026 a prime-ministerial circular directed state bodies to favour national hosting. Health data need a special derogation to be hosted abroad. The general framework imposes no data localisation requirement and permits cross-border transfers subject to conditions and authorisation (2024).

ANPTIC's government cloud at cloud.gov.bf runs eight nodes across four datacentres and serves 354 connected communes (2026). No cloud-first or cloud adoption policy was in place in 2025. IKA Solution launched IKA CLOUD, a commercial local hosting service, in Ouagadougou on 31 July 2026. It sells to firms, start-ups, individuals and public institutions. On 8 October 2026 the minister put the national data centre planned for 2028 at an estimated FCFA 20bn.

Energy

Two per cent of Burkina Faso's rural population had access to electricity in 2023, against 62.6 per cent of the urban population, a gap of about 61 percentage points. Nationally, electricity reached 21.7 per cent of the population (2023). Access to Energy is among the ten worst-scoring of the Ibrahim Index's 96 indicators for the country, at 16.3 out of 100 (2023). It ranks 45th of 54, and gained only 4.4 points over 2014-2023.

Supply is unreliable where it exists: firms report about eight electrical outages a month (2024), roughly weekly interruption. It is also dear against income. The national utility SONABEL charges residential customers on an increasing block tariff (2024). The first 50 kWh each month cost roughly XOF 75 per kWh, and use above that about XOF 128 to 138 per kWh (2024). Renewable sources, chiefly hydropower alongside expanding utility-scale solar, accounted for 13.92 per cent of generation in 2021.

Burkina Faso scores highly on RISE for its off-grid and mini-grid enabling environment (2024). It has dedicated institutions and a policy framework for decentralised electrification. The energy regulator put the rural electrification rate at 7.2 per cent in 2023, up from 1 per cent in 2003, with 615 localities electrified. The national rural electrification strategy aims at 50 per cent by 2028.

Technical Capacity

The two mini data centres commissioned in January 2026 are run by national teams, not by a foreign operator. In 2017 identity production depended on foreign suppliers. A Canadian firm gave remote assistance and training for the identity card, and a French firm for the iCivil bubble tags. Police and gendarmerie officers ran day-to-day operations. In 2025 the passport was supplied by the Chinese firm Emptech.

In 2023 Public Administration was the third best-scoring of the Ibrahim Index's sixteen sub-categories for the country, at 64.0 out of 100. It ranked 11th of 54. Effective Administration stood at 66.4, 12th of 54, down 3.2 points over 2014-2023. Budgetary and financial management, one of the country's strongest measures, slipped over the same decade.

On 23 July 2026 the Council of Ministers made board appointments at ANPTIC. It also authorised moving the remaining contract staff of the former CENATRIN into the civil service; CENATRIN was the data-processing centre created in 1970 and liquidated in 2005.

Cybersecurity

A decree of 5 March 2025 set the conditions for the technical approvals that the cybersecurity law requires for importing or selling security products. Two decrees adopted on 30 July 2026 identify and classify the country's organismes à infrastructures critiques. These are any entity housing installations, works or systems indispensable to the vital functions of society, expressly spanning energy, water, health, telecommunications and the financial sector. The decrees also fix the conditions for periodic security audits of information systems and for accrediting the auditors who conduct them. Loi n° 014-2024/ALT of 9 July 2024 had created three separate permissions. They are accreditation of auditors, homologation of the security products used by critical-infrastructure bodies, and an agrément technique conditioning any importation or sale of security hardware or software. The law left the national cyberspace-control body that issues all three to be created by decree, naming no agency.

Two years and three weeks separated the enactment of the three licensing regimes from the decrees that let any of them operate.

Penalties under Loi n° 014-2024/ALT run from 1 million to 100 million FCFA (2024).

In the first three quarters of 2026 the cybercrime brigade recorded 6,477 complaints and FCFA 2.23bn in losses, already above the whole of 2025. Mobile-money fraud is among the six types it names.

As at August 2026, 205 government platforms had been security-tested, 1,460 internet-facing .bf platforms mapped and 28 public bodies placed under enhanced protection. No public digital platform may go live without an avis de non objection from the information-systems security body. The country sits in the "Establishing" tier of the ITU's Global Cybersecurity Index 2024, for scores of 55 to 85. An information-systems security agency, ANSSI, has existed since 2018. On 23 October 2026 it launched a national network of information-security officers and cyber focal points across public bodies. Its security operations centre, in service since April 2025, monitors connected public bodies' systems; the agency reported more than 900 alerts a day in the first half of 2025. The personal-data law promulgated on 22 September 2026 adds a breach-notification duty.

DPI

Data Exchange

In 2025 data exchange between government institutions was not operational at national scale. Systems remained largely siloed and the interoperability layer was still under development. The ministry developed sixty-nine new digital platforms for public services during 2025.

In late July 2026 the Ministry of Security presented a national supervision centre for traffic enforcement. It joins the DGTTM vehicle database, the e-Contravention system launched in 2023 and the Faso Arzeka payments platform. The tax authority's 2025–2027 plan targets interconnecting seventeen of its sites over the RESINA network. The regional credit bureau takes data from 20 of the 120 microfinance institutions licensed at the end of 2025. Only those above FCFA 2bn of savings or credit report, and client consent is now required (October 2026). In 2022 iCivil linked maternity wards to the central civil registration database. A midwife could register a birth from a smartphone against a unique identifier.

In 2017 there was no formal interoperability between the ONI identity database and the civil registry, the electoral commission, or health and tax systems. No unique identifier was shared across them. In 2025 work on connecting the social registry to the planned unique identifier had begun with World Bank support. It had not been implemented. In April 2022 CEFORE left the shared Système Intégré des Guichets Uniques for its own standalone module. That system had loosely connected the land, construction and business one-stop shops since 2016. A référentiel d'interopérabilité is now among the documents a public platform must satisfy before it goes into service.

Digital Identity and CRVS

Under a decree of 6 November 2025, the Office national d'identification introduced the Carte d'Identité Biométrique de l'AES on 1 December 2025, replacing the CNIB. Mass production began in January 2026. From October 2026 territorial gendarmerie brigades may produce it; a birth-certificate extract or nationality certificate is still required to enrol. The cards then in circulation are withdrawn over a five-year transition. The new card is issued from age 5 and compulsory from 13, against 15 before (2025).

In September 2025 the identity office's director-general said about 3 million Burkinabe of age to hold an identity card had none. Individuals have no self-service route to see or manage what the database holds about them (2024).

A law on unique electronic identification of natural persons was adopted in October 2024, tying the future number to the CNIB, the passport and other records. On 30 July 2026 the Conseil des ministres adopted a decree organising mass registration for it under the WURI programme. On 5 November 2026 the Conseil des ministres adopted a decree creating the foundational identification system and the national register of the unique identifier. It named the digital transition ministry as the interim enrolling and issuing body. The President launched mass enrolment on 11 November 2026: a free, random 12-digit identifier issued on fingerprint and iris capture at 160 sites and by mobile teams. It does not replace existing identity documents. A pilot had enrolled more than 16,000 people, and a first count is due at the end of December 2026.

The security minister made the securing of academic and professional diplomas the theme of the seventh International Identity Day on 16 September 2026. No instrument, operator or timetable has been published for it. Since a decree of 15 December 2023 the national identity card is the only document a citizen may use to buy a SIM, with at most two SIMs per operator.

Digital Payments and Fintech

Burkina Faso was second only to Côte d'Ivoire in intra-WAEMU cross-border electronic-money payments sent and received in 2024. It recorded 22 million such operations, worth 1,459 billion FCFA (2024). Yet it held only 9.53% of the electronic-money accounts opened across the union, fourth behind Côte d'Ivoire, Senegal and Benin (2024).

That makes the country the carrier of roughly a quarter of the value of all cross-border mobile money moving inside the union while holding under a tenth of its accounts — a corridor economy rather than a deep domestic market.

Account ownership at a financial institution or with a mobile money provider reached 51.4% of adults (2024). Transaction volumes grew 21.04% over the year, ahead of Côte d'Ivoire and behind Senegal (2024). Burkina Faso's share of union electronic-money volume fell from 19.7% in 2023 to 18.2%.

The BCEAO launched the interoperable instant payment platform PI-SPI across all member states on 30 September 2025. It carries round-the-clock transfers between banks, microfinance institutions and electronic money issuers. Instruction No. 001-01-2024, in force from its signature in 2024, imposes complaint-handling procedures, professional civil liability insurance and security safeguards on payment institutions. At 29 June 2026 the banking commission's register for Burkina Faso listed one electronic-money issuer, Orange Money, and two payment institutions registered in 2025.

Faso Arzêka lets citizens pay taxes, duties and administrative charges by app or USSD (2024). The tax directorate counted 427,045 electronic tax returns and 75,610 electronic tax payments in 2023. More than 16,000 CARFO and CNSS pensioners were receiving their pensions by mobile money (June 2026). Safety-net transfers had been paid by mobile money since 2018. A 2023 study found illiterate recipients sharing their PINs with agents and cashing out in full. The treasury is one of only two in the union authorised to issue electronic money, from August 2024. The instrument approved is a prepaid card rather than a wallet. The regional rulebook confines a treasury scheme to a closed network of the state's own allocation beneficiaries, delegated through GIM-UEMOA.

Registries

At 30 June 2026, 433,888 households, more than 2 million people, were enrolled in the Registre Social Unique across 110 of Burkina Faso's 351 communes, 30% of the target. Each registered person carries a unique identification code under the National Social Protection Strategy 2024–2028. In 2025 its secretariat committed to including refugees.

A decree of 22 October 2026 provides for computerised processing of civil-status data, a civil-status identifier, electronic archiving of registers and online delivery of certificates. The Code des personnes et de la famille, adopted on 1 September 2025, gives digital civil registers legal standing. The VENEEM platform, piloted from June 2024 in Komsilga and one Ouagadougou arrondissement, assigns a civil-status identifier at registration. Only 60.65% of births are registered within the legal deadline (2024). The iCivil notification system operates at around 130 of 2,584 vital-event notification points (2024). Thirty communes held fully digitised civil-status registers by the end of 2025. In 2025, 121,000 civil-status documents went to internally displaced people, nearly double the 65,000 target.

The Fichier National du RCCM went online in December 2023, with free public search of 80,604 businesses (2026). The online portal took 6,916 creation requests in 2026 to 13 November, 98% from sole traders. The 2024 general business census found the economy overwhelmingly informal. Online extracts cover only businesses registered with the Ouagadougou commercial court (2023). CEFORE issues the register number and the tax identifier in one transaction. A beneficial-ownership declaration platform opened in September 2024; the register it feeds is not public (2025). SyC@D, the integrated land, cadastre and state-domain platform, launched on 21 March 2025. In April 2025 Ouagadougou's EU-supported street-addressing programme extended to arrondissements 5 and 12, after 1 and 2.

The biometric electoral register, audited in October 2020 at 6,490,926 voters, passed to the Ministry of Territorial Administration when the electoral commission was dissolved in July 2025. No elections are scheduled before 2029. A decree of 23 July 2026 redrew the register of non-profit organisations, grounding it in the anti-money-laundering law of December 2024. It fixes for the first time how long data are kept after dissolution.

Sectoral management information systems

Rural primary healthcare clinics record consultations on paper registers, which health workers summarise monthly onto paper forms (2026). District teams key the forms into DHIS2. A tablet tool evaluated in one rural district ran alongside paper reporting and did not replace it. Facilities filed 95.7% of their expected reports to the health information system in 2025, private facilities 90.6%. Primary school directors complete paper forms on a fixed annual census day (2024). The forms are physically routed to district and regional education offices, where staff enter them into the STATEDUC 2 application. In October 2026 the security minister listed the computerised occurrence book among his department's 2022–2026 results. No count of stations using it has been published. Since 2012 the police have run IRAPOL, a national police data system (2019), which INTERPOL counted in April 2020 among six fully operational national systems in its regional programme.

Financial management, treasury, tax, customs, human resources, payroll, social insurance and debt management systems are all recorded as in use (2025). Electronic procurement is the one core system absent.

Most are built to order rather than bought in: five of the seven core systems — financial management, tax, human resources, payroll and social insurance — run custom software, against commercial off-the-shelf products for customs and debt management.

In October 2023 the tax directorate launched two in-house platforms: eTITRE for land-occupation titles in Ouagadougou and Bobo-Dioulasso, and eCME for online assessment of micro-enterprises. An integrated eCustoms payment platform launched in December 2023 is to let importers pay duties by a choice of methods (2024). The SIGASPE civil-service database is linked to the identity card's biometric database to authenticate anyone working as a civil servant (2017). A national pupil file with online enrolment and school identity cards was under review with WURI support (June 2025). Progressive enrolment was due from the following school year. A conference paper reported the OpenClinic pilot at the Manga regional hospital ending in full digitisation of admissions and use of the electronic patient record across departments (2026).

Other GovTech and e-Gov

Automated camera enforcement of the Highway Code went live at midnight on 1 August 2026. The system in its first month recorded 19,910 offences — seven in ten for no seatbelt, the rest speeding, red lights and phones at the wheel. They were generating CFA 146.6 million in fines, of which CFA 16.1 million had been collected by 4 September 2026.

The UN E-Government Development Index put Burkina Faso at 0.2895, 175th of 193 states (2024). The national government service portal is informational rather than transactional (2025). None of the six portal capabilities the index tracks are in place (2025). There is no electronic government procurement system (2025). The e-Burkindi platform's procurement-planning module had reached a pilot with ministries by 2025. Direct awards and restricted tenders rose to 75.9% of contract value in 2025, against a 15% ceiling.

Taxpayers file declarations including VAT through the eSINTAX portal and register, file and pay tax online (2025). A national electronic criminal record service runs at ecasier-judiciaire.gov.bf (2025). It was joined in December 2025 by online criminal complaint and case tracking, a prison visit permit service and an online commercial register service.

On the tax side, certified invoicing systems went on sale from 7 September 2026, twenty months after the order that established it. The tax directorate then made the certified system mandatory on every invoice of large and medium firms from 1 January 2027 (November 2026). Penalties are fines per invoice, possible closure and loss of VAT deduction. FasoSign was launched on 15 September 2026, already integrated into the mission-order, administrative-mail and cadastre platforms. Its keys and certificates were presented as held by the state itself.

The construction ministry's e-demarcheur platform, launched on 21 July 2026, issues the licence an April 2026 arrêté makes compulsory for anyone brokering residential leases, wholly online. 220 business licences have been moved online through a state platform generator, the ministry still working with the line agencies to make them fully accessible (July 2026).

Digitalisation

Digitalisation of sub-national government

The government ICT agency's cloud at cloud.gov.bf runs on eight cloud nodes across four datacentres and serves 354 connected communes (2026). Thirty communes held fully digitised civil-status registers at the end of 2025. The enrolment decree of 30 July 2026 requires the biometric registration operation to be run entirely by public structures. Fingerprints and iris data are kept in national custody. The decree delivers the operation through national, regional, provincial and communal committees, the communal tier serving as the delivery machinery of a central programme.

Ouagadougou launched EU-supported street addressing in arrondissements 5 and 12 in April 2025, after arrondissements 1 and 2. The scheme covers more than 2,277 streets and 43,000 doors. It builds an address database for water, electricity, postal, fire and cadastre services. In November 2025 the commune was piloting SYCOTAX, card-terminal collection of market taxes with SMS receipts, in two markets. It said it would extend it to 23 more. The World Bank's USD 30 million Economic Governance, GovTech and Citizen Engagement project ran from 2016 to 2022 and has closed. It worked with the Ministry of Territorial Administration and Decentralisation on GovTech solutions, digital public services and digital accountability tools. Senior staff at that ministry were trained in personal data protection in July 2025. The first Maison du citoyen, named Zama Tchey, was under construction at a ministerial visit on 5 August 2026. It is to bring postal, financial and digital services closer to the population, and is delivered by the state and the postal service rather than by a commune. The digital-transition minister said 80 Maisons du citoyen had been launched on a FCFA 10bn government envelope (2026). The target is one in each of the 351 communes by 2030.

Rural digital data capture

In 2026 rural health workers were writing consultations into paper registers and summarising them monthly onto forms. District teams key the forms into DHIS2. In 2024 primary-school directors completed paper forms on a fixed annual census day. The forms travel physically through the circonscriptions and regional directorates, where staff enter them into STATEDUC 2. In October 2026 the security minister counted the computerised occurrence book among his department's results. No figure has been published for how many rural posts use it.

In 2024 main civil registry centres were networked and millions of archival records, already written by hand, were digitised. In June 2026 registry agents in the rural commune of Koloko were trained on the national VENEEM registration software. A tablet tool evaluated in one rural district in 2019–2021 ran in parallel with paper reporting. In 2023 rural electricity access was a small fraction of the urban rate. In March 2025 the state put more than 3 billion CFA francs into restoring networks knocked out by insecurity.

In 2023 Rural Economy was Burkina Faso's best-scoring Ibrahim Index sub-category, at 67.8 out of 100, 15th of 54. Rural representation and participation was one of its ten best indicators, at 78.2. Rural market access had fallen 14.5 points since 2014, to 69.3.

Technology

AI

In June 2026 Burkina Faso validated a national artificial-intelligence roadmap for 2026–2030. On 23 October 2026 the digital ministry presented it with a projected budget of FCFA 30bn, 23 pilot AI solutions and 5,000 specialised jobs by 2030. Its official publication was still awaited. In July 2026 the UEMOA Commission counted the country among four union members holding a national strategy. A ministry workshop in March 2026 set out to build language-model corpora in Mooré, Dioula, Fulfuldé and Gulmancema.

No dedicated artificial-intelligence law had been adopted by August 2026. The personal-data law adopted on 3 August 2026 bars transferring sensitive personal data abroad. The new communications regulator was given its own rule-making power, which the government said would let it keep pace with technologies such as AI.

Automated traffic enforcement began on 1 August 2026. Its cameras in Ouagadougou use AI to detect four categories of offence and read number plates against the vehicle database. In October 2025 the digital ministry and the state broadcaster agreed to use AI for public information, including Mooré-to-French translation. On 15 October 2026 the university AI centre CITADEL launched L'Essentiel.bf, which summarises four national news outlets in French and Mooré. Since 2024 the national tuberculosis programme has read mobile chest X-rays with CAD4TB software. It screened 1,730 people from September 2024 to April 2025 and found 61 cases.

ICT Industry

A 2025 public listing of GitHub contributors by country places the Burkinabè developer community in the low hundreds. The general business census of 2024, published in May 2026, counted 389,172 economic units, 96.5% of them informal. Economic diversification fell 22.0 points over 2014–2023 to 26.9 out of 100, 51st of 54.

In 2025 the contractor GAF AG upgraded the mining cadastre's eMC+ system under a World Bank-funded project. Since March 2025 Orange Money's Burkinabè operation has run on Comviva's Mobiquity Pay X platform. On 31 July 2026 IKA Solution, a Burkinabè firm founded in 2014, launched a local data-hosting service in Ouagadougou. In 2024 firms reported frequent interruptions to electricity supply. Any import or sale of security hardware or software needs a technical approval from the national cyberspace-control body (2024).

Innovation ecosystem

As at 2023, Burkina Faso's innovation hubs and fab labs included OuagaLab, InViis Lab and La Fabrique. In 2025 Burkina Faso launched the Faso Andubè platform to promote local innovation. In April 2026 the government told the UN science and technology commission it had sought its support in September 2025 to build a technology park. YENGA, built by the Burkinabè firm KREEZUS, brings three mobile operators and five wallets into one payment interface (March 2026). The second edition of Le Faso Digital opened on 17 October 2026, with 201 solutions on show from 208 entries. The virtual fair is run by the news site LeFaso.net.

In July 2025 a national workshop validated a draft startup law and its implementing decree. The draft offers tax relief and easier formalities. The FCFA 10bn Burkina Start Up fund has run since 2017. The Agence Française de Développement's AFIDBA call for projects on social business and digital innovation ran to 2022 and has closed. It supported digital entrepreneurship in Burkina Faso, Ghana, Senegal and Morocco. On 21 October 2026 the digital ministry consulted the sector on a planned Ouagadougou ICT mini-technopole and a digital startup support fund. No amount or timetable had been set for the fund.

Burkina Faso ranked 129th of 133 economies in the Global Innovation Index 2024.

Capacity

Literacy

In 2023 the World Bank ranked Burkina Faso second to last in the world on ICT skills. UNCDF's Inclusive Digital Economy Scorecard put its digital literacy sub-score at 9% (2020).

In the 2024 Ibrahim Index Burkina Faso scored 25.7 out of 100 on education enrolment, 46th of 54 African countries (2023). It scored 26.5 on education quality, 43rd, and 39.7 on completion, a score that fell over 2014-2023. Human Resources in Education scored 90.6, 16th of 54, up 10.4 points over 2014-2023. Equality in education rose 14.2 points to 52.2, 24th of 54 (2023).

In June 2025 a national pupil file with online enrolment and school identity cards was under review, with enrolment to begin the following school year. In May 2026 a university-run pilot in Manga and Bingo reported tracking more than 12,000 pupils by unique identifier.

On 28 July 2026 the national inclusive finance agency and Wave Burkina opened a financial education and consumer protection campaign at Bobo-Dioulasso. Their partnership has run since 2025 and is extending in 2026 to Ouagadougou, Bobo-Dioulasso, Kaya and Tenkodogo. Its 72 hours of sessions cover budgeting, savings, credit and consumer protection, and participants return to their communities as relays.

Training and skills

Burkina Faso's digital skills programme for public servants charges a fee for its training (2025). It reaches only strategy or programme level, and covers basic digital skills and data literacy.

The Conseil des ministres adopted the statutes of the Académie technologique du Faso on 23 July 2026. A week later, on 30 July 2026, it adopted a Répertoire général des métiers de la formation professionnelle covering 14 priority sectors, digital transition among them.

Senior staff of the Ministry of Territorial Administration were trained in personal data protection in July 2025. The training extended compliance work into the ministry that runs local administration. On 13-14 July 2026 the Prime Minister's office ran a two-day session for its own senior staff on management tools for the ethical and responsible use of artificial intelligence. In September 2026 the digital ministry gave the secretaries general of all ministries and institutions a two-day course on generative AI tools. The course covered which administrative data may be put into them. On 4 November 2026 the tax directorate began training more than 400 agents on its e-filing, certified e-invoicing and online cadastre platforms. The training runs under a FCFA 2.72bn public-finance training programme for 2026-2028, funded equally by Swiss cooperation and the government. The national artificial-intelligence roadmap is meant to build national skills in artificial intelligence, data science and emerging technologies (June 2026).

A UNDP project with the government, covering digital skills alongside digital inclusion and ICT policy, runs to 2026. Enabel's Digital for Girls and Women project, which put digital skills and digital rights awareness to girls and women, closed in 2025. Outside the state there are innovation hubs and fab labs such as OuagaLab, InViis Lab and La Fabrique (2023).

Research institutions

The Centre d'excellence en Intelligence Artificielle pour le Développement (CITADEL) began a second phase in April 2025. The centre sits at the Université Virtuelle du Burkina Faso, and the new phase is oriented to research and action. It had won competitive international funding from Canada's International Development Research Centre. In March 2026 a ministry workshop set out to build large-language-model data corpora in Mooré, Dioula, Fulfuldé and Gulmancema. The corpora are for speech recognition, automatic translation and voice synthesis. In November 2025 a workshop at the virtual university set out a roadmap to complete the national research and education network's link to the regional backbone. That link was still partial.

Inclusion

Access to services

Burkina Faso scored 61.6 out of 100 on the Ibrahim Index measure of whether public services reach all groups and regions on equal terms, 5th of 54 (2023). The score fell 6.5 points over 2014-2023. Protection against Discrimination is among the country's ten worst-scoring indicators, at 16.7 out of 100 and 23rd of 54 (2023). It did not change over the same decade.

A national identity card is required to open a bank account (2025). The law requires no birth certificate for access to public health services (2017). The card is not free (2025). Civil registration outside the towns is still done on paper, and parents in remote areas can spend days reaching an office (2024). Nearly four in ten births are not declared within the legal deadline (2024). Under article 8 of the access-to-information law, a person living with disability pays nothing where the information requested concerns their educational, professional or social integration. The same law admits a verbal request. It allows appeal to its oversight authority only against a decision on a written request or a transcribed verbal one, so an untranscribed verbal request carries no remedy.

The national government service portal remains informational only (2025), and there is no central grievance or feedback platform for public services (2025). The public-service ministry launched a complaints platform, plaintes.gov.bf, in March 2019. No later account of it has been found. In February 2026 the dysfunctions regulation commission opened DenonciationPro for confidential reports of misconduct in the administration. In November 2026 the health ministry launched Lafiako, a platform for patients' complaints with a ten-day response target. In July 2026 the state's own promotion agency said that financial inclusion takes in savings, credit and insurance as well as an account or a transfer. In November 2026 a draft second inclusive finance strategy for 2027-2031, targeting 90% inclusion, went to national validation. The agency put the rate at 81.8% at the end of December 2025.

Digital divides

In 2023 grid electricity reached most urban households and almost no rural ones. In March 2025 the state had to put more than 3 billion CFA francs into restoring networks in areas destabilised by insecurity. ARCEP's universal-service programme cleared several hundred mobile white zones in its first phase, through 183 new sites (2022). Connectivity was extended to 370 additional localities during 2025. In October 2026 the regulator still counted about 3,514 localities with no operator present, nearly 40% of the country's localities and some 4.7 million people. In 2025 mobile internet cost a substantially higher share of income here than the African average. In 2021, 39.4% of urban and 15.2% of rural people aged 15 to 49 had used the internet in the past year.

The Ibrahim Index's Public Perception of Infrastructure score rose 9.3 points over 2014-2023 to 30.5 out of 100, 26th of 54 (2023). Burkinabè rate the infrastructure around them markedly better than they did a decade ago. The score remains below a third of the maximum. The same index's Internet & Computers measure is among the country's most improved indicators, while its score stays at 24.3 out of 100 (2023).

Enabel's Digital for Girls and Women project closed in 2025. UNDP's inclusive digital transformation project with the government runs only to 2026. What is live is small, such as the financial-literacy programme Wave runs with the national inclusive-finance agency, aimed at rural women (July 2026). For disability, the commitment found is to write a gender and disability-inclusion lens into an updated digital transformation strategy (2025). On language, a March 2026 ministry workshop set out to build large-language-model corpora in Mooré, Dioula, Fulfuldé and Gulmancema. The aim is for services to reach citizens who do not speak French.

Data

National statistics

The general business census run in 2024 and published in May 2026 counted 389,172 economic units, 96.5% of them operating informally. The World Bank's 2023 statistical performance reading had found no business or establishment survey in the previous ten years. Its most recent agricultural census falls only within the last twenty. A population census and repeated household and health surveys fall inside the decade to 2023.

The system runs on loi n° 036-2021 of 6 December 2021, which abrogates the 2007 statistics law by name and by number. Any survey or census collecting individual data, public or private or run by an international organisation, needs a visa statistique from the minister before it may go into the field. An operation without one is suspended by the minister. Its results are struck with nullity by ministerial decision rather than by a court. Refusal to answer without legitimate motive, or knowingly incomplete answers, carries three months' imprisonment and a fine of 150,000 to 2,000,000 FCFA. The same law obliges the authorities to work free of political power and interest groups. It requires them to publish to a calendar announced in advance, but lets certain authorities receive statistics early under embargo. On 16 July 2025 three decrees under the law extended the visa to private surveys and obliged public bodies to share statistical data.

Capacity of the Statistical System is the ninth most improved indicator of the decade in the country's governance profile (2023). It rose 16.5 points over 2014-2023 to 44.7 out of 100, and still ranks 31st of 54. In the 2023 statistical performance reading, data use scores above 89%. Data sources score only 20-49%, the weakest of the five pillars (2023). Its national statistical yearbook for 2024 was published in December 2025. The institute's 2026-2030 plan sets out platforms to exploit administrative data, civil registration included.

The institute and the customs directorate signed a protocol on 27 December 2024 to strengthen the institute's access to customs data for foreign trade statistics. The institute's 2024 foreign trade yearbook is compiled from SYDONIA World customs declarations.

Open data

In the 2024 Open Data Inventory, agriculture scores 10 out of 10, education outcomes 8.5 and national accounts 8. Yet the digital category scores zero and population statistics, labour, crime and health outcomes 5 each. The finance ministry cited a score of 77 out of 100 in the 2025 inventory, first in West Africa and second in Africa (September 2026).

The right to ask has been law since loi n° 051-2015/CNT of 30 August 2015. It makes public information and administrative documents communicable of right from any organisme de service public. A body must answer within twenty days, extendable once by ten, and every refusal must be reasoned and cite the provision relied on. Nothing may be charged for lodging a request or for the body's search time. Electronic delivery is free where the document already exists in electronic form.

The statistics institute has gone the other way for its own data: its open data licence grants free, worldwide reuse, commercial reuse included, with attribution (October 2026). Separately, a university artificial-intelligence centre's decision-data portal held 147 datasets with no public API in June 2026.

Commercial reuse without prior authorisation and payment of the royalty carries two to five years' imprisonment and a fine of 100,000 to 1,000,000 FCFA (2015). A for-profit company is fined 5 per cent of its last closed turnover (2015). Yet an official who obstructs access faces an administrative ladder from a letter of explanation to revocation, criminal prosecution being left to the archives law and the penal code. One article withholds whole classes of information without a harm test, among them government deliberations, defence, state security, public order and international relations.

Disclosure of Public Records rose 23.0 points over the decade to 41.9 out of 100, 16th of 54 (2023). It is the third most improved indicator in the country's profile. Accessibility of Public Records stands at 48.8, 10th of 54. A government open data portal carrying a comprehensive data catalogue, updated weekly or daily, was in place in 2025.

Geospatial data

The national statistics institute put a geospatial analysis of its Système d'Information des Localités to validation in September 2025. Burkina Faso had adopted no national space or Earth observation policy as at August 2026. The GARBAL call service is built on satellite data and the meteorological agency's forecasts. It had more than 260,000 users and about two million calls by mid-2024. The agency has run the WRF forecast model at 9 km and 3 km on its own high-performance computer since June 2023.

Geopolitics

US / hyperscaler activities

In January 2026 the United States ambassador announced a digitalisation and artificial-intelligence training initiative for 2026. It is to run in American spaces in Ouagadougou, Bobo-Dioulasso and Koudougou. No US hyperscaler cloud region, data centre or platform agreement holding Burkinabè government or citizen data had been found as at August 2026.

China activities

A Chinese vendor sits in the travel-document layer of the identity system: Burkina Faso's passport is supplied by Emptech (2025). That is a procurement relationship and not an operating one.

Chinese state lenders are the largest bilateral source of digital financing to the country (2026), on two commitments: China Eximbank's RMB 560 million concessional loan for Smart Burkina Faso, and Bank of China's USD 77.35 million buyer's credit for the national fibre backbone. Together they are roughly a third of all recorded digital financing commitments to Burkina Faso. Both build fibre, and both name Huawei as contractor.

EU activities

The EU's 2021-2027 Multiannual Indicative Programme for Burkina Faso lists « Numérique – digitalisation » among its transversal themes. Digital has no priority area and no allocation of its own. Digital regulation and regional market integration sit in the EU's regional programme for sub-Saharan Africa. The country programme's digital content comes through its sector envelopes. These cover the criminal-justice chain, digital tools for vulnerable people's access to basic services, and digital financial services in agri-food chains.

Enabel's Digital for Girls and Women project, on digital skills and digital rights, closed in 2025. The Agence Française de Développement's regional call on social business and digital innovation closed in 2022. It covered Burkina Faso, Ghana, Senegal and Morocco. In April 2025 Ouagadougou's EU-backed street-addressing programme extended to arrondissements 5 and 12.

Gulf/UAE activities

On 19 September 2025 in Abu Dhabi the digital transition minister signed a memorandum of understanding with Presight International Services. Presight is a subsidiary of the UAE's G42 Group. The memorandum covers artificial intelligence skills, digital infrastructure, e-government services and cybersecurity. No Gulf investment, data-centre or platform presence in the country had been found as at September 2026.

India activities

India's Ministry of External Affairs brief of April 2026 records a 1994 Joint Commission agreement and ITEC and ICCR training cooperation. It lists lines of credit, including US$25 million for rural electrification signed on 29 March 2013. Its one digital item is Burkina Faso's inclusion in the Pan African e-Network Project.

Digital sovereignty

In February 2026 the prime minister directed the administration to adopt sovereign digital tools for email and document management. Third-party solutions are to be replaced progressively. The 2025 finance law had set 4.09 billion FCFA to clear arrears under the state's Microsoft agreement.

In March 2026 a prime-ministerial circular told state bodies to favour national hosting. The personal-data law adopted on 3 August 2026 bars transferring sensitive personal data abroad. The two administration data centres inaugurated in January 2026 are to hold identity, civil-status, public-finance, health and security data. They are run by Burkinabè engineers, and a further national data centre is planned for 2028.

Under a decree of 2024, any platform the administration paid for must have full rights in its source code assigned to the state before it goes live. In March 2026 the director-general of digital transformation said the state now refuses black-box platforms and requires skills transfer. Enrolment for the unique identifier is reserved to public bodies so that fingerprint and iris data stay in national custody (July 2026). In 2017 identity-card production relied on a Canadian firm for remote assistance and training.

As at September 2026 no terms had been published on data jurisdiction, portability or exit for the Microsoft agreement, the September 2025 memorandum with Presight or Cybastion's August 2025 modular data-centre deal.

Mali's national data centre, opened in early 2026 before the AES digital ministers, is to host shared AES infrastructure in the short term.
