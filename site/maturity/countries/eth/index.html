Strategies, plans and policies

Digital transformation strategy: national strategy

2 Preparing Last assessed Sep 2026 Stage last moved First assessment Methodology · On the map

Ethiopia's Council of Ministers approved Digital Ethiopia 2030 in November 2025, but its public text and period are not known.

Governance and finance. The National Digital Transformation Council, chaired by Deputy Prime Minister Temesgen Tiruneh, met on 30 July 2026 and was said to provide strategic leadership for the Digital Ethiopia Strategy. Public ICT and digital allocations rose from about USD 250 million in 2020 to USD 500 million in 2024, which Paradigm Initiative attributes to the predecessor, Digital Ethiopia 2025.

Standing. The Council of Ministers approved Digital Ethiopia 2030 on 29 November 2025 and the Prime Minister launched it on 20 December 2025, as the successor to Digital Ethiopia 2025.

Text. The Ethiopian News Agency describes Digital Ethiopia 2030 as a comprehensive national blueprint building on the Homegrown Economic Reform Agenda.

Period. Digital Ethiopia 2025 covered 2020 to 2025, and the Prime Minister said on 20 December 2025 that it had been completed as the country moved to Digital Ethiopia 2030.

Delivery plan. CIO Africa, on 27 January 2026, lists the targets for 2030: 100 percent 5G coverage in 100 cities, 128 million mobile subscribers, a Fayda digital ID for every adult and digital payments up sevenfold.

Reporting. On 30 July 2026 the Council reviewed progress on the Digital Ethiopia Strategy, Fayda and the 5 Million Coders initiative.

Last twelve months. Digital Ethiopia 2030 was launched on 20 December 2025, with enablers named as electricity, cybersecurity, a sovereign government cloud, broadband rollout and scaling Fayda ID and interoperable payments.

Noted, not assessed. Digital Government Strategy and Enterprise Architecture 2024-2029, Ethiopia's new digital government strategy 2025-2030, Homegrown Economic Reform Agenda, Ten-Year National Development Plan, Next Horizon: Digital & Beyond 2028, Digital Health Blueprint 2021-2030, Digital Education Strategy and Implementation Plan 2023-2028, Fayda National Digital ID, 5 Million Ethiopian Coders Initiative and Eastern Africa Regional Digital Integration Project.

Not held. No public text is held for Digital Ethiopia 2030, and no period is stated for it. No domestic finance line is tied to the 2030 strategy, and reporting is activity only, with no published results.

ICT strategy: sector policy

2 Preparing Last assessed Sep 2026 Stage last moved First assessment Methodology · On the map

Ethiopia adopted a national ICT policy and strategy in 2016, endorsed by ministers in 2017; whether its text is published is not known.

Governance and finance. The Ministry of Innovation and Technology is mandated under the Communication Services Proclamation of 2019 to develop policy instruments, mobilise resources and guide and monitor the telecom sector. It was formed in October 2018 by merging the Ministry of Communication and Information Technology with the Ministry of Science and Technology. The policy's 2017 revision had financial support from the World Bank.

Standing. The National ICT Policy and Strategy was developed in 2016 and endorsed by the Council of Ministers in 2017.

Text. UNCTAD cites the policy as a 2016 document of the federal government.

Period. The policy revises one first drafted and submitted to the Council of Ministers in 2002.

Delivery plan. UNCTAD lists the interventions through which the policy is implemented.

Reporting. UNCTAD said in September 2021 that implementation was fragmented across projects and sectors.

Last twelve months. In September 2021 the policy was still being implemented.

Noted, not assessed. Digital Ethiopia 2025, Digital Ethiopia 2030, the Communication Services Proclamation 2019, the Ten-Year Perspective Plan 2020-2030 and the Digital Skills Country Action Plan 2020-2030.

Not held. Whether the policy's text is published, the period it covers, a delivery plan with dates or costs, and any report against its targets. No domestic budget line is stated, and nothing dated in the last twelve months is held.

Data interoperability framework / roadmap: government interoperability framework

3 Establishing Last assessed Sep 2026 Stage last moved First assessment Methodology · On the map

Ethiopia approved a government interoperability framework by 2019; a 2023 assessment found the main services portal did not follow it.

Framework adopted. Ethiopia has a framework approved. The Ministry of Innovation and Technology's library of guidelines and standards lists the Ethiopian Government Interoperability Framework of 2019 with the status Approved, and the ministry's e-government assessment of February 2023 refers to the approved framework. In July 2024 the ministry was leading an interoperable digital government initiative, with a governance and compliance document for the framework attached.

Required and followed. The 2023 assessment found that the E-Services Portal offers no data interoperability features, a departure from the approved framework, and that an enterprise architecture stands alone, out of line with the national one. No source names an instrument that makes the framework compulsory. None names a body that enforces it, or counts the public bodies whose systems comply with it.

Data localisation policies: localisation rule

3 Establishing Last assessed Sep 2026 Stage last moved First assessment Methodology · On the map

Ethiopia's 2024 personal data protection proclamation requires personal data collected in the country to be stored on servers there; the enforcer is not known.

Rule in force. Ethiopia's rule covers personal data. Personal Data Protection Proclamation No. 1321/2024, adopted by Parliament on 4 April 2024, entered into force on 24 July 2024 and requires data controllers and processors to store locally collected personal data on servers in Ethiopia. The record gives no transition period, and does not say on what conditions a copy of the data may also be held abroad.

Enforcer named and rule applied. The nearest held fact is the same record, which describes the storage duty on controllers and processors and names no body that checks it. No source held names a body that checks compliance with the rule or grants exemptions from it. No source held names an organisation ordered to bring data into Ethiopia, penalised or exempted under the proclamation, and none counts those that have complied.

Legislation and regulation

Data protection legislation: general law

3 Establishing Last assessed Sep 2026 Stage last moved First assessment Methodology · On the map

A personal data protection proclamation was published in 2024 and its regulator approved about 10 data transfers by 2025; decisions against controllers are not known.

Authority. The proclamation designates the Ethiopian Communications Authority as the regulator. In November 2025 the Authority, whose mandate was extended from telecom regulation to data protection, was enforcing the law and expanding its staffing and technical capacity, its Director General Balcha Reba said.

Standing. Personal Data Protection Proclamation No. 1321/2024, the country's first comprehensive personal data protection law, was approved on 4 April 2024 and published in the Federal Negarit Gazette on 24 July 2024. It took effect on publication.

Regulations. In November 2025 four directives were expected to be approved in the coming months, and delays in issuing them had slowed implementation.

Application. The Director General said in November 2025 that the Authority had received about 10 requests to transfer data abroad and approved them as a temporary measure, and that data processors and collectors now need its permission.

Last twelve months. At a workshop co-hosted with Huawei in November 2025, Authority officials previewed a digital portal for registering data controllers and processors.

Noted, not assessed. None held.

Not held. No sanction or order on a named controller is held, and no published count of decisions for a stated period. No directive under the proclamation is held as approved.

Cybersecurity legislation: cybercrime and cybersecurity law

2 Preparing Last assessed Sep 2026 Stage last moved First assessment Methodology · On the map

A critical infrastructure cybersecurity law was passed in July 2026 and takes effect in July 2027; no use of it is known.

Law in force. The Federal Negarit Gazette, 32nd Year No. 41 carries Critical Infrastructure Cyber Security Protection Proclamation No. 1426/2026, done at Addis Ababa on 21 July 2026 and signed by the President. Its article 28 provides that it enters into force one year after publication, which puts commencement in July 2027. INSA's Director General said on 7 August 2026 that the House of People's Representatives unanimously ratified the proclamation, which sets 18 obligations for owners of 12 critical sectors.

Use of the law. The nearest fact held is that, according to INSA's Director General, institutions are to use a transition period to prepare for compliance with the proclamation's 18 obligations. The gazette text places entry into force one year after publication, so the transition runs until July 2027. The proclamation is a critical infrastructure measure for 12 sectors, under which owners face 18 obligations.

Noted, not assessed. Critical Infrastructure Cybersecurity Fund, run by INSA.

Not held. No source held says a cybercrime or cybersecurity law is in force.

Legislation covering digital id: national identity law

4 Operating Last assessed Sep 2026 Stage last moved First assessment Methodology · On the map

Ethiopia's 2023 Digital Identification Proclamation sets up a biometric identity database. No rules made under it since 2023 are known.

Law in force. Ethiopia's Digital Identification Proclamation, no. 1284/2023, was signed by the President on 18 April 2023 and came into force on its publication in the Federal Negarit Gazette. It is a proclamation of the federal parliament, written for the digital identity and for nothing else, and it is the law the identity system runs under.

Digital identity covered and rules made. The proclamation sets up a digital identification system that collects demographic and biometric data in a central database. The system it describes is digital from the start: a person is enrolled with biometric data and identified from the central database. The proclamation's second article sets out the terms the rest of the text relies on. No regulation or decree made under the law since September 2023 is on record.

Noted, not assessed. Fayda; Digital Identification System; Fayda digital ID; VeriFayda 2.

Not held. No regulation or decree applying the law, and no rule made under it that others must check identity against the system, dated in the last three years, is held.

Digital payments legislation: payments law

4 Operating Last assessed Sep 2026 Stage last moved First assessment Methodology · On the map

Ethiopia's 2020 central bank directives license payment operators and wallet issuers that are not banks; no rule requiring providers to connect is known.

Law or rules in force. The National Bank of Ethiopia introduced two sets of rules in 2020: the Payment Instrument Issuers directives and the Payment Systems Operators directive. Firms are licensed and operating under both. The Reporter gave this account of the two frameworks in July 2026. No act of parliament on payments is named.

Non-banks licensed and providers required to connect. The operators directive created four licence categories, for switch, point-of-sale, cash machine and payment gateway operators, under which companies that are not banks, Chapa, Arifpay, Santimpay and Kacha among them, were licensed. The issuers directives let telecom operators launch mobile wallets. No rule is named that requires mobile money providers and banks to connect to each other or to a national switch, so Ethiopia answers the first three questions and not the fourth.

Noted, not assessed. None held.

Not held. No rule held requires mobile money providers and banks to connect to each other or to a national or regional switch.

Access to information legislation: access to information law

3 Establishing Last assessed Oct 2026 Stage last moved Reassessed Oct 2026 Methodology · On the map

Ethiopia has had a mass media and access to information proclamation since 2008; its Ombudsman oversight is reported ineffective, and application is not known.

Law in force. Proclamation No. 590/2008 on Freedom of the Mass Media and Access to Information, dated 4 December 2008 and proclaimed under Article 55(1) of the Constitution, is published by the Ministry of Justice and affirms in its preamble individuals' right to access information held by public bodies and a duty on public officials to facilitate it. A June 2023 study lists it among Ethiopia's laws, and a draft Access to Information Proclamation prepared by a media law working group had not been passed as of March 2023.

Law put to work and applied. The same study says the Ethiopian Institution of the Ombudsman was designated the organ responsible for implementing freedom of information. The study is dated 1 June 2023. It reports studies finding the Ombudsman ineffective for lack of resources to evaluate public bodies, and it gives no date for that finding.

Noted, not assessed. None held.

Not held. Nothing held names rules made under the proclamation, says its oversight body is set up as such, or shows requests or decisions under it.

In 2024, 52.7 per cent of people aged 10 and over owned a mobile phone, by an international estimate.

Share of people who own a mobile phone, with its year. In 2024, 52.7 per cent of people aged 10 and over in Ethiopia owned a mobile phone, an estimate by the International Telecommunication Union. That leaves 47.3 per cent without a phone of their own. The same series put the share at 45.2 per cent in 2021, 49.6 per cent in 2022 and 51.2 per cent in 2023. Since 2021 the share rose by 7.5 points. Every figure since 2021 is an estimate, not a survey result. The share is past half and 12.3 points short of 65 per cent, which is two in three. The figure counts a person once, whatever the number of SIM cards they hold, and counts a phone as owned where the person has it for their own use.

In 2025, 5 GB of mobile data for a month cost 4.79 per cent of average monthly income, on international price figures.

Cost of a month of mobile data against income, with its year. In 2025, the cheapest 30-day plan of at least 5 GB from the largest operator in Ethiopia cost 4.79 per cent of average monthly income, on the International Telecommunication Union's price figures. The same figures put the cost at 8.51 per cent in 2022, 5.43 per cent in 2023 and 3.93 per cent in 2024. Since 2022 the cost fell by 3.72 points. The cost is within 5 per cent and 2.79 points above 2 per cent, the international target. The stage rests on that one figure, the newest held for 2024 or later, whoever published it.

Digitalisation of rural health clinics: HMIS

3 Establishing Last assessed Sep 2026 Stage last moved First assessment Methodology · On the map

Hospitals and health centres enter their own reports into the national system (2024); it is not known whether health posts do.

Governance, planning, finance. The Ministry of Health holds full ownership of DHIS2's customisation and implementation, as assessed in June 2022, under a budgeted health information strategy and a 10-year strategic plan. The memorandum Ethiopia signed with the United States on 23 December 2025 names DHIS2 as the country's HMIS, and the United States plans US$18,850,149 of data-systems funding over 2026 to 2030, across all the systems it lists.

Tiers in use. In facility surveys of 2019 and 2020, 305 public hospitals and health centres reported a functional HMIS, and health posts have none of their own. The Ministry of Health counted more than 33,000 health facilities on DHIS2 in 2024.

Where data is digitised. Health information technicians key routine reports into DHIS2 at their own facilities, on interviews held from October 2023 to March 2024. Health posts record largely on paper and send monthly to their health centre, which compiles and submits, as surveyed in 2019 and 2020.

Last twelve months. The share of expected monthly facility reports received in DHIS2 rose from 90% in 2024 to 93% in 2025, on Countdown to 2030's figures.

Primary clinics doing the digital input. A study published in May 2023 states that more than 95% of all health facilities used DHIS-2, online or offline. A 2025 study puts over 4,000 primary health care institutions, 95% of those eligible, on DHIS2, counting district hospitals, health centres, health posts and district health offices.

Levels in use. At June 2025 the system covered federal, regional, woreda and school levels, and deployment to 4,500 primary schools in 156 weredas was planned for July 2025 to June 2026.

How the record is kept. The system gives unique identification numbers to students, teachers and schools, as reported at June 2025. A UNESCO-commissioned case study researched in late 2019 found that schools keep registers on paper and collect attendance without storing it centrally.

Last twelve months. The sector plan published in 2021 gave a baseline of none for a student database and zero institutions networked.

Digitalisation of rural registry offices: civil registration

2 Preparing Last assessed Sep 2026 Stage last moved First assessment Methodology · On the map

Digital registration of births and deaths is in pilot, in Addis Ababa and 1,300 of 19,202 kebeles in 2025; use beyond pilots is not known.

Governance, planning, finance. The Immigration and Citizenship Service is the registration organ, with structures from federal to the lowest administrative level, under Proclamation No. 1370/2025 of 18 March 2025. The national eCRVS, which Ethiopia calls the Civil & Family Registration System, is homegrown, driven by the Prime Minister's Office and carries a three-phase rollout plan (July 2026).

Events registered digitally. The national eCRVS lists birth and death registration and certificates among its services, alongside marriage, divorce and adoption (July 2026).

How the record is made. The eCRVS is a decentralised, multi-tenant system on a shared architecture in which city administrations and regions provide all services, with offline access, rural connectivity, power and tablets listed as gaps (July 2026). Civil registration is mainly paper-based and manual, three copies of the form passing from kebele to woreda to the Service (July 2025).

Last twelve months. On 5 December 2025 the mayor of Addis Ababa launched the Integrated Civil Registration and Residency Service Technology and announced full implementation in 50 woredas from 8 December.

Local offices doing the digital entry. The eCRVS rollout begins with a pilot in Addis Ababa, then Amharic-speaking regions, then the rest of the country (July 2026). A tablet system, ME-CRVS, has been piloted in 1,300 kebeles and health facilities, in a country the Service counts at 19,202 kebeles, 70% of which provide civil registration (July 2025). In Addis Ababa the city agency's digitisation was in a pilot phase in 21 of 116 woredas in January 2025.

Noted, not assessed. Identity: Fayda, the national digital ID; the kebele ID; VeriFayda 2, EthSwitch's identity check for banks; UNHCR's refugee register PRIMES; and family registration as the 2025 proclamation provides it. Notification: birth and death notification by health facilities within the eCRVS, and health workers' birth notification. Certificate: Addis MESOB, a one-stop service centre in Addis Ababa. Outreach: a joint field mission on refugee birth registration in Afar; birth registration at sites for displaced people in Oromia; and a special civil registration drive for Fayda in Addis Ababa in October 2024. Exchange: the integration of civil registration with Fayda; the PRIMES Interoperability Gateway between the refugee and national ID systems; and the Central Statistical Agency's compilation of vital statistics from register copies.
