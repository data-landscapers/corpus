## Summary of the period

<!-- narrative: summary -->
Over the twelve months Côte d'Ivoire moved from a legislative story to an operating one. At the window's opening the 2013 statutes were in force, the cyber mandate had just transferred from the telecommunications regulator to a new agency, and the artificial-intelligence and data-governance strategies had been handed to the Prime Minister and were sitting unadopted. A year on, a compliance platform is live, a national root of trust is in service, auditors are being certified, the interoperability platform is populating against numeric targets, and a new six-year regulatory council is seated. What did not move is the statutory layer itself, and what went backwards is publication.

<!-- narrative: infrastructure -->
Three positions changed band. Satellite went from nothing to a contested four-way market — two geostationary services in January and April 2026, a low-orbit operator live in July, and a state route through regional capacity opened on 1 August — turning a coverage problem into an appropriation choice. Cyber-security moved from an agency that had only just opened its doors to a working apparatus: a national root of trust in service, a public-administration security-officer network launched, a certified auditor cohort and two master's curricula validated. And data centres moved from two private facilities and an unfunded national project to a US$170m authorised package whose scope now includes border surveillance.

Everything else accreted detail on positions already held. The rural programme delivered 500 of a stated 575 localities and kept going, the fibre count rose from 30,303 km to about 38,000, and 5G collected a second missed launch date. The strategy that authorises the cyber apparatus expired during the window and its successor is still a draft, so the framework in force is a text whose term has run out.

The identity spine did not move; the disclosure about it did. The register issuing the national identification number carries the same position it carried a year ago, and what changed is that its own director general named the coverage shortfall and the historic duplicate identities inside it. On-time birth registration, the pipeline feeding that register, remains at its 2024 reading of 55.4%, down from 66.8% in 2021, because no 2025 yearbook has been published.

Two things genuinely changed band. The exchange layer went from a twenty-month build to a live platform with twelve pilot bodies and a named political constraint on how services were selected. And the justice platform went from ten connected courts to all 39, issuing nationality certificates and criminal-record extracts nationwide.

The service layer filled in around them: an identity-backed postal application and a compulsory digital post box in the first quarter of 2026, a government assistant in March, a compliance platform in July, and an e-mining cadastre and a transport data platform in between. Payments inverted: a year ago the movement was licensing and state-fund equity, and the two hardest-edged facts now are a regional interoperability mandate binding Ivorian rails from 30 June 2026 and an unauthorised flat surcharge sitting on every counter transaction that no institution owns.

<!-- narrative: governance -->
The regulatory institutions moved and the statutes did not. A new six-year regulatory council took office in May 2026; the cyber agency consolidated a cybercrime unit, an emergency response team and an alert line into an operating apparatus; a register of data-protection officers was created; and a compliance platform replaced paper. Across the same year, the 2013 data-protection law and the 2013 electronic-transactions law were not amended, and the base is no closer to holding their texts or their decrees than it was a year ago.

The clearest regression is in publication. The regulator's statutory annual activity report has now been absent for 2024 as well as 2023, and the levy that funds it was cut 43.8% to FCFA 2.58bn for 2026. A regulator that acquired new tooling and new leadership this year still has no published account of what its levy buys.

The cyber-security strategy is in the unusual position of having advanced backwards on paper: the 2021 to 2025 text is expired but still the one in force, because the successor drafted in July 2026 has not reached the Council of Ministers. And there is still no Ivorian artificial-intelligence statute, bill or implementing decree of any kind — governance runs entirely through non-binding strategy instruments.
